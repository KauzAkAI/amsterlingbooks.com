Prologue

Twenty-Five Years Ago

The recruit was going to get herself killed.

Colonel Marcus Vane watched from the observation deck as Nicole Bastion threw herself at the combat simulation for the fourth time in six hours. Twenty-two years old, raw as unprocessed ore, and absolutely refusing to quit despite three cracked ribs and a dislocated shoulder the bioforge had only just knit back together.

The holographic enemies materialized around her in the training arena—Kashari raiders, their insectoid forms rendered in perfect detail. The simulation didn't believe in easy introductions.

Neither did Marcus.

"She's going to fail again, sir." Lieutenant Graves stood beside him, datapad glowing with performance metrics that painted an ugly picture. "Reaction time's deteriorating. Pain inhibitors are wearing off. She's running on fumes."

"Then she'll learn what fumes feel like." Marcus crossed his arms, tracking Nicole's movements through the transparent aluminum barrier. The arena was a cube fifty meters on each side—gray floors, gray walls, gray ceiling. Nothing to hide behind. Nowhere to run. Just you and the problem.

Some recruits broke here. The ones who didn't became something harder.

Nicole rolled left as a plasma bolt scorched the air where her head had been. The shot went wide, but not by much. Her combat suit—standard fleet issue, black synthetic weave with minimal armor plating—was already burned in three places. Sweat plastered her dark hair to her skull. Blood trickled from her nose.

She came up firing.

The targeting was off. Two shots wide, one high. The fourth clipped a raider's shoulder—not enough to drop it. Her weapon training scores were adequate. Not exceptional. Most recruits at her level would have washed out already based on marksmanship alone.

But Nicole Bastion didn't fight like most recruits.

She closed the distance instead of retreating. Stupid. Suicidal. The kind of decision that got marines killed in actual combat. The raider's blade—three feet of monomolecular edge—swept toward her midsection in an arc designed to bisect human torsos.

Nicole dropped under it.

Hit the ground rolling. Came up inside the raider's guard. Jammed her sidearm under its mandible and fired twice. The holographic alien burst into pixels.

Three more materialized immediately.

"Jesus Christ," Graves muttered. "She's not even slowing down."

Marcus said nothing. His eyes tracked every movement, cataloging, assessing. The way she favored her left leg—old injury, probably from the entrance trials. The slight hesitation before each trigger pull—good, meant she was thinking, not just reacting. The raw determination in every step that bordered on desperation.

He'd read her file fourteen times. Knew it better than she probably knew herself.

Nicole Adrienne Bastion. Born 2340, New Melbourne colony. Parents deceased—industrial accident when she was sixteen. Worked dock labor for four years to pay for basic augmentation. Applied to Terran Defense Fleet at twenty. Rejected. Applied again six months later. Rejected. Third application approved with provisional status.

The file didn't mention how she'd gotten her hands on military-grade combat training in the two years between applications. Marcus had made some discrete inquiries. The answers suggested connections to mercenary groups operating in the outer colonies—the kind that didn't ask questions if you could pay and didn't mind bleeding.

The kind of background that should have disqualified her permanently.

Someone had buried those records deep. Not deep enough to escape Marcus's clearance level, but deep enough to suggest she had a patron. Someone who wanted Nicole Bastion in the fleet badly enough to scrub her past.

That alone made her interesting.

What made her dangerous was the way she moved.

A plasma bolt caught her shoulder. The impact spun her sideways, combat suit smoking. Nicole stumbled, went down hard on one knee. The pain must have been excruciating—the simulation's feedback systems were calibrated to seventy percent of actual combat conditions. Enough to teach. Not enough to permanently damage.

Usually.

"She's done," Graves said. "Medical override in three... two..."

Nicole pushed to her feet.

Her left arm hung useless, the shoulder joint probably dislocated again. She switched her weapon to her right hand. Two raiders flanked her, moving with the patient efficiency of pack hunters who knew their prey was finished.

She shot the one on her left. Center mass. It dissolved.

The second raider's blade took her in the ribs.

The blow should have ended the simulation. Would have ended it for any other recruit. The combat suit's impact sensors registered a killing strike—the kind that would have opened her from sternum to spine in actual combat.

Nicole grabbed the blade.

Her hand closed on monomolecular edge that should have sliced through synthetic flesh like paper. The simulation's haptic feedback wouldn't actually cut her, but the neural response would make her brain believe it had. Pain, shock, blood loss—all processed through the lattice interface jacked directly into her nervous system.

She held on anyway.

Used the blade as an anchor point. Pulled herself forward along its length, closing distance with the raider. Her face was a mask of concentration and agony—no scream, no cry, just a locked jaw and eyes that refused to close.

Point-blank range. She fired three times into the raider's center mass.

It burst into pixels.

The simulation ended.

Nicole stood alone in the gray cube, swaying. Her combat suit was shredded. Blood—simulated, but her lattice didn't know the difference—soaked her left side. Her breathing came in harsh gasps that Marcus could hear through the observation deck's audio feed.

She didn't fall.

"I'll be damned," Graves said softly. "She actually cleared it."

"No." Marcus tapped the datapad, pulling up the performance metrics. Red indicators dominated the display. "She failed. Time limit exceeded by forty seconds. Injuries sustained exceed mission-viable parameters. If this were actual combat, she'd be dead three times over."

"But she finished—"

"She survived. There's a difference." Marcus handed the datapad back. "Schedule her for medical evaluation. Full workup. I want to know what she's running on."

Marcus had trained thousands of recruits over twenty years of service. He'd commanded spec-ops teams on seventeen worlds. He'd led marines through hell and watched good people die because they weren't quite fast enough, quite strong enough, quite stubborn enough to survive when the universe decided it was their turn.

He'd never seen anyone fight like Nicole Bastion fought.

Like she was trying to kill something that couldn't die. Like stopping meant something worse than death.

She was twenty-two years old. He was forty-three.

She was a recruit under his command. He was a colonel with a spotless record and a career built on making the hard calls and following orders even when they tasted like ash.

He should have listened to Admiral Meridian. Should have kept his distance. Should have treated Nicole Bastion like any other recruit who showed promise—trained her, graduated her, moved on to the next batch of raw talent that needed shaping into something useful.

Should have.

Marcus touched the glass with his fingertips, watching Nicole disappear into the locker room. His reflection stared back—gray eyes, dark hair starting to silver at the temples, the kind of face that had forgotten how to smile years ago.

"Goddamn it," he said quietly to the empty observation deck.

Because he already knew he wasn't going to listen.

Already knew that Nicole Adrienne Bastion was going to be the worst decision he ever made.

The kind that felt inevitable anyway.

* * *

PRESENT DAY — 2387 CE

The message reached Marcus Vane at Research Station Elysium exactly six hours before the anomaly breached containment.

It was four words long.

She's coming for you.

No signature. No identification. The message had been routed through seventeen relay stations across five sectors—the kind of obfuscation that required serious resources and serious paranoia.

Marcus knew exactly who sent it anyway.

He stood at the observation window, watching the spiral of impossible light tear reality apart, and felt something that might have been fear or anticipation or the ghost of a twenty-five-year-old mistake finally coming home to kill him.

The anomaly pulsed. Expanded.

On the other side of known space, Nicole Bastion was putting on her armor and preparing to breach the one place in the universe that was supposed to be untouchable.

Marcus smiled for the first time in six years.

"Let her come," he said to the empty lab and the watching stars and the woman who'd haunted every decision he'd made since the day he'd failed to keep his distance.

The anomaly pulsed again.

Somewhere in the Dead Sectors, something that used to be human was coming home.

Chapter One

Threshold

The anomaly waited beyond the viewport like a bruise on the skin of space.

Marcus Vane pressed his palm against the observation deck's curved transparency, felt the faint thrum of the station's life support through the composite material. The thing hung there in the black, twenty thousand kilometers distant—a sphere of distorted light that made his eyes ache when he tried to focus on its edges. Colors that shouldn't exist bled across its surface. Not quite purple, not quite green. Something his brain kept trying and failing to categorize.

Three months. They'd been studying it for three months now, ever since the survey satellite had flagged the coordinates and Research Station Elysium had been repositioned to observe. Three months of measurements that contradicted each other. Sensor readings that made no physical sense. Data that rewrote itself when he wasn't looking.

"Computer, run spectral analysis again. Full spectrum, increase resolution by thirty percent."

The station's intelligence responded with a soft chime. The holographic display materialized beside him, filling the air with cascading numbers and graphs that shifted like living things. Marcus frowned, pulled the data closer with a gesture, expanded the molecular resonance patterns.

There. That signature again. The same impossible reading that suggested the anomaly was simultaneously emitting and absorbing light across wavelengths that shouldn't interact. As if space itself had forgotten how to behave.

Behind him, the observation deck's pressure door cycled open with a hiss of equalizing atmosphere.

Marcus didn't turn around. He knew that particular rhythm of footfalls—heavy, deliberate, timed to project authority rather than achieve efficiency. Commander Thane walked like a drumbeat announcing itself.

"Dr. Vane." The voice matched the footsteps. Precise. Controlled. A voice that had learned to strip away anything resembling warmth. "I've been reviewing your latest reports."

"Then you've seen the inconsistencies." Marcus expanded another data set, highlighted the gravitational readings. "The mass fluctuates by orders of magnitude every forty-seven minutes. That's not drift. That's not measurement error. That's something fundamentally wrong with the local spacetime structure."

Thane moved to stand beside him, his reflection ghosting across the viewport's surface. The commander stood a head taller than Marcus, his military service visible in the rigid set of his shoulders and the gray threading through his close-cropped hair. The insignia on his uniform marked him as Colonial Authority, Fleet Administration, Resource Allocation Division—the branch that decided which systems got funding and which got forgotten.

"I'm not interested in what's wrong with the readings, Doctor. I'm interested in results." Thane gestured at the anomaly. "The probe launch is scheduled for fourteen hundred hours tomorrow. I need your preliminary assessment filed by midnight."

Marcus finally turned to face him. "That's eleven hours from now."

"Your mathematics are impeccable, as always."

"I need more time. The data sets are incomplete. The gravitational mapping alone requires another week of observation to establish a reliable baseline, and the energy signatures—"

"Are fascinating, I'm sure." Thane's expression didn't change, but something shifted in his eyes. Something that reminded Marcus of the way administrators looked at budget reports they were about to cut. "But the Colonial Senate has allocated resources for this research station based on specific timelines and deliverables. We've already extended your observation window by three days."

"Three days that revealed radiation patterns inconsistent with any known stellar phenomenon. Commander, we're looking at something unprecedented. If we send a probe in without understanding the basic physics involved—"

"Then we'll gather data from the probe itself. That's rather the point of using unmanned vehicles, Dr. Vane."

Marcus pulled up another holographic display, this one showing the probe's planned trajectory. The path curved through space in a graceful arc, approaching the anomaly from its polar axis—the safest approach vector based on current models. Except the current models were built on assumptions that might not apply to whatever waited out there in the dark.

"The probe's shielding is rated for standard radiation environments and gravitational stress up to point-seven-five threshold. These readings suggest we're dealing with flux values at least triple that. The vehicle will be torn apart before it gets within useful scanning range."

"Then we'll build a better probe." Thane dismissed the hologram with a wave of his hand. "But first we need baseline data to justify the expense. The Senate doesn't fund theoretical concerns, Doctor. They fund results."

"They'll fund a debris field if we rush this."

The temperature in the observation deck seemed to drop several degrees. Thane's jaw tightened—the only visible sign of the anger Marcus knew was building behind that carefully maintained facade.

"Let me be clear about the political realities you seem determined to ignore." Thane's voice remained level, which somehow made it worse. "This station costs the Colonial Authority seventeen million marks per month to operate. You, personally, represent an investment of six years of training and two years of active research with minimal return. There are forty-three other research initiatives competing for those funds. Initiatives with clearer timelines and more achievable objectives."

Marcus felt something cold settle in his stomach. "You're threatening to shut down the observation program."

"I'm explaining the parameters within which you're operating. The Senate allocated funds for a four-month research window. We're now in month three. If we don't deliver actionable intelligence on this anomaly's potential strategic value, those funds will be reallocated. The station will be mothballed. And you'll be reassigned to survey work in the inner colonies, cataloging atmospheric composition on mining worlds."

The threat landed with the precision Thane had intended. Marcus had spent five years doing exactly that kind of work before earning his position at Research Station Elysium. Five years of grinding tedium, analyzing gas giant atmospheres and mineral deposits. Work that required no creativity and offered no discovery. Just data entry with a doctorate.

"What strategic value?" Marcus heard the edge in his own voice and tried to soften it. "We don't even know what it is yet. For all we know, it's a naturally occurring phenomenon—rare, unprecedented, but ultimately just an astronomical curiosity."

"Or it's something else." Thane moved closer to the viewport, his reflection overlapping with the anomaly's distorted light. "Something that could be replicated. Weaponized. Used as a power source or a barrier or a means of travel. The Senate doesn't care about astronomical curiosities, Doctor. They care about advantage. They care about what gives humanity leverage in a universe that's proving far less empty than we hoped."

There it was. The real reason for the pressure, the rushed timeline, the threat of shutdowns and reassignments. Someone in the Colonial Senate thought the anomaly might be useful. Might be worth killing over. Might be worth dying for.

Marcus looked back at the thing hanging in the dark, that sphere of impossible physics and contradictory data. He thought about probes shattering against forces they weren't designed to measure. He thought about the cascading chain of events that followed when you poked at something you didn't understand—how curiosity could become catastrophe in the space between one heartbeat and the next.

"I'll have the preliminary assessment ready by midnight," he said quietly. "But I'm noting in the official record that this timeline is insufficient for proper analysis."

"Your objection will be noted." Thane turned away from the viewport, already moving toward the pressure door. "Fourteen hundred hours, Dr. Vane. The probe launches whether you're ready or not."

The door cycled shut behind him, leaving Marcus alone with the anomaly and the humming silence of the observation deck.

He pulled up the probe's specifications again, ran calculations in his head. The shielding could be reinforced, maybe. The approach vector adjusted to minimize exposure to whatever forces were causing those gravitational fluctuations. It wouldn't be enough—not nearly enough—but it might keep the probe intact long enough to gather something useful.

Might.

The word tasted like ash in his mouth.

* * *

Marcus was seventeen hours into his assessment, surviving on stimulant patches and recycled coffee, when the station's alarm system came alive.

The sound cut through the research lab like a blade—sharp, rhythmic, impossible to ignore. Emergency protocols overlaid themselves across his workspace displays, replaced spectral analysis charts with system warnings rendered in urgent red.

"Computer, report."

"Unscheduled vessel detected on approach vector. Range: forty-two thousand kilometers and closing. Velocity: point-three-seven lightspeed, decreasing. Configuration unknown. No response to standard hailing frequencies."

Marcus felt his pulse kick up. Research Station Elysium sat in deep space, three weeks from the nearest colony world and six weeks from anything resembling a major shipping lane. Ships didn't just appear out here by accident. Every vessel that approached the station was scheduled, logged, and cleared through Colonial Authority channels weeks in advance.

He pulled up the sensor feed, routed it to the lab's main display. The incoming ship resolved itself from the background radiation—a configuration he'd never seen before, all angles and asymmetry, with none of the smooth curves or modular design philosophy that characterized human vessels. The hull seemed to absorb light rather than reflect it, made it difficult to judge the actual size.

"Estimated mass?"

"Unable to determine. Sensor returns inconsistent with known material densities."

That cold feeling was back in Marcus's stomach, the same one he'd felt during Thane's threats. But this was different. This was wrongness of a different magnitude.

He activated the lab's communication panel, opened a channel to the command center. Thane's face appeared in the holographic projection, his expression carved from stone.

"We're tracking it, Dr. Vane. Return to your quarters and await further instructions."

"What is it?"

"Unknown. The vessel's configuration doesn't match anything in our database. No energy signature consistent with standard propulsion systems. No response to our hails."

Marcus looked back at the sensor display. The ship was still decelerating, adjusting its trajectory with small bursts of something that might have been thrust but registered on the sensors as localized spacetime distortions. As if it was pushing against reality itself rather than working within the normal parameters of physics.

"It's doing what the anomaly does," he said, speaking the realization as it formed. "Look at the gravitational readings. Those same fluctuations, the same impossible values. Commander, that ship is using the same physics as—"

"I can read a sensor display, Doctor." Thane's voice had taken on an edge Marcus hadn't heard before. Something almost like fear, buried under layers of military discipline. "The vessel will reach weapons range in approximately seven minutes. All non-essential personnel are ordered to proceed to the central hub emergency shelters. That includes you."

"I need to see this. If that ship is connected to the anomaly—"

"You need to follow orders. Command out."

The holographic projection vanished.

Marcus stood in the empty lab, listening to the alarm's insistent rhythm, feeling the weight of the moment settle over him like a physical thing.

Chapter Two

The Offer

The message arrived while Nicole was three drinks into forgetting.

Her slate vibrated against the table. Once. Twice. The pattern she'd awaited and dreaded in equal measure.

She occupied the back corner of the Lattice, a bar carved into the hollowed fuel tank of a decommissioned hauler. Through the porthole beside her booth, she watched the curve of Meridian Station's hull and beyond it the scattered lights of the mining fleet, distant as dying stars. The establishment clung to the outer ring like a barnacle, all corroded metal and flickering holo-advertisements promising things the station's corporate masters would never permit.

Nicole seized the device, thumbed it active. The screen revealed nothing but a location marker and a time. Dock Seven, Bay Thirty-Two. Twenty minutes from now. No signature, no explanation, but she recognized the source. Only one person employed that particular encryption sequence, and only when they had work.

She drained her drink—something local that scalded like rust and regret—and slid from the booth. The bartender, a woman with chrome-plated fingers and exhausted eyes, didn't glance up as Nicole dropped credits on the counter. In a place like the Lattice, people learned not to notice who came and went.

Twenty minutes later, Nicole stood in the shadows of Bay Thirty-Two, hand resting on the ceramic pistol tucked against her spine beneath her jacket. The bay sprawled dark around her, loading doors sealed, status lights glowing inactive. Old habits from older times.

A panel in the far wall hissed open. A figure emerged, silhouetted against the dim corridor light beyond. Nicole's muscles coiled, then released as she recognized the gait. The Handler moved like someone who'd spent too many years in low-gravity environments, each step deliberate and controlled.

"Nicole." His voice barely carried across the empty bay. "Thank you for coming."

"You made it sound urgent." She stepped into the anemic light. "Three coded pulses usually means someone's bleeding."

The Handler smiled, though the expression died before reaching his eyes. He was a slight man, unremarkable in every way that mattered—the kind of face you'd forget five minutes after meeting him. Nicole knew that was intentional. In their line of work, memorable was dangerous.

"No blood. Not yet." He extracted a data chip from his pocket. "But I have something that requires your particular expertise. This job is off-book. Way off-book. The kind of work that doesn't exist before or after it happens."

Nicole studied his face, reading the tension in his jaw, the way his fingers gripped the chip just a fraction too tight. She'd known the Handler for six years, worked fourteen jobs through him, and she'd never witnessed him nervous before.

"I'm listening."

"Two million credits. Half up front, half on completion. Clean transfers, no questions, no tracking."

The number struck her like a physical blow. Nicole had pulled high-risk extractions before—corporate executives, political prisoners, the occasional witness who knew too much—but the most she'd ever cleared was three hundred thousand. Two million was the kind of money that purchased new identities, retirement on a core world, or a ship large enough to vanish into.

"That's a suicide run number," she said carefully. "What's the catch?"

The Handler extended the chip. "The catch is the location. Carcosa-7."

Nicole's hand froze halfway to the chip. "You want me to extract someone from Carcosa-7."

"Yes."

"That's not a prison. That's a tomb. No one escapes Carcosa. No one even breaches it. The entire sector is locked down by the Oversight Council. Military patrols, automated defense platforms, kill-on-sight protocols for anyone without clearance."

"I'm aware of the security situation. The people funding this operation have provided access codes, patrol schedules, and a forty-eight-hour window when the security grid will be compromised. All you need to do is infiltrate, locate the target, and extract." The Handler stepped closer, his voice dropping to barely a whisper. "The target is Marcus Vane."

For a moment, Nicole couldn't process the words. They didn't cohere, couldn't cohere.

"Marcus Vane is dead," she said flatly. "He died twenty-five years ago. The Carthage Incident. Three hundred people killed when the station's core reactor went critical. I saw the casualty reports."

"The casualty reports were fiction." The Handler pressed the chip into her palm. "Marcus Vane survived the Carthage Incident. He's been held at Carcosa-7 ever since. Current biometric data is on the chip."

Nicole's mind raced. Marcus Vane had been a legend—a brilliant engineer who'd worked on classified projects for the Oversight Council, developing technologies that pushed at the boundaries of what humanity understood about physics. Then came Carthage, and the whispers. Sabotage. Treason. Experiments that violated every ethical guideline.

The official story had always insisted he'd perished in the incident, but there had been rumors. People who claimed he'd known too much, seen too much, been disappeared by the very people who'd employed him.

"Why now?" Nicole asked. "If they've kept him locked up for a quarter century, why break him out now?"

"Because they're moving him. The Council just announced Carcosa's closure—prisoner transfers begin next week. This forty-eight-hour window is the last chance to extract him before he disappears into a new facility. After that, he's gone." The Handler's expression remained neutral, but something shifted in his eyes. "I came to you because this job requires someone with your specific skill set. Someone who understands high-security extraction protocols. Someone who can think on their feet when the situation deteriorates."

"Someone expendable," Nicole said.

The Handler didn't deny it. "Someone capable. There's a difference."

Nicole closed her fist around the chip. The small piece of tech contained enough classified information to get her killed a dozen different ways. Just possessing it was a death sentence if the Oversight Council discovered it.

"Twelve hours," she said.

"Contact me through the usual channels once you've decided." The Handler turned toward the door, then paused. "For what it's worth, I think you can do this. I wouldn't have brought it to you otherwise."

He stepped through the door and vanished into the corridor beyond. The panel hissed shut behind him, leaving Nicole alone in the dark bay.

She stood there for a long moment, feeling the weight of the chip in her hand. Through the bay's external viewport, she watched the running lights of ships moving between Meridian and the mining platforms, tiny sparks of civilization against the infinite dark.

Two million credits. An impossible job. And Marcus Vane, back from the dead.

Nicole slid the chip into her jacket pocket and headed for her quarters.

* * *

Her room was a five-by-three meter cube in Meridian's residential sector, barely large enough for a bunk, a footlocker, and a fold-down desk. Nicole had lived in worse.

She locked the door, activated the privacy shield that would block any electronic surveillance, and retrieved her slate. The chip slid into the reader and she began dissecting the data systematically, years of training and experience allowing her to process the information with clinical efficiency.

The patrol schedules were genuine—she recognized the formatting, the encryption signatures that marked them as authentic Oversight Council documents. Someone on the inside was feeding information to whoever wanted Marcus Vane extracted. That was either very good or very bad, and Nicole wasn't sure which.

The access codes had been updated within the last week. She cross-referenced them against the security protocols she'd memorized from other Council facilities. They appeared legitimate, but there was no way to verify without actually deploying them, and that would mean committing to the job.

The facility layout revealed a three-dimensional labyrinth of corridors, checkpoints, and hardened bulkheads. Marcus was being held in a medical wing on Level Seven, a section of the station that required retinal scans and biometric verification at every checkpoint. The extraction would demand she either forge the credentials or find a way to circumvent the security entirely.

She pulled up the biometric data.

The image that materialized on her slate showed a man in his late sixties, with gray hair and the sunken cheeks of someone who'd spent too long in low-nutrition environments. But the eyes were sharp, intelligent, carrying the weight of knowledge that most people would never comprehend. This was Marcus Vane. Alive. Imprisoned for a quarter century in the most secure facility in known space.

Nicole had been fourteen when the Carthage Incident happened. She remembered watching the news feeds, seeing the footage of the station's wreckage tumbling through space. Three hundred dead. An entire research facility obliterated. The Oversight Council had called it an accident, a reactor malfunction, but even then there had been questions.

She'd learned later, after she'd entered the extraction business, that Carthage had been a weapons research facility. Black budget projects, experimental technology that pushed the boundaries of what was considered ethical or safe. Marcus Vane had been the lead scientist on something called the Erebus Initiative, though what exactly that entailed remained classified at levels she couldn't access.

The official story was that Vane had died in the explosion along with his entire team, his work buried in the wreckage. But if he'd survived, if he'd been imprisoned all these years, it meant the Council wanted him alive. Wanted him somewhere they could control him, keep him contained.

And now someone was willing to pay two million credits to set him free.

Nicole leaned back against the bulkhead, processing the implications. Breaking Vane out would forge powerful enemies, the kind that didn't forgive or forget. But two million credits was freedom. Real freedom. The kind that meant never running another extraction, never watching her back in every bar and corridor.

She studied the provided extraction window. Forty-eight hours from now, a planned maintenance cycle would disable several security systems for routine upgrades. The window would last six hours—long enough to infiltrate, extract Marcus, and clear the station's defense perimeter.

In theory.

Nicole pulled up the environmental data. Carcosa-7 maintained standard gravity through rotation, breathable atmosphere, temperature within comfortable human ranges. That meant she wouldn't need specialized equipment for the environment itself. The guards were another matter—the facility maintained a security force of three hundred, all military-trained, all loyal to the Council.

The logistics were staggering. She'd need a ship—something fast and well-armed. She'd need equipment, weapons, medical supplies. She'd need backup, because infiltrating a place like Carcosa-7 alone was just suicide with extra steps.

Nicole stood, paced the cramped space. Three steps to the wall, pivot, three steps back. Her father had taught her to break down complex problems into manageable pieces. What do you know? What do you need to know? What can you control?

She knew the target. Knew the location. Knew the payment and the risks.

She needed to know whether the intelligence was reliable, whether the access codes would function, whether Marcus Vane would even be capable of moving under his own power after twenty-five years in detention.

She could control her preparation, her planning, her execution. Everything else was variables.

Nicole pulled up her contact list, scrolled through the names. If she was going to do this, she'd need help. Rivan, maybe—he'd flown her out of tight situations before, and his ship could outrun most patrol vessels. Kess for electronic warfare, bypassing the security systems.

Chapter Three

Ghosts

Nicole left the client's office and threaded her way back through Meridian's commercial sector, her mind already pivoting from negotiation to logistics. The job was solid. The payment legitimate—she'd verified the escrow placement on her datapad while the elevator descended. Which gave her approximately eight hours to assemble her crew, prep the ship, and burn clear of Meridian before competing salvage operators caught wind of the Europa Station contract.

She tapped her comm. "Jace, we're on. Get everyone to the Whisper. Briefing in one hour."

His response crackled through, crisp and immediate. "Copy that. I'll round them up."

The Whisper occupied one of Meridian's shadow berths—docking arms reserved for ships whose operators paid premium rates for the privilege of minimal oversight. Station security didn't patrol these berths as regularly, and the docking officers swallowed their questions as long as the fees cleared on time. Not cheap, but worth it for the discretion.

By the time Nicole reached the ship, Jace had already corralled the others. She found them gathered in the briefing room, a converted storage bay barely large enough to contain them all.

* * *

Nicole stood at the head of the briefing table, studying the faces of the four people who'd followed her into work that turned bodies into vacuum-frozen debris.

The table projected a holographic schematic of Europa Station, the image flickering where the emitter was dying. Nicole had meant to replace it three months ago, but replacement parts devoured credits she couldn't justify when that money might mean the difference between protein paste and actual food.

"Europa Station," she said, her voice slicing through the low hum of the ship's recyclers. "Corporate research facility, officially decommissioned eight years ago after Helion Group pulled their charter. Unofficially, it's been dark closer to a decade, floating in Jupiter's orbit like a tomb nobody wants to open."

Jace leaned back in his chair, the servo-joints in his prosthetic arm whirring softly as he crossed his arms. His face bore the weathered quality that came from too many years breathing recycled air and staring down the wrong end of security rifles. "And we're opening it."

"We're extracting something from it," Nicole corrected. She swiped the display, and the station's exterior dissolved to reveal its interior layout—a maze of corridors and chambers spiraling toward the central hub. "The client wants specific hardware. They've provided coordinates. We get in, we get the item, we get out."

"Client got a name?" Sera asked. She sat with one boot propped on the table's edge, fingers dancing across a datapad in her lap. Her hair was shaved on one side, the other falling across her face in a purple curtain she'd dyed last week in what Nicole assumed was either aesthetic choice or profound boredom.

"Client wants to stay anonymous," Nicole said. "Payment's already been verified and placed in escrow. Half now, half on delivery."

Kira made a sound that might have been agreement or skepticism. She was the smallest person in the room and the most dangerous, her compact frame concealing muscle and reflexes that Nicole had seen drop men twice her size. "How much?"

"Enough to keep us flying for six months. Maybe eight if we're smart about it."

"So three months, knowing us," Daven said. He occupied the far end of the table, his bulk making the crash seat look like furniture designed for children. His laugh rumbled low, matching his voice. "What's the catch? There's always a catch with this kind of money."

Nicole had been waiting for that question. She zoomed the schematic to the station's power systems, revealing the dark veins of conduits and generators that should have hummed with energy. "Europa Station went dark suddenly. No distress calls, no evacuation orders, no explanation. The last transmission was a routine status update, and then nothing."

"Crew?" Jace asked.

"Three hundred and forty-seven researchers, engineers, and support staff." Nicole swiped again, and personnel files cascaded across the display—faces smiling for corporate ID photos, most of them young, most of them probably desperate enough for the work that they hadn't asked the right questions. "No survivors reported. No bodies recovered. Helion Group sealed the station and walked away."

Sera's fingers stopped moving on her datapad. "They just left three hundred people up there?"

"Official report says catastrophic systems failure. Environmental collapse. They classified the rest." Nicole watched their faces, reading the calculations happening behind their eyes. This was the moment when crew members decided whether the money was worth the risk, whether the vacuum between them and payday was filled with acceptable danger or the kind that turned people into cautionary tales.

Jace spoke first. "You've taken us through worse."

Not quite true, but Nicole didn't correct him. She'd taken them through situations where the odds were bad and the payment worse, where they'd scraped by on luck and the kind of desperate improvisation that looked like skill if you squinted. But she'd always brought them back.

"Station's been sealed for eight years," Kira said. "How are we getting in?"

"The client provided detailed schematics and targeting data for the docking system," Nicole said. "We'll need to manually override the clamps, but it's doable. Once we're docked, we cut our way through if the airlocks don't respond."

"And if the station's security systems are still active?" Sera asked.

"Then we deal with it. The place has been running on battery reserves at best. Most automated systems should be dead by now." Nicole studied their faces, hunting for the doubt that would signal someone backing out. She didn't see it. "Equipment check is in four hours. We burn for Jupiter in eight. Get your affairs in order. If you've got debts on Meridian, now's the time to settle them or run from them."

They filed out, Daven and Kira arguing about something in low voices, Sera already absorbed back into her datapad. Jace lingered, his organic hand drumming against the table's edge.

"You're not telling us something," he said.

Nicole kept her expression neutral. "I'm telling you what you need to know."

"We're walking into a ghost station where three hundred people vanished without a trace, and you're telling me what I need to know." His voice didn't rise, but something sharp edged into it. "I've been flying with you for two years, Nic. I know when you're holding cards close."

"The job is the job," Nicole said. "The rest doesn't matter."

He studied her for a long moment, and she met his gaze without flinching. Finally, he nodded and left, the door hissing shut behind him.

Nicole stood alone in the briefing room, staring at the rotating schematic of Europa Station. She reached out and touched the hologram, her fingers passing through the central hub where the research labs had been. Where she'd spent three years of her life, twelve years ago, learning things she wished she could forget.

The station would be empty now. Dead. The personnel records locked behind Helion Group encryption, inaccessible without corporate authorization she didn't have and couldn't get. Even if someone had downloaded crew manifests before the blackout, twelve years changed a person. She'd been nineteen when she'd arrived at Europa Station, fresh-faced and stupid with ambition.

No one would recognize her. The station was a tomb, and tombs kept their secrets.

She collapsed the hologram with a gesture, and the briefing room fell into dimness broken only by the emergency lighting strips along the floor. Nicole turned away and left, the door hissing shut behind her.

* * *

The equipment check took longer than planned. Jace discovered stress fractures in two of the pressure suits, which meant breaking into their emergency fund to purchase replacements from a vendor who charged triple the normal rate because he knew they had no choice. Kira demanded additional ammunition, arguing that walking into a dark station with light loadouts was asking to become a cautionary tale. Daven needed to recalibrate the cutting torch, which had developed an unfortunate tendency to ignite at random intervals.

Nicole supervised it all from the cargo bay, signing off on purchases and mentally recalculating their operating budget. The job payment would cover everything with room to spare, but only if nothing went wrong.

"We're going to be cutting it close on life support," Sera said, appearing at Nicole's elbow with her datapad. "If we're on Europa Station for more than six hours, we'll be into our reserve oxygen. More than eight, and we're looking at recycler strain."

"Client estimates two hours for the extraction."

"Client estimates are usually optimistic by a factor of three." Sera tapped her datapad, bringing up a logistics breakdown that mapped their consumption rates against various time scenarios. "I'm loading extra scrubber cartridges, but that only buys us another hour."

"Do it," Nicole said. "Anything else?"

"Yeah." Sera's voice dropped lower. "You sure about this?"

Nicole looked at her. "Having doubts?"

"Having thoughts. There's a difference." Sera met her eyes. "I know you don't talk about your past, and that's fine. Everyone out here is running from something. But this feels personal, and personal jobs have a way of going wrong in specific ways."

"It's not personal," Nicole said. "It's just a job."

Sera studied her for a long moment, then shrugged. "Okay. Just remember we're here because we trust you. Don't make that trust a mistake."

She walked away, leaving Nicole alone among the crates of equipment and supplies. Through the cargo bay's small viewport, she could see Meridian Station's lights, thousands of them, each one representing a person or a business or a dream that had led them to the edge of civilized space.

She wondered how many of them were running from ghosts.

* * *

They burned for Jupiter eight hours later, the Whisper's engines pushing them out of Meridian's orbit and into the dark between worlds. Nicole sat in the captain's chair, watching the station shrink in the rear display until it became just another light among the stars.

The bridge was small, designed for a crew of three but stretched to accommodate five when necessary. Sera sat at the communications console, monitoring the channels for any traffic that might indicate interest in their departure. Jace occupied the co-pilot seat, his prosthetic hand dancing across the navigation controls with the casual competence that came from years of practice. Kira and Daven were below, securing the cargo and running final checks on the equipment.

"Transit time is nine days," Jace said. "Assuming we maintain current burn and don't hit any surprises."

Nine days to Jupiter. Nine days to think about what waited for her on Europa Station, in the dark corridors where she'd spent three years of her life chasing mysteries that had ultimately chased back.

"Any traffic?" Nicole asked Sera.

"Civilian chatter, mostly. A few corporate haulers making the Mars run. Nothing military, nothing that reads like surveillance." Sera adjusted something on her console. "We're clear."

Nicole nodded, watching the stars slide past in the viewport. In nine days, she'd be back on Europa Station, walking corridors she'd tried to forget, breathing air that tasted like memory and regret.

The Whisper pushed deeper into the dark, carrying them toward Jupiter and the secrets that orbited it, waiting in the cold and the silence for someone to finally return.

Nicole closed her eyes and tried not to remember what it had felt like the first time she'd seen Europa Station, when she'd been nineteen and believed the work she'd do there would matter.

Chapter Four

First Contact

The signal had sharpened.

Nicole stood on the bridge of the Kaiyo, watching the main display resolve the anomaly into something almost coherent. Not quite a structure—the sensors couldn't parse it that precisely—but definitely not natural. Too many right angles. Too much symmetry. Too much intention.

"Distance?" she asked.

"Seven hundred kilometers and closing," Jiang said from the sensor station. "Whatever it is, it's massive. Mass readings suggest at least two hundred thousand tonnes."

Nicole felt her pulse quicken. Two hundred thousand tonnes. Cruiser-scale. Dreadnought-scale. The kind of scale that didn't materialize by accident in deep space.

"Slow to one-quarter thrust," she said. "Tactical, I want targeting solutions on three different approach vectors. Comms, are we getting anything else from that signal? Any variation at all?"

Martinez shook her head. "Still just the pulse. Same frequency, same interval. It's like—" She hesitated. "It's like a beacon."

"Or a lure," Rask said from the weapons station. His hands were already dancing across his console, loading targeting parameters. "Could be automated. Could be bait."

"Could be a lot of things." Nicole summoned the tactical display on her command screen. The anomaly sat at the center of the projection like a spider in a web, and she felt the old training reflexes ignite—the ones Marcus Vane had drilled into her twenty years ago when she'd been young enough to think that dying for something meant anything at all.

Stop thinking like an individual. Start thinking like a system.

"All right," she said. "Here's how we do this. Jiang, I want continuous active scanning. If anything on that object so much as twitches, I want to know about it. Martinez, keep broadcasting our identification and peaceful intent on all standard frequencies. Rask, bring point defense online but keep weapons tight. We approach slowly, cautiously, and we're ready to burn hard in any direction if things go wrong."

"And if things go right?" Jiang asked.

Nicole studied the display. The enormous, impossible object hanging in space where nothing should exist. The signal that had been calling out into the void for god-knows-how-long, waiting for someone to answer.

"If things go right," she said, "then we're about to make first contact with whatever left this here. Either way, we document everything. Full records, continuous transmission back to Bastion Station. If this goes badly, at least someone will know what happened to us."

She caught Harper's reflection in the display glass. The captain stood near the aft stations, arms crossed, watching Nicole work. He'd ceded tactical command without argument, and she couldn't tell if that was trust or resignation.

"Captain?" Nicole said. "Unless you want to take the conn—"

"It's your operation, Commander." Harper's voice was level. "I'm just here to sign the reports."

Nicole nodded. Professional courtesy or professional distance—she'd take either one. "All right. Helm, take us in. Nice and easy."

The Kaiyo crept forward through the black. On the main display, the anomaly swelled from a blip to a shape to something that made Nicole's spatial reasoning falter. It wasn't a ship—at least not any ship design she'd ever seen. Too organic. Too wrong. The surface seemed to undulate in ways that solid matter shouldn't, reflecting their scanning beams in patterns that made her eyes ache.

"Getting strange readings," Jiang said. "The mass keeps fluctuating. Sometimes it registers as solid, sometimes as… I don't know. Empty space? This doesn't make sense."

"Distance?" Nicole asked.

"Six hundred kilometers. Five-fifty."

Nicole checked the tactical overlay. Still room to maneuver. Still time to abort if needed. "Maintain approach speed. All stations report status."

"Sensors nominal," Jiang said.

"Weapons green," Rask confirmed.

"Comms ready," Martinez added.

The bridge had gone quiet except for the standard operational chatter. Even the normal background hum of systems seemed muted, as if the ship itself was holding its breath. Nicole felt the weight of the moment—the absolute crystalline understanding that whatever happened in the next few minutes would define everything that came after.

"Distance?" she asked again.

"Five hundred kilometers. Four-fifty."

Harper moved closer to the command station, his eyes fixed on the main display. "Commander, recommendations if the situation changes?"

Nicole appreciated the question—keeping him engaged, feeding him the tactical picture. "If we see active targeting systems, we go full burn perpendicular to our approach vector. Makes us harder to track. If they're hostile, our best bet is to break line of sight and get distance before jumping out."

"Understood."

You're trying to survive. You need to be trying to optimize.

"Martinez," Nicole said quietly. "The signal. Is it changing at all? Responding to our approach?"

Martinez tilted her head, listening to something only she could hear through her neural interface. "No change in frequency or interval. But—" She paused. "The amplitude is increasing. It's getting stronger."

"Because we're getting closer?"

"Maybe. Or because it knows we're here."

"Four hundred kilometers," Jiang said.

The anomaly consumed half the main display now. Nicole could see details that the sensors couldn't quite parse—geometric patterns that shifted and flowed, surface textures that seemed to exist in more dimensions than three, and something that might have been windows or weapon ports or something else entirely.

"I'm reading power signatures," Rask said. His voice had tightened. "Multiple sources. Whatever this thing is, it's active."

"Weapons?"

"Can't tell. The readings are… they're not matching any known technology."

Nicole's mouth had gone dry. She'd spent twenty years learning to read tactical situations, to predict outcomes, to optimize for survival. But every instinct she'd developed was screaming contradictions. The anomaly was threatening and inert, massive and weightless, present and somehow absent.

Harper leaned in slightly. "Commander?"

"I know, sir. We're in the commitment zone." Nicole pulled up the tactical overlay, checking approach vectors and escape trajectories. They were too close now—burning away would expose their stern to whatever that thing was, and their jump drive needed thirty seconds to spool up. Forward or nothing. "Maintain approach. Reduce to one-eighth thrust."

"Three hundred kilometers," Jiang said. "Two-fifty."

The bridge felt smaller. Nicole could hear her own breathing, could feel the tension radiating from every station. This was the moment—the point where exploration became something else entirely.

"Two hundred kilometers," Jiang said.

"All stop," Nicole ordered. "Hold position. Martinez, broadcast again. All frequencies. Tell them—" She paused. What did you say to something like this? "Tell them we're here. We're listening. We want to communicate."

Martinez's fingers moved across her console. "Broadcasting now."

They waited.

Seconds stretched into minutes. The anomaly hung in space ahead of them, vast and incomprehensible, and the signal continued its steady pulse. Nicole counted heartbeats, measured breaths, calculated escape trajectories she knew might not matter at all.

"Still no change," Martinez said, her voice barely above a whisper. "Same pattern, same frequency."

"Could it be completely automated?" Harper asked. "A beacon left behind by something that's already gone?"

"Possible," Nicole said. "But those power readings suggest—"

"Commander," Jiang interrupted. "Something's happening."

On the main display, the anomaly was transforming. The surface patterns were shifting, coalescing, forming something that Nicole's brain tried and failed to interpret as language or mathematics or art. Light bloomed across the structure—not reflected light from their scanning beams, but light generated from within. It was beautiful. Terrible. Completely alien.

And then the signal fractured.

The steady pulse shattered into something complex—harmonic frequencies layered over each other, weaving patterns that Martinez's systems struggled to decode. Nicole watched her communications officer's hands fly across her console as data flooded through, her expression shifting from confusion to concentration to something like alarm.

"I'm getting something," Martinez said, her professional composure cracking slightly. "It's—it's not language exactly, but it's structured. Deliberate. They're responding to us."

"Can you interpret it?"

"I'm trying. The patterns are… they're mathematical. Describing spatial relationships, maybe? Or…" She went quiet, focused entirely on the stream of information flowing through her neural interface. When she looked up, her face had gone pale. "Commander, I think they're giving us coordinates. And I think—I think it's a warning."

Nicole felt something cold settle in her chest. "What kind of warning?"

"I don't know. The mathematics are clear but the context…" Martinez pulled up a display, showing the data stream rendered as three-dimensional geometry. "They're pointing us toward something. Somewhere deeper in the Sector. And the transmission structure suggests urgency. Either stay away or… or we need to go there. The translation is ambiguous."

Harper moved to Martinez's station, studying the display. "Can we ask for clarification?"

"I can try, sir."

Martinez sent another broadcast, this time incorporating the mathematical patterns they'd received—an attempt to acknowledge understanding and request more information. Nicole watched the anomaly, searching for any sign of response, any indication that they were actually communicating or just shouting into a void that happened to shout back.

The light on the anomaly's surface pulsed once. Twice. Then went dark.

"Signal's stopped," Martinez said. "It's just… gone. The entire transmission just cut off."

Nicole stared at the dark structure on the display. "Jiang, are you still reading power signatures?"

"Negative. It's gone completely inert. Like someone flipped a switch."

"Did we do something wrong?" Harper asked.

"Unknown, Captain."

The bridge was silent except for the quiet hum of systems. Nicole looked at the coordinates Martinez had extracted from the transmission—a location deep in the Procyon Sector, in a region marked as unexplored on their charts. A location they'd been told about by something utterly alien, with a warning or instruction they couldn't properly interpret.

First contact, she thought. And we don't even know if we're invited or being warned away.

"All right," Nicole said finally. "We document everything we just experienced. Full records, detailed analysis, everything. Martinez, I want you working with Jiang to extract every bit of data we can from that transmission. Rask, keep sensors on that anomaly. If it so much as hiccups, I want to know."

"And then?" Harper asked.

Nicole looked at him. At the captain who'd given her tactical command because he understood that some decisions needed to be made by people who'd spent their lives learning how. "Then we report back to Bastion Station. Command needs to know about this."

Harper nodded slowly, his gaze returning to the dark anomaly on the display. "And after that?"

Nicole pulled up the coordinates again. Studied the point in space that something vast and incomprehensible had directed them toward. "After that, we find out what they were trying to tell us."

She didn't say what she was thinking—that this wasn't first contact so much as first warning. That whatever they'd just communicated with had been waiting in the dark for something, and that humanity had finally arrived to trigger whatever came next.

But looking at Harper's expression, at the faces of her bridge crew, she suspected they were all thinking the same thing.

Something in the Dead Sector had woken up.

Chapter Five

Preparation

Nicole stood at the observation window and watched Carcosa-7 turn beneath the Manchineel's belly—a slow, cancerous rotation that consumed forty-three standard hours. Long enough for the dark side to remain dark. Long enough for things to grow wrong.

The planet glowed with bioluminescent forests, visible even from orbit. Beautiful in a way. Eerie and wrong, but beautiful.

Ree's reflection materialized in the transparisteel beside her own. "Torres sent updated schematics three hours ago."

Nicole summoned the facility layout on her wrist display. "The power distribution doesn't match the defensive grid. There's something they're running that's not on any official manifest."

"Does it matter if it's not between us and what we're taking?"

"Everything matters until we know it doesn't."

The Manchineel drifted in high orbit, stealth plating engaged and every non-essential system throttled down. They'd lurked here sixteen hours, hidden in the clutter of the planet's ring system—debris from three failed mining stations and one spectacularly expensive yacht that had tried to run a blockade fifty years ago. The rings gave them cover, but they also limited movement. One wrong burn and they'd light up every sensor on the planet.

Kade's voice crackled through the ship's internal comm. "We've got drift on the portside stabilizer. Nothing critical, but if we're here another day I'll need to do an external adjustment."

Nicole keyed the response panel. "How long?"

"Four hours in the suit. Maybe five if the mounting brackets are corroded."

"We're not staying another day." She killed the connection before Kade could argue.

Ree had moved to the tactical display, summoning the planetary overview. "Torres confirmed the transport schedule?"

"Every forty-eight hours. Next one arrives in twenty-two hours." Nicole joined her, studying the approach vectors. "Two guard shuttles escort it from the orbital platform to the surface. They don't vary the route."

"Arrogant."

"Confident. They've never had a successful breach." Nicole expanded the view to show the surface installation—a sprawl of reinforced structures built into a mountain range on the southern continent. "Most people who want someone out of Carcosa-7 try to bribe the guards or intercept the transport. The facility itself is supposed to be impenetrable."

"Supposed to be." Ree's smile could cut. "But Torres is inside."

Nicole summoned the guard rotation schedule, data only twelve hours old. "Which means we have someone who can open doors. We still need to reach those doors without triggering the defense grid."

She'd spent two days memorizing the facility's defenses. The outer ring was detection—seismic sensors, atmospheric monitors, orbital-linked scanners that could identify a ship's signature from three hundred kilometers out. The second layer was automated weapons platforms. The inner layer was two hundred security personnel with access to weapons technically illegal under the Syndicate's own regulations.

But this far into the dead sectors, regulations were suggestions written in disappearing ink.

Torres had given her the details. The shift changes, the blind spots, the maintenance schedules. The southeastern defense platform with a power coupling that overheated every seventy hours and required a fifteen-minute shutdown. The guard barracks with windows facing away from the primary approach vector because someone had prioritized the view over tactical sense.

She knew all of it because Torres had access to everything, and Torres was very good at being exactly what people expected to see.

"I need to contact him," Nicole said. "Confirm the timing."

Ree gave her a look that suggested this wasn't entirely about tactics, but didn't argue. "Make it quick. Even encrypted burst transmissions leave signatures."

* * *

The communications suite was barely large enough for one person and the equipment lining three walls. Nicole sealed the door and settled into the acceleration couch, her fingers dancing through the encryption protocols with practiced efficiency.

The secure channel took thirty seconds to establish—thirty seconds of encryption protocols handshaking while she watched the progress indicators and listened to her own breathing. Then the connection solidified. Audio only, compressed and bounced through four relay points.

"You're early." Torres's voice came through flat, stripped of inflection by compression.

Nicole kept her words efficient. "Power anomaly. Eastern complex. Explain."

"Medical. Ward Six." Background voices, distant and indistinct. He was somewhere public, somewhere he could talk without attracting attention but not speak freely. "Experimental treatment protocols. High-value prisoners. Not on your route."

She summoned the facility schematic, overlaying his information. Ward Six sat in the eastern complex, separated from the detention blocks by three security checkpoints and two kilometers of reinforced corridor. He was right—not on their route. But the power draw was significant enough to affect overall grid distribution.

"Alert protocols. Does medical override?"

"Isolated systems. Power stays constant." His voice dropped slightly. "Your route is clear, Nicole. Trust the plan."

The words sounded rehearsed. She wondered how many times he'd repeated them to himself over the past week, building the courage for what came next. How many times he'd stared at images of his sister and convinced himself this was worth the risk.

"Tell me about your sister," Nicole said.

Silence stretched across the compressed channel. When Torres spoke again, something human had crept back into his voice. "Her name is Kira. She's sixteen. The neural degradation started eight months ago—tremors at first, then memory loss, then seizures. The doctors say she has maybe a year without treatment."

"And with treatment?"

"Full recovery. But the gene therapy costs more than I'll make in ten years." A bitter laugh cut through the static. "The Syndicate takes care of its own, they say. Unless what's wrong with you costs too much to fix."

Nicole understood desperation. She'd built a career on recognizing it in others, calculating how far someone would go when pushed to their limits. But hearing it in Torres's voice—raw and unfiltered—made the abstract concrete. He wasn't just an asset. He was someone's brother, gambling everything on a promise from strangers.

"Twenty-two hours," she said quietly. "Maintenance access."

"Unsealed and looped. I'll have eyes on the approach." Footsteps echoed in the background. "Block Seven, Cell Ninety-Four. Minimum security rotation. He's been cooperative eighteen months, so they're complacent."

Nicole visualized the route. Twelve minutes if they moved fast. Fifteen if they hit additional security. Twenty if things went wrong but not catastrophically wrong.

"Guard response time," she said. "Barracks to Block Seven."

"Seven minutes standard. I can give you eight with a diversion to northwestern sector."

"Do it." She paused, choosing her next words carefully. "Torres. If this works, you take your sister and you run. Don't stay on Carcosa-7. Don't try to work your last shift. The moment we're clear, you disappear."

"I know the protocol."

"I'm not talking about protocol. I'm talking about survival." She kept her voice level. "The Syndicate will know someone helped us. They'll tear apart everyone's records looking for who. You need to be gone before they start looking."

"My sister can't travel yet. The seizures—"

"Hire medical transport. Use part of the advance I sent you. Get her stabilized and get out." Nicole summoned the timeline on her wrist display. "You save her by staying alive, not by being brave. Understood?"

Another pause. "Understood."

"Confirmation code when we land."

"Theta-Seven-Whisper. Anything else means compromised." Something in his voice cut through the compression. "Twenty-two hours. I'll be there."

The connection died. Nicole sat in the sudden silence, staring at the dark display. Every operation had a point where planning gave way to faith. Where spreadsheets and contingencies couldn't account for human variables. Whether Torres would follow through. Whether his nerve would hold. Whether desperation to save his sister would override self-preservation at the critical moment.

She'd bet her life and her crew's lives that it would.

Nicole cleared the equipment and threaded her way back through the narrow corridor to the main compartment.

* * *

Valen and Ree stood together at the tactical display, their conversation cutting off as Nicole entered. Valen turned to face her, arms crossed and expression skeptical.

"Torres confirmed?" he asked.

"Confirmed. The plan holds."

"The plan is ambitious." He gestured at the planetary overview still glowing on the display. "We're assaulting a maximum-security facility with five people and hoping an inside source can bypass the defenses. If Torres is compromised, we're dead. If the timing slips five minutes—"

"We're dead," Nicole finished. "You've mentioned that."

"I want everyone to understand the odds."

"Everyone understands." Nicole joined them at the display, studying the amber approach vectors Ree had been refining. "We're doing it anyway."

Valen was quiet, his expression showing the calculation of someone running probabilities and not liking the results. He was the newest crew member, recruited four months ago for his understanding of military-grade security systems and willingness to work outside legal boundaries. His record said dishonorable discharge from Syndicate Navy for refusing an order. The real story was more complicated and less noble, but Nicole hadn't pressed. Everyone on the Manchineel had pasts they'd rather not excavate.

"The client is paying enough to retire on," Valen said. "All of us. That kind of money means desperation."

"Or they think Voss is worth it."

"What could a mathematician know that justifies this much risk?"

Nicole had asked herself the same question. Military secrets. Financial data. Blackmail material on someone powerful enough that exposure would shift the balance of power in an entire sector. But the client had been clear: extract Voss, ask no questions, deliver him intact. The why didn't matter as long as the payment cleared.

"We're not being paid to know," she said. "We're being paid to extract."

"And if what he knows gets us killed after extraction?"

"Then we won't have to worry about spending the money."

Ree looked up from the tactical display. "Are we doing philosophy now? Because I thought we were doing planning."

Valen pushed away from the display. "I'm saying we should understand what we're walking into."

"We understand. Heavily defended facility, valuable prisoner, high payment. That's all we need." Ree summoned a new layer—the approach vector in amber against the planet's surface. "Now can we focus on surviving long enough to debate ethics later?"

The next four hours were spent reviewing until everyone could recite their roles from memory. Nicole walked through each stage, identifying complications and developing responses. The approach would be critical—using the glowing forests as cover, coming in low during night cycle when bioluminescence would obscure their thermal signature. The Manchineel's stealth plating would handle the rest, but they'd be vulnerable during descent.

Kade joined for the final review, jumpsuit stained with something probably hydraulic fluid. His expression suggested he'd wrestled whatever problem into submission.

"Stabilizer is compensated," he said, dropping into an acceleration couch. "We're good for high-stress maneuvering if needed."

Chapter Six

The Dead Sectors

Nicole watched the approach vector narrow as Veridia Station resolved from a glint to a geometric promise against the stellar backdrop. Nicole ran her hand along the crate's edge. Plasma rifles. Breaching charges. Enough firepower to start a small war or finish one quickly. "Your prices have gone up."

"My risks have gone up." Crane's smile didn't reach his eyes. "Word is someone's been asking about you, Bastion. Compact types. The kind who don't ask twice."

"Since never. But I trust your reputation." His eyes flickered to the Obsidian Pulse armor visible beneath her jacket. "And I trust that anyone who crosses you doesn't live long enough to warn others."

Nicole pocketed the chip and signaled Jace to load the crates. The deal was done. The weapons were secured. One more piece of the puzzle in place.

She was halfway to the door when Crane spoke again.

"Bastion." His voice had lost its merchant's warmth. "Whatever you're planning—whoever you're extracting—it's got people nervous. Powerful people. The kind who burn entire stations to keep their secrets."

"It's an observation." He turned back to his displays. "Dead Sectors aren't kind to people who attract too much attention. Try not to get us all killed."

Nicole stepped through the door without answering. She didn't make promises she couldn't keep.

Behind her, Veridia Station hummed with ten thousand lives, none of them knowing that in thirty hours, everything would change.

Chapter Seven

Approach

Nicole's breath caught as the first proximity alert sliced through the bridge's quiet hum.

She leaned forward, tension coiling between her shoulder blades. The navigation display refreshed with each sensor ping, and what it revealed sent ice through her stomach. Debris. Not the scattered dust and micrometeorites you'd expect at a system's edge, but wreckage—metal fragments, composite sheeting, hull plating twisted into shapes that spoke of violence rather than entropy.

"Getting a lot of scatter," Yann said from the pilot's station, his casual tone jarring against what they were seeing. His fingers danced across the haptic interface, adjusting their approach vector. "More than the surveys indicated."

"How much more?" Nicole kept her voice level, commander's calm, even as her mind churned through implications.

Yann highlighted three distinct debris fields on the display, each clustered around a different orbital zone. "This isn't one wreck. These are multiple ships, different alloys, different construction methods. This system's been seeing traffic for a while."

Marcus materialized in the doorway, coffee mug balanced in one hand. He absorbed the display at a glance, and Nicole watched his expression shift from curiosity to concern. "Multiple wrecks in the same system. That's either bad luck or bad intention."

"Navigation hazard, maybe," Yann offered, but he didn't sound convinced. "Or—"

"Or someone was defending something," Nicole finished.

The words hung between them like smoke. She pulled up the geological surveys from the Mandate's research division, scanning again through data she'd practically memorized during the journey. Seven planetary bodies, low colonization priority, trace heavy elements. Nothing about ruins. Nothing about prior inhabitation or archaeological significance.

Nothing about debris fields that suggested this region had witnessed combat or catastrophic losses.

"The Mandate's surveys mentioned none of this," she said, hearing the blade in her own voice. "Either their intelligence is worse than we thought, or they didn't think this was information we needed."

Marcus eased into the seat beside her, the careful movement of someone thinking through implications. "What else didn't they tell us?"

It was the question that had been circling her mind since Veridia Station, the one she'd tried to suppress during jump prep and the long days between stars. Now, staring at evidence of ships that had come to Carcosa-7 and never left, it surfaced with renewed urgency.

"Pull up the equipment manifest," she said. "The detailed one."

Marcus called it up, and they studied it in silence. Standard survey gear, atmospheric testing equipment, portable analysis stations—but buried in the middle were items that told a different story. High-resolution imaging arrays designed for penetrating dense materials. Seismic mapping tools sophisticated enough to chart deep subterranean structures. And what the manifest euphemistically described as "sample extraction apparatus" but which Nicole recognized as industrial-grade excavation equipment.

"They're not sending us to document," Marcus said quietly. "They're sending us to retrieve."

Nicole felt something cold settle in her chest. "The question is what. And why they need it badly enough to risk a crew out here."

"You want my opinion?" Yann swiveled from the pilot's station, and there was something hard in his expression Nicole hadn't seen before. "We're the cheap option. Whatever's down there, the Mandate thinks it's valuable enough to risk a small crew, but not valuable enough to commit serious resources. If we find something useful, they win. If we end up like those ships out there, they write it off and send someone else."

Marcus set down his coffee mug with deliberate care. "That's a hell of a thing to say twelve hours from planetary orbit."

"It's the truth." Yann highlighted their approach vector through the debris fields. "We've got fuel for one landing attempt and maybe two weeks of surface operations. No backup, no support, no extraction plan if things go wrong. You were both military—you know what this looks like."

Nicole did know. She'd been on the planning side of enough operations to recognize the signature of a mission designed by people who cared more about outcomes than personnel. Minimum viable resources, maximum ambiguous instructions. Success attributed to institutional wisdom, failure blamed on crew incompetence.

But recognizing it was one thing. Accepting it was another.

"We're not the cheap option," she said, putting command weight behind the words. "We're the experienced option. They chose us because we've operated in uncertain conditions before. Because we can adapt when intelligence proves incomplete." She met Yann's eyes, then Marcus's. "The debris fields change our risk assessment, not our mission. We approach cautiously, we document everything, and we pull back immediately if conditions become unstable. This is reconnaissance, not a suicide run."

"Is it?" Yann asked, not backing down. "Because from where I'm sitting, it looks like we're retracing the paths of ships that didn't make it home."

"Then we'll be smarter than they were." Nicole pulled up the imaging data of the ruins, the grainy photographs that had sparked this entire expedition. The massive structure, half-buried in sediment, architecture that looked grown rather than built. Beneath it, chambers extending down into the planet's crust like roots.

She needed to give them something to focus on besides their mortality. Something concrete.

"We have advantages those other ships didn't," she said. "We have the deep-scan data. We know what we're looking for. We're not stumbling blindly into this system—we're arriving prepared." She pulled up the tactical assessment. "Yann, talk me through the landing plan. I want everyone clear on procedures before we commit."

She watched him make the decision to engage with the mission rather than the philosophy. His shoulders settled, and he brought up topographical data overlaid with atmospheric readings.

"Primary landing zone is here, about two kilometers from the main structure. Ground's stable, minimal wind shear, clear approach corridor. We set down, establish base camp, then proceed on foot."

"Why not land closer?" Marcus asked.

"Because I don't know what kind of interference that thing generates, and I'd prefer to keep the ship somewhere I can reach quickly if we need to evacuate." Yann highlighted several alternative sites. "These are backup zones if the primary's compromised. And this is emergency extraction. If everything goes wrong, this is the rendezvous point."

Nicole studied the map, committing geography to memory. The ruins sat in what might have been a valley or impact crater, surrounded by elevated terrain that would provide natural vantage points. The primary landing zone offered good sight lines and multiple exit routes—standard security protocols that suggested Yann had more tactical training than his civilian credentials indicated.

Something to ask about later, if they survived to have a later.

"Surface temperature averages minus twenty during local day, minus forty at night," Yann continued. "Atmosphere's breathable but thin. Pressure suits recommended for extended operations."

"What about the structure itself?" Nicole asked. "Stability assessment?"

Marcus pulled up a structural analysis, cross-sections based on the deep-scan data. "These lower chambers concern me. They're too regular to be natural formations, but I can't identify the construction method. No visible support columns, no conventional load-bearing elements."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning it's either built with technology we don't understand, or it's going to collapse the moment we put weight on the floor." He zoomed in on one of the deeper chambers, a space roughly fifty meters across shaped like an inverted dome. "This one especially. It shouldn't exist at that depth without massive structural reinforcement."

The ship's proximity alarm chimed softly—they'd crossed into Carcosa-7's gravitational influence. The planet itself was still too distant to see as more than a dim point of light, but Nicole felt the shift in her chest, that familiar transition from planning to execution.

"We approach systematically," she said. "Document everything, take no unnecessary risks, and maintain constant communication with the ship. At the first sign of structural instability or any condition we can't account for, we pull back and reassess." She looked at each of them in turn. "I'm not losing anyone to retrieve artifacts for people who couldn't be bothered to give us complete intelligence."

"The Mandate's expecting us to bring back something that justifies the expense," Yann said, but there was less edge in it now.

"They'll get what they get." Nicole stood, feeling the ache in her muscles from too many hours in the command chair. "Twelve hours until orbital position. Standard watch rotation—Marcus first, Yann second, I'll take third and bring us through final approach. Everyone get some rest."

As they dispersed to their quarters, Nicole caught Marcus's eye. That look of recognition passed between them, the shared understanding that had first drawn them together on Veridia Station. He knew what she was doing—choosing personnel survival over mission objectives, even when institutional pressure suggested otherwise.

He nodded once, brief and certain, then headed to take his watch.

In her cabin, barely large enough for a bunk and storage locker, Nicole stripped off her duty uniform and stretched out in the darkness. Her mind wouldn't quiet—it never did before operations. She thought about the debris fields, evidence of ships that had come to Carcosa-7 seeking something valuable enough to die for. About Yann's tactical precision and the military experience he'd never mentioned. About Marcus on the bridge, maintaining their approach through a graveyard of failed expeditions.

About what the Mandate could possibly want badly enough to send them here with incomplete intelligence and insufficient support.

The ship's engines thrummed beneath her, steady and mechanical, pushing them deeper into the system. Outside her viewport, stars wheeled slowly past, their light ancient and indifferent.

Nicole closed her eyes, letting the familiar pre-mission calm settle over her. They had a plan. They had a crew. They had just enough resources to make this work if nothing went catastrophically wrong.

And if something did go wrong—when something went wrong, because something always did—they would adapt. They would survive.

She had to believe that. Had to believe her team was more than the Mandate's cheap gamble, more than acceptable losses in someone else's calculation.

Tomorrow they would begin their descent. Everything they'd worried about during the long journey from Veridia Station would collide with reality.

But tonight, for these last hours before commitment, Nicole clung to one certainty: whatever the Mandate had sent them to retrieve, whatever secrets Carcosa-7 had kept buried for centuries, she would bring her crew home.

The thought carried her toward uneasy sleep. Outside, the planet swelled larger in the darkness, waiting with the answers to questions they were only beginning to understand how to ask.

Chapter Eight

Infiltration

The approach vector Torres had provided brought them through Carcosa-7's shadow, where the binary stars carved a narrow cone of darkness that wheeled with the planet's orbit. Nicole watched the chronometer gnaw away at their window—the brief interval before rotation exposed them to the full fury of both suns at once.

"Thirty-two minutes," she said. "Then we're lit up like a beacon."

Torres leaned over the console beside her, his fingers ghosting patterns across the display without making contact. "The detection grid resets every forty minutes. Keyed to local star position. They built it around the binary, thought they were being clever."

"And the gap?"

"Eight minutes when the grid expects both stars at position A but Carcosa's already spun past threshold. Old software. Nobody's patched it in six years." He summoned a secondary screen—layers of overlapping sensor fields rendered in amber and arterial red. "We'll drop here, in the reset window. Forty-minute descent puts you groundside with those eight minutes to reach the station entrance before everything awakens."

Nicole absorbed the approach pattern, burning each angle into memory. The pod she'd be taking wasn't built for evasive maneuvers. Once she launched, the trajectory was carved in stone. If Torres's timing slipped by even thirty seconds, every gun emplacement on the northern platform would track her descent and paint the sky with her remains.

"The facility layout," she said, pulling up the schematics he'd provided. Half the corridors wore question marks like badges of uncertainty, whole sections labeled with nothing but estimated dimensions.

Torres painted three corridors purple. "These zones run neural verification grids. Passive scanning, harvesting ambient brain activity. When you're in them, you keep your mind empty. No planning, no anticipating."

"That's not how infiltration works."

"It is when the walls read your thoughts." Something almost apologetic flickered across Torres's features. "I've walked them. Four times. The trick is to imagine yourself somewhere else entirely. Let muscle memory pilot the body while your mind shelters somewhere safe."

Nicole studied him directly. "Where did you go?"

Torres went quiet, his eyes on the display but his focus bleeding through it to somewhere beyond. "The ocean. Place on Venter-9 where I grew up. I'd walk those corridors thinking about nothing but waves breaking, salt thick in the air. Every step inside those zones, I was there instead."

The chronometer bled down to twenty-five minutes. Nicole initiated the pre-launch sequence for the insertion pod, threading through the checklist with automatic precision. Each item verified and locked.

"You're quiet," Torres said.

"I'm listening."

"That's not what I mean." He gestured at the pod's display. "Whatever you're hunting on Carcosa-7, it's not just an extraction."

"Marcus Shen is down there because someone buried him there. Someone who knew exactly what that facility does to people. I'm going to drag him out, and then I'm going to find out who."

"And if he's not the same person you remember?"

The question settled between them, carrying weight Torres probably couldn't fully measure. Nicole conjured Marcus as she'd last seen him—the way his hands moved when he spoke, the particular angle of his smile when he was about to say something he knew would needle her.

"Then I deal with that after he's out," she said.

Torres nodded and produced a small case from his jacket, setting it on the console between them. "Bypass tools, biometric mirrors, three emergency beacons. The beacons will summon Authority response within minutes, so only trigger them if you're already dead otherwise."

Nicole accepted the case, gauging its heft. "You're not coming."

"I'm your exit window. Someone has to orbit up here to catch your extraction signal." Torres met her eyes. "Besides, I burned my access codes the day I left. I materialize on Carcosa-7's network, every alarm in the facility screams simultaneously."

"They really despise defectors."

"They don't believe in them. You leave Authority security, you're already erased from their files. A ghost." Torres summoned a secondary display showing the planetary surface in real-time. "Five minutes to launch window. After you're down, I'll haunt this shadow cone for six hours, maybe seven before I have to break away."

Nicole sealed the equipment case and moved toward the pod bay, Torres trailing. The pod itself was stripped naked—barely enough volume for a single occupant and minimal cargo. No weapons, no defensive systems, nothing that would register on active scans as anything more than debris.

"You'll punch the atmosphere hard," Torres said, watching her stow the case. "Heat shielding's minimal. The pod's designed to fragment on descent, shed its outer shell to reduce signature. You'll be cocooned inside a disintegrating carcass for the last two minutes of fall."

Nicole climbed into the pod, settling into the acceleration couch and dragging the harness across her chest. She rehearsed the controls once, twice, committing each function to immediate recall.

Torres positioned himself in the bay doorway, his hand resting on the launch control panel. "The beacon signal you're hunting is on a restricted frequency. I've programmed it into your comm unit. When you're close enough to Shen, it activates automatically. Follow it to his location, but remember the facility's built vertically. You might be tracking him through three levels of reinforced structure."

"Understood."

"And Nicole?" Torres's voice dropped lower now, almost swallowed by the pod's environmental systems humming to life. "If it collapses, if you can't reach him or he's too far gone, you extract yourself. One person's not worth dying for."

Nicole locked eyes with him through the viewport. "You've never been in love."

Torres's mouth compressed, but he didn't challenge her. He stepped back and triggered the launch sequence. The bay doors began their slow separation, unveiling Carcosa-7 below. The planet hung against the void, its surface mottled grey-brown streaked with darker veins. The binary stars cast conflicting shadows across its face, breeding zones of doubled light and absolute darkness.

"Launch in sixty seconds," Torres said through the comm. "May the current carry you home."

"Wrong ocean," Nicole said, but she appreciated the sentiment.

The countdown marched forward with mechanical inevitability. Nicole disciplined her breathing, coaxing her heart rate down. She'd done this before—dropped into hostile territory with minimal equipment and a razor-thin window. But never for someone like Marcus. Never for someone who'd touched her in ways she still felt years later, like scars that had healed into her skin.

The memory surfaced without invitation.

The training facility on Bastion-3 had observation decks on every floor, glass-enclosed spaces where recruits could gaze out at the city and remember there was a world beyond the endless conditioning cycles. Nicole found herself there after a particularly vicious simulation, one that had slaughtered her avatar six times in fifteen minutes and left her actual body trembling with phantom pain.

The city lights sprawled beneath her like fallen stars. She pressed her palm against the glass, drinking in its coolness, trying to anchor herself back in physical reality.

"They design them that way on purpose."

She'd noticed him around the facility, knew his reputation as someone who excelled at cognitive challenges while fumbling the physical ones. The inverse of her own strengths.

"Well, it's working."

"You died six times because you kept hammering the same approach. Frontal assault, aggressive positioning. But the simulation was engineered to counter exactly that."

Marcus extended his hand. Nicole reached out to take it, expecting a brief professional shake. But when their palms met, when his fingers wrapped around hers with unexpected warmth, she felt something shift. Not just recognition but resonance, as though some frequency she'd been unconsciously broadcasting had finally found its match.

They held the contact longer than necessary, both aware of it but neither pulling away. The observation deck's ambient lighting cast doubled shadows behind them, and through the glass, the city continued its endless churning motion.

"Tomorrow then," Marcus said, but he still didn't release her hand.

"Tomorrow," Nicole confirmed.

When they finally separated, she felt the absence of his touch like a sudden plunge in temperature. She watched him leave, his stride purposeful but unhurried, and pressed her palm back against the glass where its coolness felt sharper now by contrast.

She didn't know then what that moment would become, how that first touch would multiply across years into something that defined her understanding of connection. All she knew was that her hand still held warmth where his had been, and that she was already counting the hours until tomorrow.

Chapter Nine

The Cell

Nicole's boots made no sound on the detention level floor. The material swallowed each step, designed to prevent warning echoes—Bastion's own security measures now working against them.

Torres moved beside her, weapon drawn, sweeping each intersection with practiced efficiency. Behind them, Kael monitored their path through a handheld display he'd stripped from a wall panel three levels up.

"Two guards at the central processing node," Kael whispered. "One hundred meters ahead, left junction."

Nicole nodded. She'd committed the detention level schematics to memory during the descent, every corridor mapped in her mind. The cells occupied three concentric rings, categorized by security level. Political detainees in the outer ring. Research subjects in the middle. High-value prisoners in the core.

Marcus would be in the core. If he was here at all.

The manifest Torres had accessed showed a prisoner matching Marcus's physical description in cell seventeen. Age appropriate. Height correct. Medical records indicated a left leg injury treated fourteen years ago—the same leg Marcus had shattered during their last mission together, when the extraction from Voslen-4 had gone catastrophic and he'd stayed behind to buy her time.

Twenty-five years since she'd seen his face.

Torres held up a closed fist. Nicole froze, pressing herself against the wall.

Voices drifted from the left junction. Two guards, their conversation casual, bored. Complaining about shift rotations and commissary food. Normal concerns. The kind of mundane grievances that reminded Nicole these were people, not obstacles.

She met Torres's eyes. He gestured toward his weapon, then toward the guards, a question in his expression.

Nicole shook her head. The sedative rounds they'd loaded would work, but the sound would echo. Instead, she pointed to herself, then to the junction, then made a motion like falling.

Torres frowned but nodded.

Nicole stepped into the intersection and stumbled, catching herself against the corridor's edge, gasping as if in pain.

The guards reacted instantly, weapons rising. "Freeze! Identify yourself!"

"Please," Nicole said, injecting panic into her voice. "Please, I'm lost. The alarm—I got separated from my research team. I don't know how I ended up down here."

She wore the lab coat they'd stolen from storage on level twelve, complete with clearance badge clipped to the breast pocket. Not detention-level clearance, but enough to suggest legitimate presence.

The guards exchanged glances. The taller one, a woman with close-cropped gray hair, lowered her weapon slightly. "Research personnel aren't authorized on this level."

"I know, I know. The emergency protocols—everything locked down and I ran the wrong direction. I'm sorry. I just need to get back to sector nine."

The second guard, younger, male, kept his weapon trained on her center mass. "We need to verify your credentials. Stay where you are."

Nicole raised her hands, keeping them visible. "Of course. My badge should have all my information."

The woman stepped closer, reaching for Nicole's badge. Torres emerged from the shadows behind them, sedative rounds catching both guards before they could turn. The hiss of compressed air, the soft impact against their necks, the surprised expressions that lasted only seconds before unconsciousness claimed them.

They crumpled in succession.

Nicole knelt beside the guards, checking their pulses. Strong and steady. The sedative would keep them under for six hours minimum. She helped Torres drag them into a maintenance alcove, positioning them so they looked like they'd taken a break rather than been incapacitated. It wouldn't survive scrutiny, but it might buy them a few extra minutes before anyone noticed.

Torres pulled the woman's comm unit and quickly modified its transponder. "If anyone pings them, this will auto-respond with their usual status codes. Should keep central from getting suspicious."

"How long?" Nicole asked.

"Ten minutes before the next checkpoint. Maybe fifteen if we're lucky."

Kael emerged from cover, already moving. "Then we don't waste time."

They pushed deeper into the detention level, the architecture closing in around them as they approached the core ring. Security intensified proportionally—reinforced doors, biometric locks, automated monitoring systems that Kael disabled with increasing urgency. Each system he bypassed added another few seconds to their exposure, another chance for security to notice the intrusion.

Nicole's mind tracked the variables. The sedated guards. The modified comms. The disabled monitoring systems. Each intervention left traces, patterns that station security would eventually recognize. They were walking a knife's edge between stealth and discovery.

The central node occupied a circular chamber where all three rings converged. Banks of monitors displayed cell interiors, vital signs of prisoners, security status updates. Nicole's eyes swept across the screens, searching for cell seventeen.

There.

The monitor showed a small space, three meters by four, containing a bed, sanitation unit, and a man sitting with his back against the wall. The image resolution was poor, deliberately degraded to reduce data load, but Nicole recognized the posture. The way he held his shoulders, the angle of his head.

Her breath caught. Twenty-five years collapsed into this moment.

"Is that him?" Torres asked quietly.

Nicole couldn't speak. She nodded.

Kael was already working on the cell's access controls, his fingers dancing across the interface. Seconds passed. Then a minute. His frown deepened.

"Problem?" Torres asked.

"Security on this level is layered. Physical locks backed by electronic fail-safes backed by biometric authorization. I can override the first two, but the third requires command-level clearance we don't have."

"How long to bypass it?" Nicole found her voice, though it sounded distant to her own ears.

"Twenty minutes. Maybe thirty. And that's if I don't trigger any alerts."

Nicole checked the time. They'd been on this level for seven minutes. "We don't have thirty minutes."

Torres moved to one of the other terminals, studying the detention protocols. "There might be another way. Emergency evacuation procedures—if the system detects an environmental hazard, it can unlock cells for controlled evacuation."

"What kind of hazard?" Kael asked.

"Fire suppression failure. Atmospheric contamination. Radiation leak." Torres scrolled through the options. "Any of them would trigger cell unlocks, but they'd also trigger immediate security response. Unless…"

"Unless what?" Nicole pressed.

"Unless we make it look like a false alarm. Trigger the unlock, then immediately send an all-clear before security can mobilize. The cells would open but stay open—the system would think the evacuation was completed."

Kael shook his head. "Security would still respond. They'd see the cell unlocks in the logs."

"Not if we edit the logs simultaneously. Make it look like a system glitch rather than an actual evacuation event."

Nicole moved closer to Torres's terminal. "Show me."

Torres pulled up the emergency protocols. "Here. If we trigger a localized radiation alert in the core ring, it unlocks all cells in that ring for evacuation. Thirty-seven cells total. We'd have maybe ninety seconds between unlock and security arrival."

Nicole stared at the protocol. Thirty-seven high-value prisoners. Murderers, traitors, compromised assets. People locked away for good reason. "And the other prisoners? What happens to them?"

"The security response would focus on containing them. We'd slip out during the chaos."

"Containing them how? Sedatives? Weapons?"

Torres met her eyes. "Whatever it takes to protect the station. These are dangerous people, Nicole."

"I know what they are." Nicole looked at the monitors showing the other cells. Faces she didn't recognize, stories she couldn't know. But she knew each one represented someone who'd been deemed too dangerous to release. "We can't do this. We can't release them just to create a diversion."

"Then what do you suggest?" Torres asked, not unkindly.

Nicole's mind raced. "There has to be another way. Could we fake the biometric authorization? Get genetic material from a command officer?"

"We'd need fresh samples," Kael said. "Hair, skin, blood. And we'd need to know which officers have core-level clearance. That information isn't in the public directories."

"What about physical breach? Could we cut through the cell door?"

"Not without explosives. The doors are reinforced duranium. We'd need shaped charges, which we don't have, and the sound would bring every guard on the station."

Nicole looked back at Marcus's monitor. He sat motionless, back against the wall, seemingly at peace. Twenty-five years he'd been here. She thought about him waiting in that cell while she'd built a new life, gathered resources, planned this rescue. She thought about the debt she owed him.

She also thought about the guards they'd sedated—people just doing their jobs. About Torres and Kael, who'd risked everything to help her. About thirty-seven prisoners who might kill or die if released. About the weight of a decision that would ripple outward in ways she couldn't predict.

"I need another option," she said firmly. "We don't release prisoners. We don't compromise our principles just because it's convenient."

Torres and Kael exchanged glances. "Nicole," Torres said carefully, "sometimes there is no clean option."

"Then we find one."

"There might not be time."

"Then we make time."

Kael turned back to his terminal, frowning in concentration. "Let me think. The biometric lock is the barrier. It requires DNA verification from authorized personnel. We can't fake that without samples we don't have. We can't bypass it without more time than we have." His fingers stilled. "Wait. What if we don't bypass it?"

"I'm listening," Nicole said.

"The biometric system processes authorization checks in real-time during normal operations. But during shift changes, there's a queue buffer—a sixty-second window when the system stores authorization requests instead of processing them immediately. If we could inject a false authorization into that queue, make Marcus's cell think it's already been approved…"

"Would that work?" Torres asked.

"In theory. The timing would have to be perfect. We'd need to hit the exact moment when the shift change queue opens."

"When's the next shift change?" Nicole asked.

Kael checked the system. "Eighteen minutes."

Nicole felt the timeline pressing in. "We don't have eighteen minutes. Station security will notice those guards missing in—" she checked her chronometer, "—three minutes."

"Then we buy ourselves more time," Torres said. He was already moving to another terminal. "I'll create a diversion. Something that makes it look like the guards are actively responding to an incident."

"They'll see through it eventually," Kael warned.

"They only need to believe it for fifteen minutes."

Nicole studied Torres's face. He was offering to extend their exposure, to shoulder additional risk, all to avoid the moral compromise of releasing dangerous prisoners. It was the kind of choice that reminded her why she'd chosen this team.

But even as she nodded approval, she felt the weight of the decision. They were betting everything on a theoretical exploit with perfect timing. If it failed, Marcus would remain imprisoned. If it succeeded but they got caught, they'd all end up in cells. And if Torres's diversion was too obvious, security would lock down everything.

"What kind of diversion?" she asked.

Torres's fingers flew across the interface. "Minor containment breach in the hydroponics sector. Nothing dangerous, but it requires immediate response—water leaking into electrical systems, potential fire hazard. The kind of thing that sounds urgent but isn't catastrophic."

"Will they believe it?"

"The sensors I'm triggering are real. Actual water pressure anomalies I'm introducing into the hydroponics feed lines. Security will see genuine sensor data, not fabricated alerts. By the time they realize it's a minor issue, we'll have our window."

He executed the command. Somewhere in the station, alarms began to wail.

Nicole watched the security feeds. Guards mobilized, abandoning their posts to respond to the apparent emergency. The detention level emptied of patrols, leaving only automated systems between them and Marcus's cell.

"Fourteen minutes," Kael said, his voice tight with concentration. "Shift change buffer opens in fourteen minutes."

They waited. Nicole's heart hammered against her ribs, each second stretching into an eternity. On the monitor, Marcus hadn't moved. He sat with the stillness of someone who'd learned that hope was a luxury prisoners couldn't afford.

I'm here, she thought. I'm coming.

The minutes crawled past. Twelve. Ten. Eight.

"Security's returning," Torres warned. "Hydroponics breach contained faster than expected."

"How long?" Nicole demanded.

"Three minutes. Maybe four."

Kael's hands trembled over the console. "Buffer opens in six minutes. We're not going to make it."

Nicole made a decision. "Torres, buy us more time. Whatever it takes."

He met her eyes, understanding passing between them. Then he was moving, slipping back into the corridors they'd cleared, heading toward the returning guards.

"What's he doing?" Kael asked.

"His job." Nicole forced herself to watch the monitors, to trust that Torres knew what he was doing. "Focus on that buffer."

The seconds bled away. On the security feeds, she saw Torres intercept the guards, gesturing urgently, leading them away from the detention level. She couldn't hear what he was saying, but whatever story he was selling, they were buying it.

Five minutes. Four. Three.

"Buffer's opening," Kael breathed. "Injecting authorization now."

The console flickered. Error codes cascaded across the screen, then resolved into a single green confirmation.

Authorization accepted. Cell 17 unlocked.

The door to Marcus's cell slid open.

On the monitor, the man inside looked up. For twenty-five years he'd heard that door seal shut. Now it stood open, and he stared at it like he was seeing a ghost.

Nicole was already running.

She reached the cell in seconds, skidding to a stop in the doorway. Marcus rose slowly, his movements careful, uncertain. Age had claimed him—silver hair, lines carved deep into his face, a body that had surrendered muscle to time and confinement.

But his eyes. God, his eyes were the same.

"Nicole?" His voice cracked on her name, rough from disuse. "That's not—you can't be—"

"We need to move." She reached for him, her hand closing around his arm. Real. Solid. Alive. "Security will be here in minutes."

He didn't move. His gaze traveled across her face, cataloging changes, searching for the woman he'd known. "You came for me."

"I came for you."

Something broke behind his eyes. Twenty-five years of walls, crumbling in an instant.

Then his expression hardened. "You shouldn't have. Nicole, you don't understand what I know. What they'll do to get it back."

"Tell me later." She pulled him toward the door. "Right now, we run."

Behind them, alarms began to scream.

Chapter Ten

Extraction

Nicole pressed her palm against the biometric scanner, the seconds stretching as the system digested her stolen credentials. The detention level corridor yawned empty in both directions, but she could hear the distant rhythm of patrol boots echoing through the ventilation system. Harkin had given her a twelve-minute window while security chased a phantom alert in the medical bay. She'd already burned three of those minutes navigating down from the maintenance sublevel, another two coaxing the checkpoint scanner to accept codes that shouldn't have worked but did.

The panel flashed green. The door whispered open on hydraulic breath.

The cell beyond was smaller than she'd expected. Three meters by four, barely enough room for the cot bolted to the far wall and the sanitation unit wedged in the corner. The man on the cot didn't stir when she entered. His breathing came steady, unconscious from whatever sedative they'd pumped into him to keep him compliant.

She recognized his face from the surveillance footage Harkin had shown her. Marcus Kellen. The man who'd supposedly breached Bastion's containment protocols and exposed them all to whatever had been locked in Sector Nine. His features were harder than in the files, his jaw shadowed with stubble, his hair matted against his skull.

Nicole crossed to the cot and pressed two fingers against his throat. His pulse beat strong beneath her touch. Good. She wouldn't have time to revive him if they'd overdosed the sedatives.

"Marcus." She kept her voice low, barely above a whisper. "Marcus Kellen."

No response. His eyelids didn't even flutter.

She pulled the stim injector from her belt and pressed it against his carotid. The device hissed as it released its payload directly into his bloodstream. She'd calibrated the dose herself—enough to counteract the sedatives but not so much that it would shock his system into arrest.

His eyes snapped open.

For three seconds he stared at the ceiling, his pupils contracting in the amber light. Then his gaze shifted to her face, and every muscle in his body went rigid.

"Easy." She held her hands where he could see them, the injector visible in her palm. "I'm not here to hurt you. My name is Nicole Chen. I'm getting you out of here, but we need to move now. Can you stand?"

His jaw worked, but no sound emerged. His eyes tracked from her face to the door, calculating distances and possibilities with the desperate focus of someone who'd learned not to trust rescue.

"Chen." His voice came rough, damaged by intubation or screaming or both. "The weapons researcher."

"Right now I'm someone who needs you mobile." She moved to the door, checking the corridor through the narrow window. Still empty. Eight minutes left on Harkin's diversion. "We have maybe five minutes before patrol rounds bring guards past this cell. Can you walk?"

He pushed himself up on his elbows, managed to swing his legs off the cot. His hands trembled. "Where's your team?"

"I don't have a team." She pulled her sidearm, checked the charge. "Stay behind me. Don't speak unless I tell you to."

Marcus stood slowly, steadied himself against the wall. The stimulant was working faster now—she could see clarity returning to his eyes. "Did they hurt you?"

"Define hurt."

"Can you fight if you have to?"

"If I have to." He pushed away from the wall, took three steps toward her. His gait was unsteady but determined. "Where are we going?"

"Maintenance shaft seven-delta. It connects to an auxiliary corridor that isn't monitored. Then we access the cargo transfer station." She opened the door and slipped into the corridor. "Stay close."

Nicole moved quickly but carefully, her boots silent against the floor. Marcus followed, his breathing audible but controlled as the stimulant continued burning through the sedatives. She turned left, heading for the maintenance access point Harkin had marked on her stolen map.

They made it to the first intersection when the lights shifted from amber to red.

No alarms yet—just the visual warning that the security system had registered an anomaly. Nicole's pulse spiked. Too soon. The diversion should have held for another five minutes at least.

Unless Harkin had been discovered. Unless someone had checked the monitoring logs and found the gaps where Nicole had slipped through blind spots. Unless—

The thought severed as she heard voices ahead. Guard frequencies, discussing search patterns. They weren't responding to an alarm yet, but they would be soon.

"Change of plans." Nicole grabbed Marcus's arm and pulled him toward a side corridor. "Move."

They ran now, speed more important than stealth. Behind them, a comm unit crackled—someone calling in coordinates, reporting an open cell door, initiating lockdown procedures. The full alarm would shriek any second.

The maintenance access appeared on her right, a square panel set into the wall at knee height. Nicole dropped to a crouch and worked her fingers into the recessed grip. The panel resisted for a moment. Behind them, the voices swelled—guards entering their corridor, systematic and efficient.

She pulled harder. The panel's internal mechanism had corroded, the metal edges caught against their housing. Nicole braced her feet and wrenched with all her strength.

The panel finally tore free. She gestured frantically to the opening. "Inside. Go."

Marcus dropped flat and crawled into the maintenance shaft, his body disappearing into the darkness beyond. Nicole followed immediately, dragging the panel back into place just as the three-tone alarm began its cycle through the detention block.

The shaft was barely wide enough for her shoulders. The air tasted of recycled atmosphere and industrial lubricant. Somewhere ahead, Marcus was already moving, his breathing audible in the confined space. Nicole crawled after him, her weapon pressed against her side, her muscles burning with the effort of dragging herself forward on her elbows.

She could hear guards in the corridor they'd just abandoned—voices calling out grid references, footsteps moving in coordinated patterns. Someone was examining the corridor systematically, checking every access panel, every potential hiding spot.

Nicole forced herself to move faster. The shaft extended for twelve meters before connecting to a junction box. She could see Marcus ahead, silhouetted against the faint glow of the junction's indicator lights. He'd reached the access point and was waiting for her.

She caught up to him, her breath coming harder than she'd like. "You remember how to open these?"

"I remember." His hands moved across the junction's control surface, inputting the maintenance code that would grant them access to the auxiliary corridor. His movements were steadier now, the last traces of sedation burned away by necessity. "These haven't changed since I worked here."

"You worked maintenance?"

"I worked everything. Part of the job." The junction panel flashed green. He pulled the release lever and the hatch swung open, revealing the corridor beyond. "After you."

Nicole pulled herself through the opening and stood, her legs grateful for the chance to straighten. The auxiliary corridor was narrower than the main passages, designed for equipment transport rather than personnel. Emergency lighting cast everything in the same amber glow that permeated the entire detention level.

Marcus emerged behind her, moving with increasing confidence. The stimulant had fully counteracted the sedatives. His eyes had cleared, his movements sharp and controlled. He looked like someone who'd remembered how to be dangerous.

"Which way?" he asked.

Nicole consulted the map in her memory. "South sixty meters, then up one level through the service elevator. From there we access the cargo transfer station."

"They'll be watching the elevator."

"They'll be watching all the standard routes. That's why we're not taking standard routes." She started walking, keeping her pace just below a run. "The cargo transfer station has three access points. Two are monitored. The third hasn't been used in six months."

They reached the service elevator without encountering guards. The doors opened at Nicole's touch, revealing a cargo platform large enough to hold six equipment crates. She'd spent an hour reviewing security footage—no cameras inside, another oversight in Bastion's aging infrastructure.

They stepped inside. Nicole pressed the control for level three, and the elevator began to rise with a mechanical groan that set her teeth on edge.

"Why are you doing this?" Marcus asked.

The question caught her off-guard. She'd expected tactical queries, strategic planning, not introspection. "What?"

"Rescuing me. Risking everything to break me out." He leaned against the elevator wall, his arms crossed. "You don't know me. We've never met. Why?"

Nicole considered lying. It would be easier to claim altruism, to say she couldn't stand by while an innocent man was imprisoned. But Marcus Kellen didn't strike her as someone who appreciated comfortable falsehoods.

"Because you know what happened in Sector Nine," she said. "And I need to know if what I built caused it."

His expression shifted. Something like recognition flickered across his features. "The resonance weapon. You were part of that project."

"I led that project." The elevator shuddered as it passed level two. Almost there. "Whatever you saw in Sector Nine, whatever you activated or encountered or released—I need to know if it was connected to my work."

"And if it was?"

"Then I need to know how to stop it."

The elevator reached level three and the doors slid open. Nicole stepped out into another corridor, this one wider than the auxiliary passage but still empty of personnel. The cargo transfer station lay two hundred meters ahead, through a security checkpoint and past a storage depot.

Marcus followed her into the corridor. His posture had changed. He stood straighter, his movements more confident. The stimulant had finished its work, bringing him fully back from whatever sedated haze they'd kept him in.

"I can't tell you if it was your weapon," he said. "Because I don't know what I activated."

"Tell me what you saw."

"I saw—" He stopped walking. His face had gone pale. "I saw something that shouldn't exist. Something that defies physics and biology and every understanding I have of how reality functions."

Nicole turned to face him. "Then help me understand it. Help me find a way to—"

The explosion came from somewhere above them.

The corridor shuddered. Dust rained from the ceiling. Emergency lights flickered and died, replaced a moment later by backup power running at half intensity.

"What the hell was that?" Marcus braced himself against the wall.

Nicole's mind raced through possibilities. Harkin's diversion was supposed to be subtle—a false security alert, maybe a minor system malfunction. Not an explosion. Not something that would cause structural damage to the station itself.

Unless it wasn't Harkin.

Unless something else was happening on Bastion. Something her carefully researched plan hadn't accounted for. "We need to move." She started running, no longer concerned with stealth. If that explosion was what she thought it was, every security protocol on the station would be activating. Lockdown procedures. Emergency response teams. Armed guards flooding every corridor looking for threats.

Marcus ran beside her, matching her pace. "Where's the extraction point?"

"Docking bay seventeen. Through the cargo transfer station, past the—"

Three guards emerged from a side passage ahead.

Nicole saw them the same moment they saw her. Time seemed to compress as everyone's training kicked in simultaneously. The guards reached for their weapons. Nicole raised her sidearm. Marcus dove for cover behind a support pillar.

She fired first.

The weapon's discharge lit up the corridor in strobing white light. The first guard dropped, center mass, before his hand touched his holster. The second spun with the impact, slamming against the wall. The third managed to draw, managed to aim, managed to squeeze the trigger—

Nicole was already moving. The shot scorched past her shoulder, close enough to sear fabric. She fired twice more, controlled bursts that punched through body armor at this range. The guard crumpled.

Silence crashed back into the corridor, broken only by the distant wail of alarms and the rasp of her own breathing.

"Clear," she said, scanning for additional threats. "Move."

They stepped over the bodies. Marcus paused, looking down at the fallen guards with an expression she couldn't read.

"They were just doing their jobs," he said quietly.

"And we're doing ours." Nicole grabbed his arm. "Mourn later. Survive now."

The cargo transfer station loomed ahead—a cavernous space filled with shipping containers and loading equipment. Through the far window, she could see docking bay seventeen. The Whisper waited there, engines hot, Kira ready to punch them into the black the moment they were aboard.

Two hundred meters. They could make it.

They were halfway across the station when Marcus stopped dead.

"Nicole."

Something in his voice made her turn. He was staring at her—not at the escape route, not at the guards who might be pursuing them, but at her face. His eyes held a strange intensity, as if he was seeing her for the first time.

"You said your name was Chen," he said slowly. "Nicole Chen."

"We don't have time for—"

"I know you." His voice cracked. "Twenty-five years ago. Europa Station. You were a recruit. I was—" He swallowed hard. "I was your commanding officer."

The words hit her like a physical blow. She'd known, of course. Known since the moment she'd seen his file. But hearing him say it, watching recognition dawn across features aged by decades of captivity—

"Marcus." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "We need to go."

"You came back for me." Wonder and anguish warred in his expression. "After everything. After I—" He couldn't finish.

"I never stopped looking." She reached for him, her hand finding his. Warm. Real. Alive. "I never believed you were dead. And I was right."

Behind them, boots thundered on metal. Shouts echoed through the transfer station. Security was closing in.

Marcus squeezed her hand once, hard. Then his jaw set with the determination she remembered from another lifetime.

"Then let's give them something to chase."

They ran together toward the docking bay, toward the ship, toward a future neither of them had dared to imagine.

Behind them, Bastion's alarms screamed into the void.

Chapter Eleven

Escape

Nicole's breath snagged in her chest as the door whispered open, revealing the figure crumpled on the cot.

Marcus lifted his head, and for a suspended moment neither of them moved. His face had whittled down to sharper planes than she remembered—cheekbones jutting, stubble patching his jaw in uneven scruff. Bruised shadows pooled beneath his eyes, but those eyes—they remained unchanged. Gray-blue and keen, even now.

"Nicole." Her name scraped out of him, disbelieving.

She needed to speak. Anything. But the words wedged in her throat, snarled with everything that had fractured between them, everything left unspoken during their last transmission before his ship had dissolved into the Meridian Drift.

"We need to move," she managed. Her voice rang foreign in her own ears. "Now."

Marcus hauled himself upright. He moved stiffly, favoring his left side. When he stepped forward, she caught the slight hitch in his gait.

"They know you're here?" he asked.

"Not yet. But that changes in about ninety seconds."

He nodded, closing the small space between them. For a heartbeat he stood close enough that she could taste the stale air of confinement on him, could trace the new scar that cleaved through his eyebrow. Then he was past her, melting into the corridor.

Nicole followed, the weight of the moment settling into her chest like dropped ballast. She'd conjured this reunion a thousand different ways during the months he'd been missing. None of those imagined scenes had included breaking him out of a Union black site while klaxons wailed in the distance.

"Left," she said, steering him toward the service passage. "Harkin's waiting with the ship."

"Harkin?" Something flickered across Marcus's expression. "You're working with—"

"I'm working with whoever gets us out of here breathing."

They rounded the corner. The service passage yawned ahead, gray walls and exposed piping breeding shadows that prickled Nicole's skin. She drew the sidearm from her hip, keeping it low but ready.

Behind them, footsteps clattered in the main corridor. Voices barked out—sharp, commanding.

Marcus quickened despite the limp. Nicole matched his pace, her mind churning through distances and timing. Thirty meters to the junction. Another fifty to the docking bay. The patrol behind them would reach the cell in less than a minute, would trigger the alert, would seal every exit.

They wouldn't make it.

"Here," she said, yanking Marcus into a maintenance alcove.

The space barely contained both of them. She pressed against the wall, feeling the heat radiating from him in the cramped darkness. Her heart hammered against her ribs, so loud she was certain the approaching guards would hear it.

Two soldiers passed. Their boots struck the floor in synchronized rhythm, weapons held ready. The light from their helmets swept across the alcove entrance.

Nicole held her breath.

The soldiers continued past, their voices bleeding away as they moved toward the cell block.

"Go," Nicole whispered.

They emerged from the alcove and ran. The junction materialized ahead, marked by yellow striping on the walls. Nicole took the turn without slowing, Marcus a half-step behind her.

The docking bay access panel glowed amber. Nicole punched in the override code, her fingers slipping on the first attempt. She forced herself to slow down, to input each digit deliberately.

The panel flashed green.

The door opened onto chaos.

Harkin had the ship's boarding ramp down, engines already cycling to full power. But between Nicole and that ramp stood four guards in full combat gear, weapons trained on the ship.

"Down!" Nicole shouted, tackling Marcus sideways as plasma fire erupted across the docking bay.

They struck the deck hard. Pain jolted through Nicole's shoulder where she'd landed wrong. She rolled, bringing her sidearm up, and squeezed off two rounds. The first shot went wide. The second punched into a guard's chest, dropping him.

Marcus moved before she could stop him. He lunged for the fallen guard's weapon, scooped it up, came up firing. Two more guards collapsed.

Chapter Twelve

The Man Who Died

Marcus Heyn had forgotten what silence sounded like without the hum.

He lay on the examination table in the Meridian's medical bay, staring at ceiling panels still scarred with mounting brackets from when this space had stored cargo. Everything temporary. Everything makeshift. Like his freedom—something that might evaporate if he examined it too closely.

The hum's absence pressed against his eardrums with physical weight. For twenty-five years, the containment cell had thrummed at a frequency just below conscious perception, a mechanical purr that had scored the soundtrack to his slow deterioration. Now that constant companion had vanished, and every sound registered too sharply. Footsteps in the corridor made him flinch. Voices carried edges that set his teeth grinding.

"I don't know how to be this person," he said quietly, though no one was there to hear.

The tremor in his hands had worsened since the rescue—nerve damage from prolonged exposure to the Varroc containment fields. Another part of him that didn't work quite right anymore. He gripped the examination table's edge, feeling cold metal beneath his palms. Real. Solid. Not the cell.

The door chimed.

Marcus's chest constricted. He knew that pattern of hesitation, the particular weight of those footsteps. "Come in."

Nicole entered slowly, maintaining distance. She'd changed from the command jacket into simple crew utilities, as if shedding rank might make this easier. It didn't. The wariness in her stance remained—that careful approach someone might use with a wounded animal.

"They said you refused quarters," she said. "The medical bay isn't meant for long-term residence."

"The medical bay has open sight lines. No enclosed spaces." Marcus sat up slowly, bones protesting. "I can see all the exits."

Understanding flickered across Nicole's face. She drew over a chair but didn't sit, just stood with one hand resting on its back. "How are you feeling?"

"That's not why you came."

"No." She smiled slightly—sad and genuine. "I thought we might talk. Really talk, not just the careful conversation we managed earlier."

Marcus gestured at the empty space across from him. "Then sit. Please."

She did, settling into the chair with the controlled grace that command had taught her. They'd once been close enough that he could map the geography of her skin with his eyes closed, read her moods by the set of her shoulders. Now three meters of deck plating might as well have been an ocean.

The silence stretched between them. Marcus counted heartbeats—the damaged organ in his chest laboring through its rhythm. Sixty-eight beats before he found words.

"You got the Meridian," he said. "I'm glad. You always deserved a ship of your own."

"Marcus—"

"I need to understand something." The words came carefully, each one tested for weight. "When I disappeared. After. How long before…" He trailed off, unable to articulate the question properly.

Nicole's jaw tightened. "Before what?"

"Before you stopped waiting." The question emerged rawer than intended. "I'm not accusing you of anything. I just need to know what I came back to. Where I fit, if I fit at all."

Color drained from Nicole's face. She was quiet for a long moment, and Marcus watched emotions flicker across features he'd once known intimately. Pain. Anger. Something that might have been grief.

"Three years," she said finally. "I looked for three years."

Marcus absorbed this, turned it over in his mind. Three years. The number felt both significant and meaningless—a fraction of his captivity, but an eternity for someone searching without hope.

"The Admiralty declared you dead after eighteen months," Nicole continued. Her voice remained steady, but her hands clenched in her lap. "Standard protocol for missing personnel in hostile space. They wanted me to move on. Accept reality." She laughed—a sound without humor. "I couldn't. I burned through my savings following leads. Called in every favor. Destroyed my career prospects chasing rumors."

"But you stopped."

"I found something." Nicole leaned forward slightly, urgency sharpening her tone. "A smuggler operating near the Dead Sectors. He'd seen a Varroc facility, claimed they were holding prisoners. Humans among them. When I reported it to Naval Intelligence, they grounded me immediately."

Marcus felt his attention sharpen despite the medication dulling his anxiety. "They grounded you for finding evidence?"

"They said the information was unreliable. That acting on it would provoke the Varroc, potentially trigger a wider conflict." Her voice hardened. "When I pushed back, they threatened court martial. Told me if I pursued it, I'd be charged with insubordination and conduct unbecoming."

The pieces didn't fit properly. Marcus turned them over, searching for the logic. "If they thought the intelligence was false, why threaten court martial? That's excessive for chasing bad information."

"I know."

"And if they believed it was real—if they knew the Varroc were holding prisoners—why not act on it?"

Nicole's expression shifted into something Marcus recognized from their years together—that particular look she got when facing an uncomfortable truth. "I've had twenty-two years to think about that. The only explanation that makes sense is that they knew. They knew the Varroc had prisoners, possibly for years. And they decided the political cost of confrontation was too high."

The words settled over Marcus like a weight. The Admiralty had known. Had chosen to leave him—had chosen to leave all of them—in those cells rather than risk conflict. He'd been abandoned not through ignorance but through calculated decision.

"So you let it go," he said quietly.

"So I found another way." Nicole's voice carried steel now. "I took every shit assignment they offered. Rebuilt my reputation one patrol at a time. Made myself valuable enough that they couldn't dismiss me. And when they finally offered me a command, I refused the naval commission and went private instead."

Marcus looked at her fully then, seeing past his own damage to the woman before him. The lines at the corners of her eyes. The way command had reshaped her posture. Twenty-five years of determination carved into flesh and bone.

"The Meridian," he said. "You became a privateer so the Admiralty couldn't stop you."

"I became a privateer so I'd have the resources and independence to come back. To look for you again." She met his eyes. "It took two decades to position myself properly. To build a crew I could trust, establish routes into restricted space, develop the intelligence networks I needed. But I did it."

The admission hung between them, raw and undefended. Marcus felt something shift in his chest—not quite hope, but maybe the foundation that hope could build on.

"You want to know if I moved on?" Nicole continued. "Yes. I had relationships. I built a life. Because the alternative was climbing into a cell of my own making and rotting there." Her voice softened. "But I never stopped looking. Never stopped preparing for the moment I'd have another chance."

Marcus nodded slowly. His hands still trembled in his lap, but the panic had receded to manageable levels. "I'm sorry. I shouldn't have asked like that. Shouldn't have made it sound like an accusation."

"You're allowed to ask. You're allowed to feel everything you're feeling." Nicole shifted forward in her chair, closing the distance slightly. "But don't punish me for surviving."

"I'm not trying to punish anyone. I'm just trying to understand who I am now. Where I belong." He gestured vaguely at the air between them. "Everything feels displaced. Like I'm slightly to the left of where I'm supposed to be, and I can't figure out how to align myself."

Nicole's expression softened into something that might have been understanding. "Tell me about it. The containment. What they did to you."

Marcus felt his breath catch. The medication helped, but talking about the cell still triggered responses his damaged nervous system couldn't fully control. He forced air into his lungs, held it, released slowly.

"Nothing," he said. "That was the worst part. They did nothing."

He told her then—about waking in the cell with no memory of abduction, no transition between freedom and captivity. About the walls that hummed and the food that came through a port without human contact. About the window that showed other cells, other prisoners, and his desperate attempts to communicate with beings who never acknowledged his existence.

Nicole listened without interrupting. Her hand rested on the arm of her chair, close enough to reach for him if she wanted. She didn't. Maybe she understood that touch would send his heart rate spiking, trigger the panic that lived just beneath his chemically stabilized surface.

"I counted days by my beard growth," Marcus said. His voice had gone distant, pulled back toward memory. "Estimated the passage of time. But sometimes I'd wake up and my beard would be gone—they'd reset me somehow while I slept. Reset my body but not my mind. So I'd think weeks had passed, but then I'd have no physical proof."

"They were studying you," Nicole said quietly. "Human resilience. Psychology."

"I know." Marcus cut her off, not harshly but firmly. "I had twenty-five years to theorize about their motivations. But knowing why didn't make it easier. It just meant I understood that my suffering had purpose. That somewhere, something was learning from my deterioration."

The tremor in his hands intensified. He gripped the examination table harder, knuckles bleaching white. Nicole noticed but didn't comment, and he appreciated that small mercy.

"I thought I was strong enough at first," he continued. "I told myself stories, ran through memories, exercised to maintain fitness. But you can't stay strong indefinitely. Not alone. Eventually everything starts breaking down—body and mind together."

"When did it get bad?"

"Year eight, maybe. I started hallucinating." He forced himself to meet her eyes. "Conversations with people who weren't there. You, mostly."

Nicole's breath hitched, but she didn't look away.

"You were very understanding in my hallucinations," Marcus said. "Patient. You'd sit with me in the cell and we'd talk about getting out, about the life we'd have after rescue. I knew you weren't real, but I needed you anyway." He paused, gathering himself. "The worst part was when the hallucinations stopped. When my mind got too damaged to even conjure comfort anymore. That's when I really started dying."

The admission stripped him down to nerve endings. But Nicole deserved the truth. Deserved to understand what she'd pulled out of that cell.

"The Varroc kept me alive artificially," he continued. "Their technology prevented my body from shutting down completely. But I wanted to die. Spent years trying to will my heart to stop, my lungs to quit functioning. It never worked. I'd lose consciousness and wake up again, still trapped, still dying but unable to finish."

Nicole wiped at her eyes—not quite crying, but moisture gathered at the corners of her lashes. "We'll fix this. The damage. We'll find a way."

"There's no fixing this." Marcus heard the flatness in his own voice and hated it. "I can't touch another person without panicking. Can't sleep without waking in terror. Can't distinguish between what's real and what my damaged brain conjures. I'm functional enough to have this conversation, but that's about the extent of my recovery."

"You don't know that."

"I do." He stood slowly, joints creaking like an old man's. "I've been cataloging my symptoms. The gaps in memory. The way time still feels fluid and unreliable. I'm not the Marcus you knew. That person died in the cell years ago. I'm just what's left over."

Nicole stood as well, drawing herself to full height. Command presence radiated from her now—the officer who'd built a career from ruins, who'd spent two decades preparing for a moment that might never come.

"You're wrong," she said. "About what survived. About what's left."

"Nicole—"

"That person in the cell, the one who hallucinated me, who kept counting days, who refused to let his heart stop even when he wanted to?" She stepped closer, not touching but present. "That was strength. That was survival. You didn't die in there, Marcus. You endured."

He wanted to believe her. Wanted it so badly his damaged heart ached with it. But hope felt dangerous—another thing that could be taken away.

"I don't know how to do this," he admitted. "How to be free. How to be with people again. How to be with you again."

"Then we figure it out together." Nicole's voice held no pity, only determination. "One day at a time. One hour if that's what it takes. I didn't spend twenty-two years preparing for your rescue just to lose you now."

Marcus looked at her—really looked, past his own damage and fear. She was older, harder, shaped by years he hadn't witnessed. But underneath the command presence and the scars of her own survival, he could still see her. The woman he'd loved. The woman who'd never stopped searching.

"Okay," he said quietly. "One hour at a time."

Something in Nicole's expression cracked—relief, maybe, or the release of tension held too long. She didn't reach for him, didn't push for more than he could give. But she smiled, and for a moment, the medical bay felt less like a temporary refuge and more like a beginning.

The door chimed again. Jace's voice came through the intercom, sharp with urgency.

"Captain, we've got a problem. Long-range sensors just picked up three ships on intercept course. Compact military signatures." A pause. "They're running dark, but I recognize the configuration. Those are hunter-killer squadrons."

Nicole's expression hardened instantly, command presence snapping back into place. She turned toward the door, then paused, looking back at Marcus.

"Can you move?"

He tested his legs, found them steadier than expected. "I can move."

"Then stay close." She palmed the door open. "Because whoever sold us out just made their first move, and I intend to make them regret it."

Chapter Thirteen

Hunters

The transmission reached Commander Sielle Thane during her third watch review, and she understood before reading it that something had shifted in the careful architecture of her assignment.

She stood in the observation deck of the Meridian Lance, watching Bastion Station rotate against the bruised purple atmosphere of Kolthen-9. Below, the planet churned with storms that never died, electrical discharges sparking between cloud banks in silent pulses of light. Beautiful, in its way. Violent and predictable and contained.

Unlike this message now crystallizing on her hand terminal.

Marcus Heyn. Extraction successful. Subject departed Bastion Station 0347 hours ship time. Suspect facilitation by station personnel.

Thane read it twice, her expression unchanging. The muscles in her jaw worked once, then stilled. Around her, the observation deck hummed with the low activity of off-shift crew—engineers running diagnostics on the viewing array, a pair of junior tacticians reviewing fleet positioning near the window's far end. None of them noticed when she turned and walked to the privacy alcove set into the deck's rear wall.

She sealed the partition and opened the full report.

The details unfolded with clinical precision. Time stamps. Security footage analysis. Ghost signatures in the power grid that suggested deliberate interference with surveillance systems. The extraction revealed sophisticated planning and inside knowledge—whoever had orchestrated this possessed intimate access to Bastion's infrastructure.

Engineering-level access.

Thane pulled up Bastion Station's personnel roster, filtering for anyone with the technical capability and system permissions to execute this kind of operation. The list contracted to six names. Six senior engineering staff with the requisite clearances.

One name made something cold settle in her chest.

Nicole Lemarr. Acting Chief of Engineering. Bastion Station.

Thane stared at the name until the characters seemed to separate from their meaning, becoming abstract symbols on the screen. Then she closed her eyes and saw instead a different name, a different report, from four years ago.

Benjamin Lemarr. Deceased. Collateral damage during Syndicate enforcement action, Outpost Kellis-7.

She'd signed that report herself. Had wielded the clinical language that transformed a man's death into administrative necessity. Benjamin Lemarr had been part of a network supplying modified technology to insurgent cells—not weapons, but the infrastructure that made resistance possible. Communications arrays. Power systems. The enabling technology that kept organized opposition functioning.

The enforcement team had arrived expecting minimal resistance. They'd encountered instead a coordinated defensive network and evidence of intelligence sharing that had cost three of Thane's people their lives before they'd breached the outpost's inner ring.

Benjamin Lemarr had died during the final breach. Not armed, but not innocent either. He'd been trying to wipe data cores when the charges blew, and the structural collapse had crushed him along with two of his associates.

The official inquiry had cleared Thane's team. Justified force. Proper protocols followed. The file had been sealed and archived, another data point in the Syndicate's endless catalog of operational outcomes.

She'd never known Benjamin Lemarr had a daughter.

"Commander?" The voice came from outside the partition—Lieutenant Commander Voss, her executive officer. "We have a situation developing in Sector Seven. Regional command is requesting your assessment."

Thane opened her eyes. The operational rhythm of the ship continued regardless of what she'd just discovered. Duty compounded on duty, each moment demanding its measure of attention and decision.

"Conference room," she said, her voice carrying clearly through the partition. "Five minutes. Pull the senior staff."

She heard Voss acknowledge and move off, his footsteps receding across the observation deck. Thane looked down at her terminal, at Nicole Lemarr's personnel record still glowing on the screen.

Five minutes before she had to compartmentalize this and focus on other operational priorities. She used them.

Nicole's file showed a career trajectory that was exemplary without being exceptional. Solid performance reviews. Steady advancement through engineering ranks. No disciplinary flags, no political complications. The kind of personnel record that whispered competence and reliability.

But the timeline told a different story. Nicole's transfer to Bastion Station had come through three months after Marcus Heyn's arrival in the containment facility. Not immediately—that would have been too obvious. But soon enough to suggest intent behind the bureaucratic machinery.

Thane pulled up the transfer authorization chain. Standard processing. No irregularities. Which meant either it truly was coincidence, or Nicole had planned far enough ahead to make it appear routine.

The second option felt more likely. A woman who could orchestrate an extraction this sophisticated would have started with the foundation—getting into position, building the access, learning every system she'd need to compromise.

Four years of planning. Four years of grief calcifying into purpose.

Thane understood that trajectory better than she cared to admit.

She closed the file and made her way to the conference room. The senior staff was already assembling when she arrived—Voss at the head of the table, the intelligence officer Cortland reviewing something on her tablet, the ship's tactical coordinator pulling up displays on the projection array.

"What's the situation?" Thane asked, taking her position at the head of the table.

"Three independent traders reported Syndicate patrol ships conducting aggressive interdictions in Sector Seven," Voss said. "No official authorization on file. Regional command thinks it might be freelancers using stolen credentials."

"Or it's an authorized operation that regional command wasn't briefed on." Thane summoned the sector map. "What's in Seven that would justify a quiet operation?"

"Old mining infrastructure," Cortland said without looking up from her tablet. "Mostly abandoned, but some of the facilities still have functional systems. Good place to hide if you know how to keep a low profile."

Thane studied the map, her tactical mind overlaying what she knew about Marcus Heyn's extraction with the geography of possible escape routes. Bastion Station's position relative to Sector Seven. Transit times. Fuel consumption rates for a ship trying to run dark.

"How long ago were these interdictions reported?"

"First one came in six hours ago. Most recent was ninety minutes past."

Six hours. The same timeframe as Marcus Heyn's extraction from Bastion.

"Commander?" Voss was watching her with the careful attention of someone who'd learned to read her tactical assessments from minimal cues. "You think this connects to the Bastion situation?"

"Send a query to regional command," Thane said. "Ask if they've had any reports of unusual traffic or communications in Seven over the past twelve hours. Anything that doesn't fit normal patterns. But keep it general—don't mention Heyn or the extraction."

Voss nodded and began composing the message. Thane turned to Cortland.

"I need a deep analysis on Nicole Lemarr. Personal history, professional connections, anyone she might turn to for support. Start with her father—Benjamin Lemarr, killed during the Kellis-7 enforcement action four years ago. Find out what networks he was part of, who his associates were."

Cortland looked up, her expression sharpening with understanding. "You think the daughter is running his old networks?"

"I think she's had four years to plan, and planners don't start from zero. If her father had connections to people who could support a fugitive operation, she'd know about them."

"That's assuming she views her father's networks as reliable." Cortland made notes on her tablet. "If he got himself killed, she might consider his associates compromised or incompetent."

"Then find me her networks. University connections, professional associations, anyone she maintained contact with across multiple postings. Engineers talk to engineers. Someone out there knows where she might run."

Thane pulled up the extraction report again, reading through the tactical summary with fresh context. The precision of the operation suggested military or paramilitary training—more than just an engineering chief working alone.

"We're going to need additional resources for this," she said. "Contact Central Command. Tell them we have probable identification of the primary facilitator and request authorization for extended pursuit operations."

"You think this is going to take that long?" Voss asked.

"I think someone who spent four years getting into position isn't going to be easy to find." Thane met his eyes. "But we will find her. And when we do, she's going to answer for every decision that led to this moment."

The statement hung in the air, carrying weight beyond simple operational necessity. Voss glanced at Cortland, some silent communication passing between them.

"Commander," Cortland said carefully, "are you planning to lead this pursuit personally?"

"Central Command will make that determination. But yes, if they ask, I'm volunteering."

"Because of the Kellis-7 connection?"

"Because I commanded that operation and I know exactly what Benjamin Lemarr was part of. If his daughter is activating those networks, I can anticipate the patterns better than someone coming in cold."

It was true, as far as it went. But Thane could see in their expressions that they understood the unspoken dimension—the personal element that complicated clean operational assessment.

"I'm not compromised," she said, making it explicit. "I'm informed. There's a difference."

"Understood, Commander." Voss turned back to his terminal. "I'll compose the request to Central Command."

The meeting continued, spiraling into the detailed logistics of coordinating a multi-sector search operation. But part of Thane's attention remained fixed on Nicole Lemarr's file, on the shape of a vendetta four years in the making.

She thought about Benjamin Lemarr in those final moments at Kellis-7. Had he known his death would lead here? Had he understood that the networks he'd built, the resistance infrastructure he'd enabled, would pull his daughter into a conflict she couldn't win?

Probably not. People rarely saw that far forward. They made choices based on immediate circumstances, on what seemed right or necessary in the moment. Only later, when the consequences compounded beyond control, did the full shape of their decisions become clear.

Thane had learned to think further ahead. To see past the immediate satisfaction of any single choice and calculate its trajectory through time. It was why she'd risen through ranks, why Central Command would likely approve her request to lead this pursuit.

It was why she'd win.

The authorization came through within two hours. Thane read it in her private quarters, the text glowing on her terminal with the weight of official sanction behind every word.

Commander Thane. You are hereby assigned primary pursuit of escaped asset Marcus Heyn and affiliated personnel. Resources have been allocated for support operations. Your authority supersedes all regional commanders for the duration of this operation. Retrieve the asset intact. Eliminate or capture all facilitators. Report directly to Central Command.

She read it twice, noting what was said and what was carefully omitted. Resources allocated, but no specification of which resources. Support operations authorized, but no details on scope or scale. The phrasing gave her wide latitude while maintaining plausible deniability for Central Command.

They wanted results, but they wanted clean separation if those results got messy.

Thane understood the game. She'd played it long enough to know that authorization letters were both weapons and shields—useful until they weren't, then disavowed with bureaucratic efficiency.

She opened a secondary channel and composed a message to a contact she hadn't used in eighteen months. The response came faster than expected.

Commander Thane. Always a pleasure. What do you need?

Obsidian Solutions. The Syndicate's deniable assets—mercenaries who operated in the spaces where official forces couldn't be seen. Expensive, brutal, and effective. Exactly what this operation required.

Target package attached. Female, late thirties, engineering background. Traveling with escaped asset Marcus Heyn. Last known trajectory suggests movement toward Sector Seven. I want them found.

Standard recovery rates. Asset alive. Facilitator status negotiable.

Negotiable. A word that meant different things to different people. To Obsidian Solutions, it meant they could exercise discretion—bring Nicole Lemarr in breathing or not, depending on circumstances.

Thane should have felt something at that. Some echo of the conscience she'd once possessed, back when she'd believed the Syndicate's enforcement actions served justice rather than expedience. But that woman had died somewhere between Kellis-7 and now, replaced by something harder and more efficient.

Accepted. We'll have eyes on Sector Seven within six hours. Updates to follow.

Chapter Fourteen

Reconnection

Like warning.

The artifact's pulse shifted, deepening into something that felt less like light and more like a heartbeat. And somewhere in Nicole's mind, a door she hadn't known existed began to open.

Like hunger.

Chapter Sixteen

Trust what we built.

Chapter Seventeen

Ambush

Like something that had been waiting for her all along.

Chapter Eighteen

Wounds

They'd nearly died. All of them. Another three seconds and that torpedo would have punched straight through to the generator assembly, scattering them across dimensional space in pieces too small to identify.

"We don't have days."

"I know."

Marcus knelt beside the damaged coupling, forcing himself to focus on the technical problem instead of the memory of alarms shrieking and Nicole's voice cutting through the chaos with coordinates that might save them or might kill them faster. The metal felt warm under his fingers, almost feverish. He could see where the molecular bonds had separated under stress, creating a fracture line that spider-webbed through the entire housing.

"The resonance cascade," Marcus said slowly. "It's not the coupling itself that's the problem. It's the harmonic feedback loop between the coupling and the containment field."

"We don't need to recalibrate the containment field. We need to introduce a counter-harmonic into the coupling itself." The solution was taking shape in his mind, pushing aside the post-combat tremors. "The crystalline nodes from the sensor array—the ones Yuri replaced last month. They're calibrated to detect resonance patterns across multiple frequencies. If we reorient their quantum matrices and wire them into the coupling's control system, they should be able to read the feedback patterns in real-time and modulate the coupling's output to compensate."

"You sound very certain for someone who's never worked on a hyperspace generator before."

Marcus met his eyes. "I've worked on plenty of resonance systems. The principles are the same."

They worked mostly in silence at first, the kind of focused quiet that came from exhaustion and technical necessity. Marcus's hands had finally stopped shaking by the time he returned with the crystalline nodes from storage. "This is delicate work," Devon said. "If we damage the matrix while installing these, the whole coupling becomes useless."

"Then we don't damage it."

Devon handed him a micro-soldering tool, and they positioned themselves on opposite sides of the coupling. Marcus lay on his back on the cold deck plating, the control matrix hanging above him like a mechanical constellation. He selected the first node and began working, his hands steady despite the ache in his ribs.

The soldering required absolute precision, but Marcus found himself grateful for it. Better to focus on heat bleeding from the tool and the acrid smell of fusing metal than on how close they'd come to dying. Better to concentrate on the tiny click as each connection locked into place than on the sound of that torpedo detonating against their shields.

They worked like that for twenty minutes before Devon broke the silence.

"Where did you learn to work like this?"

Marcus positioned the second node, checking the alignment before beginning the connection sequence. "Academic research facilities. Lots of experimental equipment that needed custom modifications."

"What kind of research?"

"Xenobiology, mostly. Studying how non-terrestrial organisms adapted to different environmental conditions." Marcus completed the connection, testing it before moving to the next position. "It was the kind of work where every discovery opened ten new questions."

"Is that what you were doing at the Frontier Science Collective?"

The question came carefully, like Devon had been working up to it. Marcus kept his hands moving, starting on the third node. The physical work made it easier somehow. Gave him something to do with the nervous energy still thrumming through his system.

"Yes," Marcus said finally. "Nicole was there too."

Devon made a small sound of acknowledgment. They worked in silence for another few minutes, completing the third node installation.

Marcus slid out from beneath the coupling, sitting up slowly. His ribs protested the movement. They were past the point of simple technical collaboration now, had crossed some invisible line into actual conversation. Maybe it was the post-combat vulnerability, the way surviving something together stripped away normal social barriers. Or maybe Marcus was just tired of carrying everything alone.

"We had a complicated working relationship," he said. "There were disagreements about research protocols. About how far to push certain experiments." He paused, then made himself say it. "About what constituted acceptable risk in the pursuit of knowledge."

"She pushed too far?"

"I did."

The admission came easier than he'd expected. Devon listened without judgment as Marcus explained the project, the alien flora, the escalating experimental protocols. Described his impatience, Nicole's concerns, his decision to push forward anyway. Told him about the catastrophic energy discharge, the injuries, the investigation that had ended his research career.

"And you blamed her for what happened to your career."

"For a while, yes." Marcus leaned back against the generator housing. "It was easier than accepting responsibility for my own choices. It took me a long time to understand that Nicole had done exactly what she should have done."

Devon was quiet for a moment, then returned to work on the upper quadrant of the coupling. "Thank you for telling me."

"I'm not sure why I did."

"Maybe because you're tired of carrying it alone." Devon secured another connection. "And maybe because you're starting to realize that Nicole isn't carrying it alone either. Whatever happened between you two, she's been living with it just as long as you have."

They completed the installation forty minutes later. Marcus tested each connection while Devon ran diagnostic sequences, verifying that the modified coupling would integrate properly with the hyperspace generator's control systems.

"Resonance readings are dropping," Devon announced. "The adaptive modulation is working. We're down to point-three percent variance from optimal parameters."

"Will that hold through a jump?"

"Only one way to find out." Devon shut down the diagnostic sequence, then stood, stretching muscles that had been held tense for too long. "Whatever else happened in your past, you know what you're doing with resonance systems."

Marcus gathered the tools, returning each to its proper case. The work had steadied him, given him back a sense of control. But Devon's earlier observation about Nicole kept circling through his mind.

Maybe she's afraid she will. Trust you, I mean. And afraid of what that might cost if she's wrong.

Elena appeared in the engineering bay doorway before he could follow that thought further. Her expression was tight with urgency. "Nicole wants everyone in the briefing room. Now."

* * *

The briefing room felt smaller than usual with all seven of them crowded inside. Nicole stood at the head of the table, a holo-display projecting star charts behind her. Marcus found a position against the wall, putting himself at the periphery rather than claiming a seat.

He could see the exhaustion in her posture, the way she held herself rigidly upright through what had to be pure willpower. She'd been on the bridge through the entire attack, making split-second decisions that had kept them alive. Now she was here, still in command, still holding it together while everyone else got to fall apart in private.

"Damage assessment," Nicole began without preamble. "Engineering?"

Devon stepped forward. "Hyperspace generator is functional. We installed an adaptive fix for the coupling resonance issue. It should hold through at least one jump, possibly more. But we need proper repair facilities eventually."

"Medical?"

"Three minor injuries," Zara reported. "All treated. No one's compromised for duty."

"Weapons?"

Yuri's jaw tightened. "We've got maybe two more volleys before the plasma cannon overheats beyond safety margins. The missile racks are empty. Point defense is at forty percent capacity. If we get into another engagement, we're basically flying a target."

Nicole's eyes flicked to him, and for a moment something passed between them—the echo of a hundred briefings just like this one, back when they'd worked together at the Collective. Back when finishing each other's sentences had been automatic, comfortable, right.

The crew absorbed this in silence. Marcus watched their faces—exhaustion, determination, the careful calculation of risks and alternatives. These people had followed Nicole into a firefight and come out alive. They'd follow her into a nebula too.

"Any questions?" Nicole asked.

No one spoke.

"Then get some rest. We jump in two hours." She killed the display. "Dismissed."

The crew filed out, murmuring to each other in low voices. Marcus lingered, waiting until the room emptied.

"You should rest too," Marcus said quietly.

"I'll rest when we're safe."

"We're never going to be safe. Not anymore." He moved closer, stopping just outside arm's reach. "Nicole. What you did during the attack—the maneuvers, the timing—that was exceptional piloting. You saved us."

She turned to face him. Up close, he could see the cost written in the lines around her eyes, the pallor beneath her skin. "I did my job."

The silence stretched between them, heavy with everything they'd shared and everything they'd lost. Nicole's hand moved, almost reaching for him, then fell back to her side.

"Get some sleep, Marcus," she said softly. "We'll talk more when we're on the other side of the nebula."

He nodded and turned toward the door. He was almost through it when her voice stopped him.

"Marcus."

He looked back.

"Thank you. For the repair work. For..." She paused, searching for words. "For being honest. Finally."

"Forty hours," he said. "We'll make it."

He left her standing in the empty briefing room, silhouetted against the star charts that showed their path into darkness.

Behind him, the ship hummed with the sound of systems preparing for a jump that would take them into the unknown. Ahead, the Kessler Formation waited—dense and dangerous and full of places to hide.

And somewhere in the void, Obsidian Solutions was hunting.

Chapter Nineteen

Veridia

Nicole watched the viewport as the Avalon limped toward Veridia Station, its hull plates scorched black along the starboard quarter where the attack had nearly breached their main reactor housing. The station hung against the bruised purple nebula like a weathered monument to desperation, its docking arms extended in what might have been welcome or simply the mechanical indifference of automated systems.

She'd been awake for thirty-six hours straight, running damage assessments and rerouting power through systems never designed to compensate for the cascade failures they'd suffered. Her hands trembled slightly as she verified the approach vectors one final time. Not from fear—she'd burned through fear somewhere around hour twenty—but from the particular exhaustion that came from holding a dying ship together through sheer force of will.

"Station control is responding," Marcus said from the communications console. His voice carried an edge she hadn't heard before, something between wonder and dread. "They're clearing bay seven for emergency docking."

Nicole glanced at him. He sat rigid in his chair, eyes fixed on the station as if it might vanish should he look away. This was his first time seeing the Dead Sectors, she realized. The first time understanding what existed beyond the mapped territories, in the places where official star charts simply ended with notation warnings about navigation hazards and disputed boundaries.

"Bay seven," she repeated, adjusting their approach angle. "That's deep in the commercial district. Viktor's influence, probably."

Elena looked up from the engineering station where she'd been monitoring their failing coolant systems. "You trust him?"

"I trust that he wants something from us badly enough to keep us alive." Nicole brought the Avalon around slowly, fighting the sluggish response from their damaged maneuvering thrusters. "Beyond that, I don't trust Viktor Crane with anything more valuable than yesterday's waste reclamation."

The station swelled in the viewport, resolving into distinct sections—the central hub with its rotating habitat rings, the industrial sectors bristling with repair docks and fabrication facilities, and the outer rings where the real business of Veridia happened in the shadows between functioning lights and failing life support systems. Veridia wasn't dying so much as it had achieved a state of permanent half-life, sustained by the commerce that flowed through the Dead Sectors like blood through damaged vessels.

Nicole had been born here. Had spent the first sixteen years of her life learning to read people the way others learned to read star charts, understanding that in places like Veridia, everyone wanted something and the only question was whether you'd see the knife coming before it found your back.

The docking sequence engaged with a series of mechanical clangs that shuddered through the Avalon's frame. Nicole felt the ship tremble as the magnetic clamps locked onto their hull, as the pressurized tunnel extended to mate with their primary airlock. She ran through the shutdown procedures with practiced efficiency, isolating damaged systems and putting the ship into a maintenance-ready state that would allow repair crews access to the critical areas.

"We're secure," she announced, though the words felt premature. They wouldn't be secure until they were away from Veridia, and even then the term would be relative.

Thomas appeared in the hatchway leading from the crew quarters, his expression carefully neutral in a way that meant he'd already assessed their situation and found it wanting. "Viktor's people are waiting at the airlock. Six of them."

"Armed?" Nicole asked.

"Politely armed. Weapons visible but holstered." Thomas moved to the viewport, studying the station with the methodical attention he gave everything. "He's making a show of protection rather than force."

Nicole nodded. That was Viktor's style—the appearance of courtesy masking calculations that ran deep enough to drown in. She'd watched him operate during her years on Veridia, had seen him build his network through a combination of genuine service and carefully maintained leverage. He helped people, and then he owned them. The question was always whether the help was worth the price.

The airlock cycled open onto controlled chaos. Bay seven hummed with activity—repair crews moving between ships in various states of distress, cargo handlers operating loaders that looked like they'd been salvaged from three different vessels and welded together through hope and desperation. The air tasted of recycled oxygen and industrial solvents, familiar in a way that made Nicole's chest tighten with memories she'd spent years trying to bury.

Viktor Crane waited at the base of the docking ramp, flanked by his six security personnel. He looked exactly as Nicole remembered—tall and spare, with silver hair pulled back in a neat queue and eyes that seemed to catalog everything without ever quite focusing on anything specific. He wore civilian clothes that probably cost more than most ships docked here, but he wore them like work clothes, comfortable and unconcerned with their value.

"Nicole Voss," he said, and his voice carried genuine warmth that made her immediately suspicious. "It's been too long."

"Four years," Nicole replied, descending the ramp with Thomas and Marcus behind her. "I thought I'd made my position clear."

"You did. And I respected it." Viktor gestured toward the bay's exit, an invitation rather than a demand. "But circumstances change, and you reached out. I'm simply responding to a friend in need."

"We're not friends, Viktor. We're people with aligned interests for the next seventy-two hours."

He smiled, acknowledging the distinction with a slight incline of his head. "Then let me be helpful to someone with aligned interests. Your ship needs significant work—hull repairs, reactor recalibration, and I noticed your sensor array took damage that's affecting your long-range capabilities. I've already assigned my best crews."

Nicole studied him, searching for the angle. Viktor didn't deploy his best anything without expecting substantial return. "What do you want?"

"To talk. Just talk." He started toward the bay exit, clearly expecting them to follow. "You've been to Bastion. You've seen things that most people in the mapped territories would dismiss as ghost stories. I'm interested in information, nothing more."

"Information is never nothing," Nicole said, but she fell into step beside him. Thomas moved slightly ahead, maintaining the protective positioning so automatic she doubted he even thought about it anymore. Marcus stayed close on her other side, his eyes wide as he drank in the bay's activity.

They passed a ship that had clearly been in a fight—its hull torn open along the port side, revealing interior spaces burned black by weapons fire. Crew members worked to cut away the damaged sections, their welding torches throwing sparks that fell in slow cascades in the station's slightly lower gravity. Beyond it, another vessel was having its reactor housing completely replaced, the old unit suspended from lifter cables while technicians prepared to install its replacement.

"How many ships come through here?" Marcus asked quietly.

Viktor glanced at him with interest. "You're new to the Sectors. I can always tell—there's a look people get when they first realize how much exists beyond the official boundaries." He gestured expansively at the bay. "We process between thirty and fifty vessels per rotation, not counting the permanent residents and the transients who dock for less than six hours. Veridia serves as one of the primary hubs for this entire region."

"Primary hubs for what?" Marcus pressed.

"For survival," Viktor said simply. "The Dead Sectors aren't actually dead, despite what your government's charts suggest. They're simply unmapped, unclaimed, and therefore ungoverned. That makes them dangerous, yes, but also free in ways that the mapped territories haven't been for generations."

They entered a lift that rose through the station's interior, the transparent walls offering a view of Veridia's full scope. Nicole watched Marcus's expression shift as he saw it—the vast rotating habitat rings, the industrial sections extending like branches from the central trunk, the smaller modules and improvised structures that had been added over decades until the station resembled something organic rather than engineered.

"There are thousands of people here," Marcus said.

"Sixty thousand at last census," Viktor replied. "Though census is a generous term. Many prefer not to be counted." The lift stopped at what Nicole recognized as the commercial district's upper level, where Viktor maintained his primary offices. "We serve a purpose that the mapped territories either can't or won't—we provide a place for people who don't fit within their boundaries."

They walked through corridors that showed better maintenance than the docking bays, past businesses that ranged from legitimate trading firms to establishments whose purposes were deliberately ambiguous. Nicole noticed security cameras at regular intervals, some obvious and others concealed but visible if you knew what to look for. Viktor controlled his territory with a light touch, but he controlled it absolutely.

His office occupied a corner section with viewports overlooking both the station's interior and the nebula beyond. The space was furnished simply—a desk, several chairs, equipment for communication and data analysis. No decoration beyond function, which told Nicole more about Viktor's actual priorities than any amount of expensive art would have.

"Please, sit," Viktor said, moving to a cabinet and producing glasses and a bottle of something amber. "You look like you could use this."

Nicole accepted the glass but didn't drink. "You said you wanted to talk about Bastion."

"I want to talk about what's happening in the deeper Sectors," Viktor corrected, pouring drinks for Thomas and Marcus as well. "Bastion is simply the most dramatic symptom of a larger pattern. Ships have been disappearing at three times the normal rate over the past eighteen months. Not just small vessels or independent operators—convoy escorts, commercial haulers, even a few military picket ships that officially don't exist in territories that officially aren't claimed."

He settled behind his desk, his expression serious now, the facade of casual friendliness dropping away to reveal genuine concern. "Something is active out there. Something organized and purposeful. The Bastion incident suggests it's escalating beyond simple piracy or territorial disputes."

Nicole took a careful sip of the drink. It burned pleasantly, some kind of local distillation that probably violated several dozen import regulations. "What do you think it is?"

"I don't think," Viktor said. "I accumulate data and draw conclusions based on probability. The data suggests coordinated action by an entity with significant resources and technological capability. The probability suggests this entity is based somewhere in the deeper Dead Sectors, beyond normal patrol ranges and outside the territory that even independent operators usually risk."

Thomas leaned forward slightly. "You're describing a military operation."

"I'm describing something that displays military characteristics," Viktor agreed.

Chapter Twenty

The Directorate

The summons arrived during the third watch. Thane had been reviewing surveillance feeds from Veridia Station's outer perimeter when his terminal flashed amber three times—priority encryption that meant Director Frost herself wanted immediate contact.

He dismissed the feeds and accepted the transmission. The holo-emitter above his desk shimmered, then coalesced into Frost's face, rendered in cold blue light that leached what little color she possessed.

"Commander Corvus," she said without preamble. "Your quarry has docked."

"I'm aware." Thane settled back in his chair, studying the minute tells in her expression that most people missed. The faint tension bracketing her eyes. The way she held her jaw a fraction too rigid. "The Avalon limped in damaged. They won't be departing for at least forty-eight hours."

"Then you have a narrow window." Frost's image stuttered as interference rippled through the quantum relay. "The situation has evolved. We need Marcus Chen alive and cooperative."

Something cold crystallized in Thane's chest. "That wasn't the original mandate."

"The weapon system he activated in Sector Seven changes everything. If Chen can interface with other caches…" She trailed off, but Thane understood. A functional Predecessor weapons platform after millennia of dormancy wasn't just an archaeological curiosity—it was a strategic revolution. "You'll bring him in. Undamaged. Make whatever arrangements necessary."

"And Nicole Venn?"

The question hung in the blue light between them. Frost's pause lasted exactly two seconds—long enough for Thane to know the decision had already calcified.

"Dr. Venn has become a complication. Chen has demonstrated interface capability. She has not." Frost's distinction carried the weight of a death sentence wrapped in tactical assessment. "He'll prove more manageable without her influence."

"He won't cooperate if I kill her."

"He will if he believes we hold others he cares about." Frost's smile was thin as wire. "Your brother's recovery is progressing well, I'm told. The medical staff remain optimistic."

The transmission severed before Thane could respond.

He sat in the sudden darkness of his cabin, listening to the subtle thrum of the ship's systems around him. The Silence Runner hung two thousand kilometers off Veridia Station's main traffic approaches, masked in the thermal shadow of a derelict mining hauler.

Thane recognized the manipulation for what it was. Not a threat—Frost was too sophisticated for that—just a gentle reminder that Kaelen's continued existence depended on cooperation.

The old burn scar on his shoulder began to ache. His brother's face flickered through his thoughts—younger, unscarred, laughing at something during their last shore leave together. Before the operation that had obliterated everything. Before Frost's order to eliminate the infection vector, when "infection vector" had meant putting a bullet through Kaelen's chest.

Thane had squeezed the trigger. But in that fraction of a second, his hand had shifted. Not enough for Frost to notice through the tactical feed, not enough for anyone to prove it had been anything but combat chaos affecting his aim.

Just enough that the shot had torn through Kaelen's shoulder instead of his heart.

His brother had gone down screaming, and Thane had called for immediate medical extraction. The decontamination teams had reached them in time—barely. Now Kaelen existed in a medical facility that appeared on no official records, receiving care that cost enough to bankrupt a minor colony.

Care that Frost personally authorized and funded.

Care that transformed Kaelen's existence into leverage over everything Thane did.

He forced the memories down and summoned the surveillance data on Marcus Chen and Nicole Venn instead.

The Avalon's damage ran deeper than the station authorities realized. Thane's combat engineering background let him read the hull scorching patterns, the emergency patches already showing stress fractures. Someone wanted them dead, and the attack pattern matched Syndicate hunter-killer tactics. But the response time was wrong—they shouldn't have known where the Avalon was heading.

Chen and Venn had sheltered on the ship for the first six hours after docking. Then Chen had emerged alone, moving carefully through the commerce districts. Not expert tradecraft, but competent enough to shake casual observation.

Nicole Venn departed the ship forty minutes later, heading to the station's medical quarter. She spent thirty-one minutes acquiring trauma stabilization units and life support filters. Practical purchases for a damaged ship.

But the way she moved through the corridors carried a tension that Thane recognized. Venn wasn't just shopping. She was assessing. Her eyes tracked exit routes, personnel concentrations, security camera positions.

That was going to complicate things.

Thane pulled up the station's schematic and began marking potential intercept points. Veridia Station had started as a corporate mining hub, been abandoned when the deposits proved less valuable than projected, then repopulated by pirates, refugees, and independent traders. Now it functioned as a neutral haven by default, protected mainly by the fact that no major power wanted to spend resources controlling something so economically marginal.

Which made it perfect for people trying to disappear.

And perfect for people trying to make other people disappear.

His terminal chimed with an incoming message from one of his assets on the station. Chen had made contact with someone in the lower commerce ring. The surveillance drone had captured clear facial recognition on Chen's contact.

The image made Thane pause.

Damien Cross. Former Directorate tactical officer, discharged under circumstances that even Thane's clearance only partially explained. Cross had been part of a deep reconnaissance unit that went dark for three months in the border sectors, then returned with half its personnel missing. The subsequent investigation had been classified at a level that meant Frost herself had been involved.

And now Cross was here, talking to Marcus Chen.

Thane sent a priority query to the intelligence database. The response flagged Cross as a person of interest, suspected of information brokering and possible Syndicate contacts, but no active warrants. Status: observe but do not engage without authorization.

Thane added Cross to his operational map and continued planning.

* * *

The problem with killing Nicole Venn on Veridia Station was that she'd made herself difficult to isolate. After returning from the medical quarter, she'd remained on the Avalon, and the ship's docking berth offered limited approach vectors that didn't involve multiple security checkpoints and civilian witnesses.

Thane could arrange an accident. Systems failed all the time on stations as decrepit as Veridia. But accidents took time to orchestrate, and Frost wanted results on a narrow timetable.

Which meant getting more direct.

He accessed the secure channel to his support team on the station and sent coded instructions: acquire forged credentials, identify Venn's movement patterns, prepare a medical emergency scenario that could justify quarantine protocols.

The responses came back confirming receipt. No questions, no hesitation.

He was finalizing the timeline when his proximity alert triggered.

A small courier vessel had detached from the main station cluster and was executing a trajectory that would bring it within five hundred meters of the derelict hauler Thane was using for cover.

The approach vector was too precise. The course corrections too deliberate.

Someone knew he was here.

Thane initialized the Silence Runner's combat protocols while keeping them powered down enough to avoid active sensor detection. His fingers moved across the tactical display, plotting firing solutions and escape vectors simultaneously.

The courier vessel slowed to station-keeping at four hundred eighty meters and held position.

No communication attempts. No scan sweeps. Just silent presence.

After two minutes, the courier vessel's running lights flickered in a sequence of old Directorate tactical code, deprecated fifteen years ago. The pattern spelled out a simple message.

We should talk.

Thane opened a tight-beam communication channel. "Identify yourself."

The response came in a voice he didn't recognize—female, middle-aged, accent that suggested core world education. "My name isn't important. What matters is that we both have an interest in Marcus Chen staying alive and out of Director Frost's hands."

"Then we don't have aligned interests." Thane kept his tone neutral while running a trace on the communication source. The signal bounced through so many relays that pinpointing its origin was impossible. Professional work.

"Don't we?" The woman sounded amused. "Tell me, Commander—how is your brother's recovery actually progressing? The real reports, not the sanitized versions she shows you."

Thane's finger tightened on the weapons release. "You have ten seconds to explain how you know about that."

"I know because I used to work in the same dark places you do now. I know because I saw what happened to the last operative who thought Frost would honor her commitments regarding family." The amusement drained from the woman's voice. "She'll keep your brother alive exactly as long as you're useful. The moment you complete this assignment or become a liability, Kaelen's life support will experience an unfortunate malfunction. You know I'm right."

Thane said nothing. His silence was answer enough.

"Marcus Chen discovered something that terrifies the Directorate more than they're admitting. Frost needs Chen compliant and isolated. That's why she wants Nicole Venn dead—not because Venn is useless, but because Venn asks questions that the Directorate doesn't want answered."

"And you want what? To rescue Chen out of altruism?"

"I want balance. The Directorate holding exclusive access to functional Predecessor weapons would destabilize everything. What I'm offering is a third option—help them escape, buy time for them to understand what they've found before any single faction can monopolize it."

Thane processed that while his tactical system continued trying to identify the courier vessel's origin. "You're talking about starting a war."

"We'll have conflicts either way. At least this way they'll be distributed, not a single power accumulating enough force to dominate everyone else." The woman paused. "I'm not asking you to betray the Directorate. I'm asking you to consider whether Frost's vision of the future is the one you want your brother to wake up into, if he ever fully recovers."

The courier vessel's engines began cycling up. "You have twelve hours to decide. If you move against them, I'll intervene. But if you stand down, if you give them time to leave Veridia safely, then I'll give you something in return."

"What?"

"Proof that Kaelen's condition isn't improving the way Frost claims. Proof that she's keeping him sedated enough to prevent recovery because she needs the leverage." The courier's engines reached full power. "Twelve hours, Commander. Choose carefully."

The communication severed. The courier vessel accelerated away, merging back into the traffic patterns before Thane could lock targeting solutions.

He sat in the darkness, listening to his own breathing.

* * *

His terminal chimed an hour later. Incoming surveillance update: Nicole Venn had departed the Avalon again, heading toward the lower commerce ring. The drone tracked her to a small cantina called the Rust Bucket.

Thane pulled up the cantina's layout. Two exits, multiple sight lines, enough civilian traffic to make a clean extraction difficult but not impossible. If he moved now, he could have operatives in position before Venn finished whatever business had brought her there.

His hand moved toward the communication controls.

Then stopped.

He thought about Kaelen's face. Not the broken version in the medical feeds, but the real one—his little brother laughing during shore leave, challenging him to drinking contests he always lost, talking about the future like it was something worth having.

Kaelen had believed in the Directorate once. They both had. They'd accepted the hard choices as necessary costs for the greater stability their work provided. They'd told themselves that the people they hunted, the secrets they buried, the lives they ended—all of it served a purpose larger than any individual.

Now Thane sat in the dark, holding his brother's life in one hand and Frost's orders in the other, and he couldn't remember what that purpose had been.

The woman on the courier vessel was right. He knew it with the same certainty he'd known when his aim had shifted a fraction of an inch, saving Kaelen's life and condemning them both to this slow-motion execution.

Frost would never let his brother go. Whether Thane succeeded or failed, whether he killed Nicole Venn or let her escape, the outcome remained the same. Kaelen existed only as a tool to ensure compliance. The moment that tool became unnecessary, it would be discarded.

Thane had known this for months. He'd simply refused to admit it.

His hand moved away from the communication controls.

Nicole Venn's tracker showed her still inside the Rust Bucket. Marcus Chen was somewhere in the lower commerce ring, meeting with Damien Cross. The Avalon sat damaged in its berth, vulnerable to a dozen different approaches.

Twelve hours. The woman had given him twelve hours.

Thane pulled up the station's departure schedules, cross-referencing them against the Avalon's estimated repair timeline. If Chen and Venn pushed hard, if they prioritized getting mobile over getting fully operational, they could be flight-ready in eight hours.

He could give them that. Could look the other way long enough for them to slip through the net. Could pretend his operatives had been outmaneuvered, his surveillance had failed, his timing had been off by critical minutes.

It would mean betraying everything he'd spent his career building. Every principle he'd claimed to serve.

But Kaelen was dying slowly in a facility that existed only to keep Thane compliant. And the woman on the courier vessel had promised proof.

Thane made his decision.

He opened a channel to his support team on the station. "Stand down. Maintain observation only. Do not approach the targets until further notice."

The acknowledgment came back confused but obedient.

Then he composed a message to an address he'd memorized years ago—a dead drop that connected to people who operated outside every official structure, people who might be able to extract a patient from a facility that didn't exist.

I'm listening, he wrote. Tell me what you need.

The response came faster than he expected, as if someone had been waiting.

Meet us at the following coordinates in six hours. Come alone. And Commander—welcome to the other side.

Thane deleted the message and stared at the empty screen.

Behind him, through the viewport, Veridia Station turned slowly against the stars—a waypoint between the life he'd known and whatever came next.

He'd spent twenty years hunting people who made the choice he'd just made.

Now he was one of them.

Chapter Twenty-One

Memories

The observation deck stood empty at this hour, the lights dimmed to their lowest setting. Nicole pressed her forehead against the reinforced glass and watched Bastion's outer defense grid pulse in the distance—thousands of automated platforms maintaining their eternal vigil against threats that hadn't materialized in decades.

She'd been standing here for two hours. Maybe three. Time had grown slippery since the transmission from the Markov Belt, since Marcus's voice had crackled through static and seventeen years of silence.

Her reflection stared back from the glass, ghost-pale and translucent. She looked like she hadn't slept in weeks. She probably hadn't, not properly. The dreams came every time she closed her eyes now—fragments of a life she'd spent nearly two decades trying to bury.

The door cycled behind her. She didn't turn.

"Ma'am, you have a briefing in forty minutes." Kavin's voice, carefully neutral. "Lieutenant Commander Oresh has been looking for you."

"Tell him I'm indisposed."

"I've already told him that twice."

Nicole closed her eyes. The darkness behind her eyelids offered no refuge. She could still see Marcus the way he'd looked that last morning on the Infinite Reach, sleep-rumpled and smiling, completely unaware that in six hours everything they'd built together would shatter into chaos and blood.

"I need a few more minutes," she said, softening her tone. "Please, Kavin."

His footsteps retreated. The door cycled again. Silence returned, but it brought no comfort.

Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the glass, feeling the faint vibration of Bastion's primary reactors thrumming through the superstructure. Solid. Real. Present.

Unlike everything else.

The observation deck lights brightened automatically as the station's chronometer ticked over to morning cycle. Nicole flinched at the sudden illumination, her eyes burning. She'd been standing here all night.

Her personal comm chimed. She pulled it from her pocket, saw Oresh's name, and silenced it without reading the message. He would be concerned. Frustrated. Possibly angry. She was behaving like someone coming apart at the seams, and in the military that kind of unraveling got you relieved of duty.

The defense platforms completed another rotation. Nicole watched them align, each one finding its assigned position with mechanical precision. If only human relationships could be so easily calculated and controlled.

The comm chimed again. This time she answered.

"Commander Oresh is concerned about your fitness for duty." Director Kress's voice emerged from the speaker. "I'm coming to the observation deck. Wait there."

The connection closed before Nicole could respond.

She turned away from the window, trying to summon the professional bearing that had become second nature over the past seventeen years. But exhaustion dragged at every movement. Her mind kept sliding backward, pulled by a gravitational force she couldn't resist.

Seventeen years ago. The first time she'd met Marcus Talin.

* * *

She was twenty-six, standing in the Infinite Reach's secondary cargo bay with a plasma cutter in her hands and grease smeared across her forehead. The transport frigate wasn't much to look at—a century-old Meridian-class vessel that had been decommissioned, sold, refitted, sold again, and finally purchased by a consortium of freight haulers who didn't ask questions about their crew's past mistakes.

Nicole had been working aboard for three months, keeping her head down and her service record buried under a false identity. The name on her crew manifest was Nicole Voss—her mother's maiden name, borrowed and slightly altered. The last thing she needed was for someone to connect her real name to the Galway incident. Never mind that the inquiry had cleared her of wrongdoing. Never mind that the court-martial had ended with full exoneration. In the merchant fleet, a military discharge of any kind marked you as suspect.

"You're doing that wrong."

She'd looked up to find a man watching her from the doorway—lean and dark-haired, with the kind of face that looked like it smiled often but wasn't smiling now. He wore the insignia of a junior navigator, though something about the way he carried himself suggested a background that didn't quite match the rank.

"Excuse me?"

"The plasma cutter. You're holding it at the wrong angle for a Meridian-class hull plate. The alloy composition is different from military standard. You'll burn through the secondary seal and compromise the environmental system for the entire cargo section."

Nicole bristled. "I've been cutting hull plates since before you learned which end of a ship was forward."

"Then you should know better."

They'd stared at each other for a long moment. Then he'd crossed the bay, taken the cutter from her hands with a gentleness that surprised her, and demonstrated the correct angle. His movements were precise, economical, the work of someone who'd spent years in actual shipyards rather than just reading technical manuals.

"You're military," she'd said when he finished. "Or you were."

"So are you."

It wasn't a question. Nicole had felt her chest tighten. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"Sure you don't." He'd handed back the cutter. "Your stance gives you away. And the way you carry yourself. And about fifty other tells that anyone with field experience would recognize in ten seconds."

"If you're planning to report me—"

"I'm not." He'd smiled then, and it transformed his entire face. "I'm Marcus Talin. I left the service two years ago under circumstances I don't discuss. You're Nicole Voss, though I'm guessing that's not your real name. You left under circumstances you don't discuss. We're both here because we don't fit anywhere else. Seems like a good basis for friendship."

She should have walked away. Should have maintained distance, kept her secrets locked down, refused to let anyone close enough to hurt her again. But there had been something in his eyes—a recognition of shared wounds, of survival against odds that should have broken them both.

"Nicole Reyez," she'd said finally, offering her actual surname. "And I don't need friends."

"Neither do I. Let's get a drink anyway."

* * *

The observation deck door cycled. Nicole turned to find Director Kress entering the compartment, moving with her usual measured grace, her uniform immaculate despite the early hour. Her eyes found Nicole immediately, cataloging the rumpled clothes and the dark circles and the general appearance of someone coming apart.

"Commander Oresh requested that I intervene," Kress said without preamble. "He believes you're not fit for duty."

Nicole straightened. "I apologize, Director. I've been dealing with a personal matter. It won't interfere with my responsibilities."

"It already has." Kress crossed to the window, standing close enough that Nicole could smell her perfume—something subtle and expensive, like everything about the Director. "You've been an asset to Bastion since the day we recovered you from that escape pod. Your dedication has been exemplary. But everyone has limits, Nicole. Even you."

"I'm fine."

"You're grieving." Kress's voice was surprisingly gentle. "I've read the transmission from the Markov Belt. The one addressed to you specifically. I know who Marcus Talin was to you."

Nicole felt her carefully constructed composure crack. "How—"

"I've had access to your full service record from the beginning. Including the parts you tried to erase." Kress turned to face her. "I know about the Infinite Reach. I know about your relationship with Talin. And I know that you've spent seventeen years believing he was dead."

The observation deck seemed to tilt. Nicole reached out, bracing herself against the glass. "You knew. All this time, you knew, and you never—"

"I never mentioned it because it wasn't relevant. You'd survived a traumatic incident and were rebuilding your life. What purpose would it have served to force you to relive that trauma?" Kress's expression remained unreadable. "But now circumstances have changed. The past has returned. And I need to know if it's compromised your ability to serve."

"It hasn't."

"Don't lie to me, Nicole. You're standing in an observation deck at dawn after spending the entire night here alone. You've missed multiple briefings and ignored direct communications from your command staff. And according to station medical records, you haven't slept more than three hours in the past week." Kress moved closer. "Those are not the behaviors of someone who's fine."

Nicole closed her eyes. The exhaustion was suddenly overwhelming, a weight pressing down on every part of her consciousness. "I don't know what to do. He's asking me to come find him. To see what they discovered out there. And part of me—" Her voice broke. "Part of me wants to go. To hear his explanation. To understand why he did what he did."

"And the other part?"

"The other part knows that even if he tells the truth, even if there was a valid reason for the mutiny, it doesn't change the fact that he betrayed me. Questions

Three hours.

What kind of person.

Chapter Twenty-Five

Why

But so was the man who authorized operations in facilities that tortured children." She turned to face Daven. "How do I hold both versions in my head at the same time?"

Daven was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice carried a weight she rarely heard from him. "My uncle was a good man. Funny, kind, always had time for family. He also beat my aunt so badly she lost hearing in one ear." He met her eyes. "People contain contradictions. Your father could be both the man who loved you and the man who made terrible choices."

"That doesn't make it hurt less."

"No. But it might make it possible to eventually understand him. Not forgive—some things don't deserve forgiveness. But understand that people are capable of both great love and terrible cruelty."

Nicole felt tears finally break free, tracking hot lines down her cheeks. She didn't try to stop them. Daven stood and crossed to her, his hand finding her shoulder—a steady pressure that anchored her to the moment.

She let herself cry, silent tears that released some of the pressure building in her chest. Daven didn't offer platitudes or empty comfort, just his presence. The simple gift of not being alone.

Eventually the tears slowed. Nicole wiped her face with her sleeve, embarrassed but also somehow lighter.

"Thank you," she said. "For not letting me be alone with this."

"That's what first officers are for." He studied her face. "What about Marcus?"

Nicole turned back to the viewport. "I'm angry at him. But I also understand why he told me. He could have kept it hidden. Should have, probably. But he chose honesty instead." She drew a breath. "I need time before I can look at him without seeing my father's sins. But I also need him to know that I understand the difference between the boy who followed orders and the man who's trying to make amends."

"Do you want me to tell him that?"

"No. I will." The decision settled into her bones. "Just not tonight. Tonight I need to sit with this."

Daven nodded. "The crew's noticed something's wrong. Nothing specific, but the tension is obvious."

Nicole grimaced. "I'll call a briefing tomorrow. Not about this—this stays private. But we need to discuss the ruins anyway. Give everyone a clear objective."

"Good." Daven moved toward the door, then paused. "Marcus is tearing himself apart over this. Whatever you decide about him, he's going to assume the worst until you tell him otherwise."

"I know." Fresh guilt stabbed through her chest. "I'll talk to him soon. I just need a day."

After Daven left, Nicole retrieved a cleaning kit and spent several minutes gathering the broken holo-frame pieces. The display was ruined, but she kept the fragments anyway. She placed them in her bottom desk drawer, next to the physical letters her father had written during her first academy deployment.

Maybe someday she'd be able to read them without the bitter taste of betrayal. Maybe not.

* * *

Morning came too early. Nicole had managed perhaps three hours of fitful sleep, her dreams populated by shadowy laboratories and her father's face twisted into unfamiliar expressions. She woke feeling hollowed out, running on exhausted determination.

The briefing room felt crowded with the assembled department heads. Nicole stood at the head of the table, holographic display showing Veridia's primary ruin site. Daven sat to her right, datapad ready. Sera occupied the left, studying topographical data. Taryn claimed the far end, arms crossed. Marcus's seat remained empty.

"Where's our security chief?" Taryn asked.

"Following up on some personal research," Nicole said, keeping her voice neutral. "I've been briefed on his security recommendations."

It wasn't quite a lie. Marcus had sent her a detailed report at 0400 hours—clearly he wasn't sleeping either—outlining defensive postures for any ground expedition. The report was thorough, professional, completely devoid of personal content.

Daven gave her a sidelong glance but said nothing.

"Right." Taryn didn't look convinced. "So what's our play with these ruins?"

Nicole activated the next display layer. The ruin site expanded, revealing details their orbital scans had captured. "The structure extends at least three levels underground, possibly more. But this site shows signs of recent activity."

"Define recent," Sera said.

"Within the last standard year. We've detected trace residue from landing thrusters in this clearing." Nicole highlighted an area adjacent to the main complex. "Chemical signature matches Commonwealth military vessels, but the pattern is unusual. Someone made multiple visits."

"Drayton?" Daven suggested.

"Possibly. Or another faction." Nicole pulled up the next scan layer. "Here's where it gets interesting. Deep-penetration sonar shows structural modifications that don't match the original architecture. Someone has been building inside the ruins—adding rooms, reinforcing corridors, installing what looks like modern power infrastructure."

Sera leaned forward, studying the scan data. "That's significant resource investment. Whoever did this wasn't just visiting—they were establishing a base."

"Exactly." Nicole highlighted the power signatures. "And these energy readings suggest the installation is still active. Someone's down there right now, or was recently enough that the systems haven't gone dormant."

Taryn's expression shifted from skepticism to concern. "So we're not just exploring ancient ruins. We're potentially walking into an occupied facility."

"Which is why we're going in prepared." Nicole pulled up Marcus's security recommendations, grateful for the professional distance the document provided. "Four-person advance team, full tactical loadout. Reconnaissance only—no engagement unless absolutely necessary."

"Who's on the team?" Daven asked.

Nicole hesitated. The logical choice was obvious, but every part of her resisted saying it aloud. "Myself, Sera for technical analysis, Taryn for close-quarters capability, and—" She forced the words out. "And Marcus for security coordination."

The room went quiet. Everyone had noticed the tension between them; everyone was too professional to comment directly.

"Are you sure that's wise?" Daven asked carefully. "Given current... operational stress levels?"

"Marcus is the best tactical mind we have. Personal complications don't change that assessment." Nicole met Daven's eyes, willing him to understand. "We go in four hours. Use the time to prepare."

The department heads filed out, conversations resuming in low voices once they cleared the doorway. Nicole remained at the display, studying the ruin schematics without really seeing them.

Daven lingered. "You're pushing yourself."

"I'm doing my job."

"You're using your job to avoid dealing with what you're feeling." He moved closer, lowering his voice. "Going into a potentially hostile environment with someone you're struggling to trust—that's not professionalism. That's avoidance with tactical risks."

"Maybe." Nicole finally looked at him. "But it's also the fastest way to force myself through this. If I can work with Marcus under pressure, if I can trust him to watch my back when it matters—then maybe I can start trusting him with everything else."

"Or you could both get killed because your heads aren't in the mission."

"Then I guess we'd better make sure our heads are in the mission." Nicole deactivated the display. "I need to talk to him before we go down. Clear the air enough that it won't compromise us."

Daven nodded slowly. "And if you can't?"

Nicole thought about her father, about the contradictions Daven had described, about the way people could be both heroes and monsters depending on which angle you viewed them from.

"Then I learn to hold two truths at once," she said. "The man who hurt me, and the man I might still be able to trust. They're the same person. I just have to figure out how to see both."

She found Marcus in his quarters, staring at the same data reports she'd been avoiding. His face was haggard, marked by the same sleepless exhaustion she felt in her own bones.

"We go down in four hours," she said from the doorway. "I need to know if you can do this."

He looked up. The guilt in his expression nearly broke her resolve. "Nicole—"

"Not now. Later, we'll talk about everything. Right now, I need my security chief. Can you be that?"

A long pause. Then Marcus straightened, something shifting in his posture—the professional soldier emerging from beneath the weight of confession.

"Yes," he said. "I can be that."

"Good." Nicole turned to leave, then paused. "Marcus? The boy who followed orders seventeen years ago—I don't blame him. The man who told me the truth yesterday, even knowing how much it would hurt—I don't blame him either. But I need time to reconcile both of them with the person I thought I knew."

"I understand."

"I hope so." She stepped into the corridor, the door sliding shut between them.

Four hours. Then they'd descend into ruins that someone had already claimed, searching for answers that might destroy everything they thought they understood.

And somewhere beneath Veridia's ancient surface, something was waiting.

Chapter Twenty-Seven

Closing In

The interrogation chamber reeked of recycled fear and industrial solvent. Through the observation panel, Thane studied the trembling figure strapped to the chair—a man who'd survived three months in the Dead Sectors and bore every day of it in his flesh. His skin held the gray pallor of someone who'd breathed filtered air too long, eyes darting between her officers like a cornered animal hunting escape routes that didn't exist.

She'd been standing here nine hours. Her legs ached. Her eyes burned from the harsh overhead lighting. But rest meant closing her eyes, and closing her eyes meant seeing Marcus's face—not as it had been in those final moments, but as it should have been. Young. Idealistic. Alive.

Commander Voss worked the panel beside her, adjusting the audio feed until the prisoner's ragged breathing flooded the observation room. "Third contact we've pulled from this sector. Stories match the first two—scattered survivors, failed colonies, things in the dark that don't register on scanners."

"And the weapon?" The word tasted like ash in her mouth.

"He claims he's heard rumors. Nothing concrete." Voss glanced at her, his weathered face creased with concern. "Commander, the first two interrogators got nowhere. Maybe we're pushing too hard. Maybe he genuinely doesn't know anything useful."

Thane's jaw tightened. The first interrogator had wielded intimidation—standard pressure tactics that left the prisoner more terrified but no more forthcoming. The second had attempted rapport-building, spent three hours establishing trust only to strike the same walls. "Send in Lieutenant Marsh. Different approach—I want someone who can read between the lines. If he knows something but doesn't know he knows it, Marsh will excavate it."

Voss's silence spoke volumes, but he activated the comm panel and relayed the order. Through the observation window, the prisoner's head snapped up as the chamber door cycled open.

Thane had chosen Marsh specifically for this. The Lieutenant had spent two years in counterintelligence before transferring to her command—a patient interrogator who understood that information often hid in the spaces between words. Marsh entered with calm precision, back straight, movements economical, expression neutral. The prisoner's breathing accelerated.

Fear, Thane noted. But beneath it—he wants to talk. Needs to talk. He's been holding something for months and it's devouring him from within.

She'd witnessed it before. Survivors who carried knowledge like a weight, desperate to share the burden but terrified of the consequences. The key was making confession feel safer than silence.

Marsh settled into the chair across from him with deliberate ease. "State your designation. For the record."

The prisoner swallowed hard. "Designation?" His laugh held no humor. "We stopped using those after the sixth month. Names felt more… human. I'm Cade. Just Cade."

"Cade." Marsh leaned back slightly, a calculated gesture. "You were part of the Thornwood expedition?"

"Colony ship. Not expedition." Cade's hands clenched against the table. "We weren't exploring. We were supposed to be building something. A new start, away from the Core systems and their…" He trailed off, seemed to reconsider his words. "We wanted freedom."

Truth, Thane decided. Or at least, what he believes to be truth. How many idealists had she watched the Dead Sectors chew up? How many bright-eyed believers in new starts had learned the hard way that some spaces were dead for good reason?

"Tell me about the weapon," Marsh said.

Cade's expression shifted—fear giving way to something darker. Frustration, maybe. Or exhaustion with a question he'd already answered twice. "I told the others. It's just rumors. Stories people trade when the recyclers fail and you're sitting in the dark wondering if your oxygen will last another day."

"Stories come from somewhere." Marsh's tone remained neutral, but she'd shifted her posture—mirroring his lean, creating unconscious rapport. "What did you hear?"

"That something's waking up out here. Something old." Cade's voice dropped to barely above a whisper. "The sectors weren't always dead. Before the Coalition, before the expansion—there were others. We found their structures. Their… remnants."

Thane's pulse quickened. The briefing materials from Command had been sparse, carefully edited to excise classified details. But she'd read between the lines, recognized the patterns. Ancient installations. Power signatures that defied explanation. And Faren, somehow threaded through it all.

"What kind of remnants?" Marsh asked.

"Dead cities. Stations built into asteroids, technology we couldn't begin to understand." Cade's hands trembled now. "We thought we could salvage them. Use their materials, their power systems. But every team we sent came back wrong. Or didn't come back at all."

"Wrong how?"

The prisoner's eyes lost focus, staring at something beyond the chamber walls. "They'd forget things. Simple things at first—their quarters assignments, their shift schedules. Then bigger pieces. Names of crew members they'd worked with for years. Eventually, they forgot they were human at all."

Silence flooded the chamber. Thane felt ice crystallize along her spine. Memory loss. Progressive deterioration. Consistent with the psychological profiles, yes—but what if it's not psychological?

She thought of the scattered intelligence reports, the fragments Command had deemed too sensitive for standard distribution. Anomalous structures. Unexplained power signatures. Research teams that stopped reporting. All of it concentrated in these sectors, all of it brushed aside as equipment failure or stress-induced hallucination.

All of it potentially real.

Marsh had gone very still. Whatever she heard in Cade's testimony had triggered recognition, some connection to intelligence Thane hadn't shared with the full crew. Good. It meant her trust in Marsh's counterintelligence experience was justified.

"And the weapon?" Marsh pressed, but her voice had changed—acquired an edge that suggested she already suspected the answer.

Cade laughed, a sharp bark that dissolved into something close to sobbing. "You're not listening. The weapon isn't separate from the rest of it. It's all connected—the structures, the changes, the things in the dark. We thought we were exploring empty space. We were walking through a graveyard, and we woke something up."

Voss shifted beside Thane. "Commander, this is consistent with the psychological profiles we've developed for extended Dead Sector exposure. Paranoia, pattern-recognition failures, tendency toward mysticism—"

"Three separate survivors." Thane kept her voice level, but her mind raced through the implications. "Pulled from different locations. Describing the same phenomena. Either they've all been exposed to the same psychological trauma, developed the same delusions independently, or something in the Dead Sectors is generating consistent experiences."

"Or evidence of shared trauma responses. Mass hysteria isn't uncommon in isolated populations."

"Maybe." Thane studied Cade's face through the observation glass. The trembling wasn't performance—it ran too deep, settled in his bones like radiation exposure. "But I want to know what's generating the hysteria. Fear needs a source, even if it's misidentified."

She leaned toward the comm panel. "Lieutenant, ask him about recent activity. Specifically, any military vessels operating in this region."

Marsh nodded almost imperceptibly. "Have you seen any Coalition ships in this sector? Research vessels? Scientific expeditions?"

Cade's expression shifted to confusion. "Military? You're the first Coalition ship we've seen in eight months. Before that, maybe a patrol vessel every few weeks. Standard grid runs."

"No research vessels? Scientific expeditions?"

"Nothing official." Cade hesitated, and Thane saw the calculation in his eyes—weighing what he knew against what might keep him valuable, keep him alive. "But there were ships. Private ones, mostly. People who didn't want to be found, running from debts or warrants or failed marriages. The Dead Sectors attract those types."

"And recently?" Marsh asked.

Another pause, longer this time. Cade's fingers drummed against the table, a nervous pattern that accelerated as he made his decision. "There was a station. Three weeks ago, maybe four. Hard to track time out here. We picked up its beacon by accident, thought maybe it was another survivor group."

"Was it?"

"No." Cade's voice had gone flat. "It was operational. Full power, atmosphere, the works. But when we hailed them, they told us to stay away. Said they were conducting sensitive research and any approach would be considered hostile."

Thane's hands clenched at her sides. Her breath caught in her throat. A research station. Out here. Operational while survivors died in the dark. "Did they identify themselves?"

"Called themselves the Bastion Initiative."

The words hit like a physical blow. Thane's vision narrowed, her pulse thundering in her ears. Bastion. The word from Marcus's final transmission. The name buried in classified files she'd spent months accessing through unofficial channels. The connection between Faren's unauthorized research and whatever had gotten her brother killed.

Everything crystallized in that moment. The pieces she'd been gathering, the fragments of intelligence that Command had deemed too sensitive for standard distribution—they coalesced into something approaching pattern.

An ancient weapon. A hidden research facility. Faren, conducting unauthorized experiments in the Dead Sectors before Marcus discovered her activities.

Before she'd silenced him permanently.

"Get coordinates," Thane ordered, her voice cutting through the observation room like a blade. "Everything he knows about that station's location, capabilities, defensive systems. Everything."

Voss was already moving, but his voice carried carefully measured concern. "Commander, if this is a classified research facility, approaching without proper authorization could constitute a breach of operational protocols. We should relay this intelligence to Command and await—"

"Await what? More delays? More convenient classifications?" She turned on him, keeping her voice low enough that only he could hear. "We have credible intelligence regarding illegal operations in a restricted sector. We have a witness placing unusual activity in proximity to confirmed Dead Sector anomalies. That's well within our operational mandate."

"And if Command has reasons for keeping this classified? Reasons beyond protecting a murder suspect?"

"Then they can explain those reasons when we report our findings." Thane's expression hardened. "But I won't turn away from evidence because it might be inconvenient for someone's black budget project."

Voss held her gaze for a long moment before nodding. "Understood, Commander. But I'm logging my concerns for the record."

"I'd expect nothing less."

Marsh had pulled up a holographic display, and Cade was tracing coordinates with shaking hands. His voice filtered through the speakers, describing drift patterns and navigation hazards that had forced his crew off their intended course. Describing the station's silhouette against the stars, the way its power signature had blazed across their sensors like a beacon before they'd fled.

Describing everything Thane needed to find it.

The memory came unbidden—Marcus at eighteen, fresh from the academy, arguing with their father over dinner about the nature of military service. His eyes bright with conviction as he insisted that force should be the last resort, not the first. Their father's patient condescension as he reminded his son that the universe had sharp edges, and idealism dulled faster than blade steel.

Marcus had believed in justice. In accountability. In a system that worked when good people enforced it.

She would honor that.

Chapter Twenty-Eight

Reunion

Nicole stood outside the observation deck, her fingers trembling against the access panel. Through the narrow viewport, she could see Marcus silhouetted against the curve of Bastion's hull, his posture rigid as he stared into the void beyond. Three days had passed since his shuttle docked—since Commander Thane's terse shipwide announcement that Lieutenant Marcus Chen, declared KIA seven months prior, had returned from the Dead Sectors. Three days since Nicole had watched him disembark, hollow-eyed and twenty pounds lighter, and felt the universe tilt sideways.

The station hummed around her, its familiar vibrations doing nothing to settle her nerves. She'd faced down hostile boarding parties, navigated debris fields that would've shredded lesser pilots, survived the collapse of the Tertian Line during the war. None of that compared to the terror of pressing this button and stepping through that door.

Her hand moved before her courage failed completely. The panel chirped its acceptance, and the door whispered open on hydraulic breath.

Marcus didn't turn. His reflection in the reinforced glass showed the exhaustion that had carved itself into his features since his return—deeper lines bracketing his eyes, a hollowness in his cheeks that spoke of more than physical deprivation. The man who'd kissed her goodbye seven months ago had been solid, certain, full of the quiet confidence that had first drawn her to him. This version wore his body like borrowed clothes.

"Thought you might be Voss," he said quietly. "Coming to debrief me again."

"Just me." Nicole moved to stand beside him, keeping a careful distance. Close enough to talk. Far enough to retreat. "You've been avoiding the crew quarters."

"Been busy."

"You've been sleeping in the secondary maintenance bay. Chen mentioned it."

A muscle worked in his jaw. "Chen should mind his own business."

The silence stretched between them, filled only by the station's ambient noise—air circulators, the distant clang of cargo transfer, voices echoing through corridors designed to carry sound too far. Nicole studied Marcus's reflection, trying to reconcile the familiar lines of his face with the stranger inhabiting them.

"I don't know how to do this," she said finally. "I held your memorial service. I spoke about you in past tense while your mother wept in the front row. I watched them launch your empty coffin toward the solar wind because regulations required a ceremony even without remains. And now you're here, and I'm supposed to—what? Pretend those seven months didn't happen?"

Marcus's eyes closed. When he spoke, his voice carried a weight that made her chest ache. "I don't expect anything from you. I know what my disappearance did. What it cost."

"Do you?" The words came sharper than she'd intended. "Do you know I couldn't sleep in our quarters for two months? That I still wear your jacket when I run diagnostics because it's the only thing that makes the control room feel less empty? That I transferred to Bastion specifically because staying on the Meridian meant walking past your empty station every shift?"

He flinched as if she'd struck him. "Nicole—"

"You were dead." Her voice cracked on the word. "The scan data was conclusive. The debris pattern matched a catastrophic drive failure. There wasn't supposed to be any way you survived that, Marcus. And I made peace with it. I had to, because the alternative was falling apart when people needed me functional."

"I know."

"Then help me understand. Please." She forced herself to breathe, to gather the courage she'd been hoarding for three days. "Because right now you're standing three meters away and you might as well be on the other side of the Dead Sectors for all the distance between us."

Marcus turned finally, and the naked pain in his expression nearly undid her. He opened his mouth, closed it, his hands clenching and unclenching at his sides. For a long moment he just looked at her, and she watched thoughts move behind his eyes like ships navigating through fog.

"I don't know how to bridge that distance," he said at last. "Everything I went through out there—I can't explain it in a way that would make sense. And the things I had to do to survive, the compromises I made…" He trailed off, his voice catching. "I'm not sure I'm the same person you said goodbye to."

Nicole studied his face, this familiar stranger, and felt something shift in her chest. Fear, yes. Uncertainty. But underneath it, like bedrock beneath unstable ground, the same recognition that had first sparked between them in the Meridian's cramped navigation bay two years ago.

"Tell me about the last thing you remember," she said. "Before the accident. Before everything went wrong."

His eyebrows rose slightly. "Why?"

"Because I need to know if you remember it the way I do. I need to know if that's still real, even if everything after is complicated."

Marcus's expression softened. He moved closer, closing half the distance between them, and she could see the memory playing behind his eyes. "The night before the survey mission."

"Yes."

He was quiet for a moment, his gaze growing distant. "We were in crew quarters. You'd just finished a double shift routing supply manifests. You were exhausted, but you came anyway."

"You'd made dinner," Nicole said, the memory rising vivid and sharp. "Actual food, not ration packs. You'd traded your entire week's recreation credits for fresh vegetables from the hydroponics section."

* * *

The quarters smelled like sautéed peppers and garlic, an impossibly luxurious scent in the recycled air of a military vessel. Nicole stepped through the door and stopped, her exhaustion temporarily forgotten at the sight of Marcus bent over the small cooking unit they'd smuggled aboard in violation of at least three regulations.

"You actually did it," she said, laughing. "After all your talk about getting real food. I thought you were joking."

"Does this look like I'm joking?" Marcus glanced up, his face flushed from the heat of the improvised stove, and grinned. "Besides, tomorrow I'm heading out for a three-week survey run. I wanted one good meal with you before I spend twenty-one days eating rehydrated protein paste."

She crossed to him, wound her arms around his waist from behind, pressed her face against his shoulder. He was warm and solid and real, and the knot of tension she'd been carrying through fourteen hours of logistics work began to unwind.

"You're trying to bribe me with food," she murmured against his shirt.

"Is it working?"

"Completely."

They ate sitting on his bunk, plates balanced on their knees, talking about nothing important. Nicole told him about the requisition officer who'd tried to argue that fuel rods were discretionary supplies. Marcus described the navigation charts for the survey sector, pointed out the peculiar gravity eddies that made standard plotting algorithms unreliable. Easy conversation, comfortable in its familiarity.

Afterward, when the plates were set aside and the illegal cooking unit dismantled and hidden, Marcus pulled her close. The quarters were barely large enough for two people, the walls close and gray, but in that moment it felt perfect.

"Come back safe," Nicole said against his chest. "Three weeks isn't that long, but come back anyway."

"Always." He tilted her chin up, his eyes serious. "I've got too much here worth coming back to."

She kissed him then, tasting garlic and promises, feeling the steady beat of his heart beneath her palm. They made love slowly, carefully, as if they had all the time in the universe instead of just one night. His hands traced familiar paths along her skin, and she memorized the sound of his breathing, the way he whispered her name in the darkness.

Later, as she drifted toward sleep wrapped in his arms, he spoke so quietly she almost missed it.

"Whatever happens out there," he said, "remember this. Remember us like this."

At the time, she'd thought it was just sentiment. Just the kind of thing people said before temporary separations. She'd kissed his shoulder and fallen asleep without realizing it was goodbye.

* * *

"I thought you were being romantic," Nicole said, the observation deck snapping back into focus around them. Her eyes stung with unshed tears. "That last thing you said. I didn't know you had a premonition."

"I didn't." Marcus's voice was rough. "Or maybe I did. There'd been equipment failures all week. Small things. Inconsistencies in the survey vessel's diagnostic reports. I logged them, but the mission timeline was tight and Command didn't want delays. So I went anyway, and I spent that whole night trying not to think about what might go wrong."

"You should have told me."

"And said what? That I had a bad feeling? That I was scared?" He shook his head. "You would have worried, and it wouldn't have changed anything. The mission was mandatory. The only choice I had was whether to let fear poison our last night together."

Nicole felt anger flash through her, hot and clarifying. "Don't do that. Don't decide what I can handle. I would have worried anyway—I always did when you went out. But at least I would have understood. At least that last night wouldn't feel like a beautiful lie now."

The words landed harder than she'd intended. Marcus went still, something breaking behind his eyes.

"You're right," he said quietly. "You're right, and I'm sorry. I was trying to protect you, and all I did was make it worse."

"Yes. You did." She let the truth sit between them, sharp-edged and necessary. "So no more protection. No more deciding what I can bear. If we're going to have any chance of rebuilding this, I need to know you'll trust me with the hard things."

"Even if they hurt?"

"Especially then."

Marcus looked at her for a long moment, something shifting in his expression. "I'm sorry," he said again, and this time it carried more weight. "For leaving you. For surviving in a way that made the loss worse when you found out. For coming back different. For every decision I made that put that grief in your eyes."

"Stop apologizing and tell me what I need to hear." Nicole moved closer, drawn by the rawness in his voice despite her fear. "Tell me if there's anything left of what we had. If that night I keep replaying was real or just something I'm holding onto because grief makes us stupid."

"It was real." His hand reached out, hesitant, fingers trembling in the space between them. "Everything we had was real. Everything I felt was real. That hasn't changed, Nicole. That's the one thing that didn't break out there in the dark."

She caught his hand before she could second-guess herself. The touch sent electricity through her nerves—seven months of absence, three days of careful distance, and now this. His skin against hers, warm and alive and impossible. She'd dreamed this contact so many times in the weeks after his death, phantom sensations that left her aching when she woke.

"I don't know how to trust that," she whispered. "I don't know how to believe in something that was taken away so completely."

"Then don't trust it yet." Marcus moved closer, closing the last of the distance between them, until she could feel the heat radiating from his body. "Just let me show you I'm here. Let me prove it's real, one day at a time, for as long as it takes."

Nicole looked up at him, this man who was Marcus and not-Marcus, familiar and foreign. Fear warred with longing in her chest. The safe choice would be to step back, to maintain distance until she could process everything. The safe choice would be to protect herself from the possibility of losing him again.

She'd never been good at safe choices.

Her free hand rose to his chest, pressed flat against his sternum. His heart hammered beneath her palm, as rapid and uncertain as her own. "If I do this," she said, "if I let you back in—I need you to promise me something."

"Anything."

"No more disappearing. No more deciding what I can handle on my own. If you're going to die, you die with me knowing. You don't leave me to bury an empty coffin while you're fighting for survival somewhere I can't reach."

"I promise." His voice broke on the words. "Nicole, I promise. Never again."

She studied his face, searching for the deception she'd learned to expect from the universe. Found only raw, desperate honesty—the kind that couldn't be faked, that came from someone who'd spent seven months alone with nothing but regret for company.

"Okay," she whispered. "Okay."

His arms came around her, tentative at first, as if he expected her to pull away. When she didn't, when she pressed closer instead, his embrace tightened with something like desperation. She felt him shaking—or maybe she was shaking, or both of them together, trembling with the effort of bridging seven months of grief and silence.

"I'm sorry," he said against her hair. "I'm so sorry."

"I know." She tilted her head back, found his eyes. "I'm still angry. I'm still hurt. But I'm also—" Her voice caught. "I'm so glad you're alive. I'm so glad you came back."

He kissed her then, and it was nothing like the careful, practiced kisses from before. This was desperate and raw, tasting of tears and relief and the particular flavor of second chances. Nicole felt something crack open in her chest—the armored compartment where she'd locked away everything she'd felt for him, sealed tight against the pain of remembering.

The kiss deepened. Her fingers found the back of his neck, pulled him closer. His hands spread across her back, tracing the familiar geography of her spine as if relearning something precious.

They didn't speak. Words felt inadequate for what was passing between them—the slow, tentative process of rebuilding trust through touch. Nicole let herself feel it all: the fear, the longing, the grief that hadn't fully faded and maybe never would. She let herself be present in his arms without knowing what came next.

When they finally broke apart, both breathing hard, Marcus pressed his forehead against hers.

"I don't deserve this," he said quietly. "I don't deserve you giving me another chance."

"Probably not." Nicole traced her thumb along his jaw, feeling the stubble he hadn't bothered to shave. "But I've spent seven months learning to live without you. I don't want to spend the rest of my life wondering what would have happened if I'd chosen forgiveness."

"Is that what this is? Forgiveness?"

"It's a start." She stepped back, just far enough to see his whole face. "The rest we figure out together. One day at a time, like you said."

Marcus nodded slowly. Something like hope flickered in his eyes—fragile and uncertain, but present. Alive.

The observation deck door chimed. They both turned as it slid open, revealing Daven's weathered face.

"Sorry to interrupt," he said, his expression carefully neutral despite what he'd obviously witnessed. "But Command just flagged an incoming transmission. Priority one, encrypted, originating from the Dead Sectors." His eyes met Nicole's. "It's addressed to Marcus. By his real name."

Nicole felt the moment shatter around her. Three days of processing, three days of rebuilding, and now this—proof that whatever Marcus had survived out there, it hadn't finished with him.

"Who knows that name?" she asked, her voice steady despite the ice forming in her stomach.

Marcus's face had gone pale. "No one who should be sending messages."

"Then we'd better find out who is." Nicole straightened, commander posture reasserting itself over lover vulnerability. "Briefing room. Five minutes."

She moved toward the door, paused, looked back at Marcus. He stood frozen where she'd left him, caught between the reunion they'd barely begun and the shadows reaching out from his past.

"Whatever this is," she said, "we face it together. That's how it works now."

He nodded once, something hardening in his expression. The man who'd survived seven months in the Dead Sectors, emerging from beneath the vulnerable surface she'd just touched.

"Together," he agreed.

But as Nicole walked toward the briefing room, she couldn't shake the feeling that whatever waited in that transmission was about to change everything. Again.

Chapter Twenty-Nine

Strike

Nicole's terminal chimed at 0347 hours, dragging her from fitful sleep in quarters she'd barely visited these past three days. She blinked at the message priority code—tactical alert, command center, immediate response required.

She dressed and moved in under two minutes.

The station's corridors possessed that hollow quality they assumed during night cycle, her footsteps ricocheting off bulkheads as she navigated toward the command center. A few other figures hurried through the passages—shift workers, maintenance crews, and now others responding to the same alert she'd received. No alarms, no announcements. Whatever this was, Thane was containing it.

Marcus intercepted her at the command center entrance, looking like he'd never surrendered to sleep. "Long-range sensors flagged something twenty minutes ago," he said without preamble. "You need to see this."

Inside, Captain Thane stood at the tactical console, her posture radiating controlled tension. The main display showed the system map, three highlighted contacts glowing at the edge of sensor range.

"Three vessels," Thane said as Nicole approached. "Heavy configuration, civilian transponders, no filed flight plans. They've been in-system for at least six hours."

"Six hours?" Nicole stared at the display. "How did they penetrate that close without—"

"They masked themselves in commercial traffic until they cleared the main shipping lanes." Marcus summoned the sensor logs. "Our long-range systems tagged them as probable bulk haulers. It wasn't until they separated from the traffic pattern that the computer started analyzing their drive signatures properly." He highlighted the readings. "These aren't haulers. They're running military-grade engines behind civilian facades."

Nicole studied the trajectories. "Heading?"

"Directly toward us. If they maintain current velocity, they'll reach minimum weapons range in approximately four hours."

The pieces assembled themselves with cold certainty. "Obsidian."

"Almost certainly," Thane said. "Though they're not advertising it."

Nicole pulled up a secondary console, accessing the legal briefings Marcus had been compiling over the past weeks. Obsidian Solutions had been escalating—first the complaints about "unauthorized appropriation," then the acquisition of patrol contracts in adjacent sectors, now vessels with military capabilities approaching the station with no legitimate business reason.

"You think they're coming for the artifact," she said.

"I think we're about to discover what happens when corporate legal strategies fail." Thane gestured at the display. "They've spent weeks constructing a paper trail. Complaints filed through proper channels, offers to 'assist' with archaeological assessment, friendly suggestions about proper custody procedures. When none of that worked…"

"They move to less friendly suggestions." Marcus summoned another file. "I've been tracking their hiring. Former military personnel, dozens of them. The kind with experience in hostile boarding actions. And look at this—they quietly acquired a salvage litigation firm specializing in disputed claims in gray legal zones."

Nicole felt ice settle in her stomach. "They're preparing for the aftermath. They're going to do something aggressive, then litigate it in courts while they exploit whatever they take."

"That's my assessment," Thane said. "Which is why we're moving to elevated alert status. Quietly. I don't want station-wide panic, but I want us ready." She turned to Nicole. "The artifact. If someone attempted to seize it by force, what would happen?"

There it was—the question Nicole had been dreading since the first corporate probe arrived.

"The access protocols require specific authorization," she said carefully. "Biometric confirmation, neural signature matching, voluntary compliance. But those are software locks. If they're willing to damage the artifact itself, to physically tear it from its mountings…" She hesitated. "We've seen what happens when you try to brute-force access to sealed sections. The feedback effect that injured Technician Harrison. But that was small-scale."

"And large-scale?" Marcus asked.

"The artifact's power systems are integrated with station infrastructure now. A major feedback pulse could cascade through our network. System-wide failures, at minimum." Nicole met Thane's eyes. "The artifact contains active processes we still don't understand. Quantum states that shouldn't be stable according to our physics. If those were disrupted violently…"

"Worst case," Thane said.

"I honestly don't know. Nothing good."

Thane remained silent for a moment, then nodded. "Security teams to strategic positions. Damage control parties staged at key locations. Engineering, I want emergency power systems ready to assume control if the main grid fails. And someone get me a direct line to Sector Authority—I want them aware we have potentially hostile vessels inbound."

Orders rippled outward, the command center shifting from late-night monitoring to combat preparation with practiced efficiency. Nicole watched the tactical display, three red icons slowly converging on the station's position. Four hours. Maybe less if they accelerated.

"I need to check the artifact's containment protocols," she said. "Make sure everything's as secure as I can make it."

"Go," Thane said. "Marcus, go with her. I want our two artifact specialists alive and together where I can find them."

* * *

The artifact chamber felt different in the pre-dawn hours, the crystalline matrices pulsing with that eternal internal light. Nicole went straight to the control console while Marcus sealed the entrance behind them.

"What are you thinking?" he asked.

"Enhanced security protocols. Additional authorization layers." Her fingers danced across the interface, summoning the access management systems. "I can add biometric confirmation requirements, neural signature matching that checks for coercion through baseline brainwave analysis. Even if they capture me, they won't be able to force access without—"

"Without what? You don't think they'd have ways to make you cooperate?"

Nicole's hands stilled on the console. "Then what do you suggest?"

"I don't know." Marcus leaned against the bulkhead, exhaustion and tension warring in his expression. "We're two archaeologists and a mid-sized station crew facing what might be a corporate military operation. If they're really willing to attack, if they're willing to accept the legal consequences…" He shook his head. "Maybe we should just surrender it. Let them take it and deal with whatever happens."

"You don't mean that."

"No," he admitted. "But I don't want to watch people die over an artifact either, no matter how significant."

Nicole turned back to the console. "Then we make sure they can't use it even if they take it. We lock it down so thoroughly that extracting it becomes more trouble than it's worth."

She worked quickly, implementing layer after layer of authorization requirements. Biometric locks. Neural signature verification. Coercion detection algorithms that would refuse commands given under altered mental states. Mathematical challenges that required specific knowledge only she possessed. Each barrier was surmountable given enough time and expertise, but together they formed a fortress of security protocols.

The console chimed confirmation as she finished. On the main display, the chronometer showed 0521 hours. Somewhere out there, those three vessels were drawing closer.

Marcus's comm chirped. He listened, his face tightening. "Understood. We're on our way." He looked at Nicole. "Thane's hailing them. She wants us in the command center."

They sealed the artifact chamber and headed back through corridors that were no longer empty. Security teams moved with purpose toward assigned positions. Crew members hurried to duty stations. The station had woken to the threat, and now everyone was preparing for what might come.

In the command center, Thane stood at the communications console. "Broadcasting on all standard frequencies," she said. "Unidentified vessels, this is Bastion Station actual. You are approaching restricted space. Identify yourselves and state your business or alter course immediately."

The response came quickly—too quickly, like they'd been waiting for contact.

"Bastion Station, this is commercial vessel Prometheus Dawn. We are experiencing navigation system malfunctions and request permission to approach for technical assistance."

Marcus pulled up the sensor data. "They're not experiencing malfunctions. Their systems are operating normally, and they just adjusted course to compensate for stellar drift. Perfect precision."

"So they're lying," Nicole said.

"Badly." Thane keyed the comm again. "Prometheus Dawn, our sensors show no signs of system malfunction. You are ordered to alter course and depart this sector immediately."

Silence. Then: "Bastion Station, we have personnel aboard requiring medical attention. Request emergency docking clearance under rescue protocols."

"They're not even trying to make this convincing," Marcus muttered.

Thane's expression hardened. "All hands, this is the captain. Alert status elevated to full defensive posture. Seal all pressure doors and prepare for potential hostile action. This is not a drill."

The command center transformed, crew members flowing to combat stations with the smooth efficiency of repeated drills. Nicole found herself at an auxiliary console, feeling useless while everyone else executed specific emergency protocols.

"What can I do?" she asked Marcus.

"Stay alive. If they board, you're the primary target. The artifact's useless to them without you."

The tactical display updated. The approaching vessels had begun accelerating, their facade of distress dropping away. New data scrolled across the screen—weapons systems powering up, launch bay doors opening, shields activating.

"Captain!" the sensor officer called. "Multiple launch signatures. Missiles inbound, impact in four minutes."

The klaxon screamed to life.

"Point defense systems engage!" Thane's voice cut through the alarm. "All personnel to hardened locations. Damage control teams stand ready."

Nicole watched the missiles track across the display, feeling oddly detached from the reality of incoming weapons. This couldn't actually be happening. Corporate disputes didn't escalate to military force without months of escalation, without diplomatic intervention, without—

The first impact shattered her disbelief.

The station shuddered, deck plates ringing like a struck bell. Lights flickered. On the tactical display, three missiles had penetrated the point defense network. Secondary explosions bloomed as station systems failed.

"Breaches in Sectors Seven and Twelve," someone reported. "Damage control responding."

More impacts. The Obsidian vessels had closed to weapons range and were methodically targeting defensive emplacements, power distribution nodes, communication arrays. Each hit was precise, calculated, part of a practiced assault pattern.

"They've done this before," Marcus said quietly. "This isn't their first station assault."

The weapons fire paused. On the tactical display, smaller craft separated from the main vessels—boarding pods, at least a dozen of them, each carrying armed personnel.

"Here they come," Thane said. "All security teams, prepare to repel boarders. Non-essential personnel to designated safe zones."

Nicole looked at Marcus. "The artifact."

"We need to get down there. Now."

They ran, emergency lighting flickering in corridors that shook with repeated impacts. Other crew members rushed past them—security teams assembling at defensive positions, medical personnel moving toward casualty stations, engineers trying to contain power failures before they cascaded.

An explosion somewhere nearby sent Nicole stumbling into a bulkhead. Marcus pulled her upright, and they kept moving. The artifact chamber was three levels down and two sectors over—an eternity through corridors that were rapidly becoming combat zones.

They rounded a corner and nearly collided with a security team taking up defensive positions. The squad leader recognized Nicole and waved them through.

"Boarders in Sector Nine," she reported. "Moving toward the research wing. Get to cover—we'll hold them here."

More gunfire, closer now. The distinctive crack of plasma weapons echoed through the metal corridors. Nicole heard screaming somewhere behind them—cut off abruptly.

They reached the artifact chamber's outer corridor. The pressure door was sealed, emergency protocols activated. Nicole pressed her palm to the biometric scanner, praying her hands weren't shaking too badly for recognition.

The door opened.

Inside, the artifact pulsed with agitated light, its crystalline matrices flickering in patterns she'd never seen. It knew something was wrong. Could feel the violence permeating the station.

"Seal us in," Nicole said. "Full lockdown."

Marcus activated the security protocols. Heavy blast doors descended, transforming the chamber into a hardened bunker. On the auxiliary console, they could monitor station-wide communications—desperate calls for reinforcement, damage reports cascading faster than anyone could track, Thane's voice cutting through the chaos with orders that sounded increasingly like prayers.

Then a new voice, unexpected and familiar.

"Bastion Station, this is Viktor Crane aboard the Iron Resolve. I heard you were having a party. Mind if we crash it?"

Nicole stared at the comm system. Viktor Crane—the arms dealer from Veridia's lower levels, the man she'd negotiated weapons contracts with three weeks ago. What was he doing here?

"Crane," Thane responded, surprise leaking through her command voice. "Iron Resolve, what's your status?"

"Three ships, fully armed, extremely motivated. Obsidian Solutions has been muscling into my territory for months. Consider this a professional courtesy." A pause, then: "Targeting solutions locked. Opening fire."

On the tactical display, new contacts appeared—Crane's vessels, emerging from behind a debris field where they'd apparently been waiting. Their weapons blazed to life, plasma bolts and missiles streaking toward the Obsidian fleet.

The attackers hadn't expected reinforcement. Their formation scattered as Crane's ships tore into their flank. One Obsidian vessel took a direct hit to its engine section and began venting atmosphere. Another broke off its assault run, turning to engage this new threat.

"Boarders are retreating," someone reported on the command channel. "Pulling back to their pods."

"Don't let them escape," Thane ordered. "Security teams, pursue and contain."

The battle shifted from desperate defense to controlled counterattack. Crane's ships drove the Obsidian fleet back, his pilots fighting with the vicious efficiency of people who took corporate incursion personally. On the station, security teams pushed the boarders toward airlocks that were no longer under their control.

Twenty minutes later, it was over.

One Obsidian vessel destroyed. One crippled and surrendering. The third fleeing at maximum burn, too damaged to continue the assault. Boarding parties captured or killed. The station damaged but intact.

Nicole watched the surviving attackers retreat on the tactical display, her legs suddenly too weak to hold her. She sank into a chair, the adrenaline crash hitting like a physical blow.

"We won," Marcus said, his voice carrying more disbelief than triumph.

"Crane won." Nicole looked at the artifact, still pulsing with that agitated light. "We just survived."

The comm crackled with Crane's voice, casual as ever despite the battle he'd just fought on their behalf. "Bastion Station, Iron Resolve. I believe we have terms to discuss. At your convenience, of course."

Thane's response was dry. "At our convenience. Right."

Nicole leaned back, exhaustion settling into her bones. They'd survived. The artifact was secure. But Crane's timely arrival raised questions she didn't have answers for—questions about who else was watching, who else was waiting, who else had plans for the thing they'd pulled from the ruins.

"This isn't over," she said quietly. "Obsidian won't stop because one assault failed."

"No," Marcus agreed. "But at least now we know what they're willing to do." He moved to stand beside her, his hand finding her shoulder. "And we know we're not as alone as we thought."

Through the chamber's reinforced walls, Nicole could feel the station settling around them—damage being assessed, casualties being counted, the cost of survival being calculated. And through it all, the artifact pulsed with patient light, keeping its own counsel about what it had witnessed.

What it had felt.

What it might do next.
