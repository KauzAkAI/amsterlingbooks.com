Submitted: Three Days After the Original Deadline  ·  Peer-Reviewed: By Colleagues Who All Said "We Were Going To Do This Study"  ·  Accepted: At 9:47am, Which Was Originally Scheduled for 9:00am  ·  Dedicated: To the 6am Self. You Tried. You Always Try. It Never Works. This Paper Explains Why.

This paper presents the first formal multi-disciplinary analysis of the Snooze Button Paradox (SBP): the documented, near-universal phenomenon in which a human subject sets an alarm at night with genuine intention, is awakened by that alarm at the appointed time, and immediately chooses — in a series of repeated, confident decisions — to delay waking until significantly after the appointed time, producing an outcome that is both the opposite of the original intention and completely predictable to everyone except the subject at the moment of alarm-setting.

We present formal proof, across six disciplines, that the 10:47pm Alarm-Setter (Person A) and the 6:09am Snooze-Presser (Person B) are demonstrably distinct individuals under philosophy of personal identity, neuroscience, game theory, and behavioral economics. This finding has significant implications: if A and B are different people, Person A cannot be held responsible for Person B's behavior. Person B, however, is fully responsible for everything that happens after 7:15am, which is when Person A's original schedule would have required him to already be somewhere else.

Our central findings: the snooze arithmetic series does not converge through internal mechanism; the prefrontal cortex is measurably offline during snooze events; the 6am self consistently loses to the 6:09am self in every documented game-theoretic encounter despite holding what should be a decisive structural advantage; and the lifetime productivity cost of the snooze habit is a number we are going to tell you but would like you to be seated when you read it.

§ 1 Introduction: The Phenomenon and Its Architecture

At approximately 10:47pm on any given weeknight, a human subject — typically male, though the phenomenon is documented across all demographics — reaches for his phone, opens the alarm application, and sets an alarm for 6:00am. He does this with full cognitive function. He is awake. His prefrontal cortex is operational. He has correctly assessed that the following morning requires him to be somewhere at a specific time and that 6:00am provides adequate preparation time. This is a rational decision, made by a rational person, in full possession of the relevant facts.

At 6:00am, the alarm sounds.

At 6:00am and approximately four seconds, the alarm is silenced.

At 6:09am, the alarm sounds again.

This cycle continues.

The research team documents the remainder of the event below, in real time, as a service to the reader and as a monument to the human capacity for optimism in the face of overwhelming counter-evidence.

§ 2 Philosophy: Are These the Same Person?

The philosophy of personal identity has grappled for centuries with the question of what makes a person at one time the same person as a person at another time. John Locke argued that personal identity consists in psychological continuity — specifically, the continuity of conscious memory. Derek Parfit extended this to argue that personal identity is not what matters; what matters is psychological connectedness, which can come in degrees.

The research team applies these frameworks to the 10:47pm alarm-setter and the 6:09am snooze-presser. The results are, Prof. Ravenswood-Clarke notes, "more definitive than I expected when I started, and more personally relevant than I was comfortable with."

2.1   The Lockean Test: Memory Continuity

For Locke, Person A and Person B are the same person if and only if Person B can consciously remember being Person A. This requires an accessible memory chain between the two states. The research team tested this directly: at 7:17am, subjects were asked what they remembered from 10:47pm the previous evening. Specifically: what time did you set the alarm for, and why?

91% of subjects remembered setting an alarm. 78% remembered the general time. 12% could recall the specific reasoning — "I had the 8:30 meeting." 0% reported any vivid, continuous conscious experience bridging the alarm-setting and the first snooze press. The memory chain is broken by sleep. Under Locke's criterion, Person A and Person B are not the same person.

2.2   The Parfit Test: Psychological Connectedness

Parfit requires not strict memory continuity but psychological connectedness — overlapping chains of intentions, beliefs, and desires. Person A had specific intentions: wake at 6, prepare by 7:15, arrive at destination at 8:30. Person B has no access to these intentions at 6:00am. Person B has one operational priority: more sleep. Person B does not know the meeting is at 8:30. Person B does not know about the meeting. Person B barely knows what year it is.

The psychological connectedness between 10:47pm and 6:09am is, Parfit's framework confirms, at or near zero. These are not meaningfully the same person. They share a body. They do not share a decision-making apparatus, a set of active intentions, or functional prefrontal cortex availability.

‡ Prof. Ravenswood-Clarke notes the significant practical implication of this finding: if Person A and Person B are distinct individuals under both major frameworks of personal identity theory, then Person A's decision to set an alarm at 6am is, strictly speaking, an attempt to bind a future person without that person's consent. This is legally and philosophically contentious. Person B did not agree to be awoken at 6am. Person B was not consulted. Person B, upon awakening, demonstrates his disagreement seven times in nine-minute increments. The research team notes that Person B's position, while inconvenient, is philosophically coherent. This does not mean Person B is right. It means Person A needs a better commitment device. Section 8 addresses this. It does not fully solve the problem.

§ 3 Game Theory: The 6am Self Loses Every Time

In game theory, a commitment problem occurs when a player cannot credibly commit to a future action because the future self who must execute the action has different preferences than the present self who made the commitment. This is precisely the structure of the Snooze Button Paradox.

3.1   The Game Structure

Person A (10:47pm) sets an alarm as a commitment device — a mechanism designed to force Person B (6:00am) to wake up. This is a classic pre-commitment strategy, the same logic behind Ulysses having himself tied to the mast. The alarm is the mast. Person A is Ulysses. Person B is also Ulysses but without the rope, because Person B has been given full access to the snooze button, which is a rope with an off switch, which entirely defeats the purpose of the rope.

3.2   Why Person A Keeps Setting the Alarm

The most puzzling finding in the game theory analysis is not that Person B wins. It is that Person A keeps trying. Despite a win rate of 8.2%, Person A sets the alarm again the following evening with full confidence. This is not irrational optimism. This is a structural feature of the game: Person A and Person B never meet. Person A has never experienced losing. Person B has never experienced setting the alarm. Each operates in complete ignorance of the other's track record. The game resets every 24 hours. The alarm gets set. Person B wins. Neither learns anything.

§ 4 Mathematics: The Snooze Arithmetic Progression

The snooze sequence is mathematically a simple arithmetic progression with first term a₀ = 6:00am and common difference d = 9 minutes:

an = a0 + n·d = 6:00 + n(9 min)

a1 = 6:09  ·  a2 = 6:18  ·  a3 = 6:27  ·  an = 6:00 + 9n
The series is unbounded. It has no natural termination point — it will continue generating valid alarm times indefinitely. The subject believes, at each term, that the next term will be the last. This belief is generated fresh at each alarm and has never, in the documented literature, been correct for the reason the subject believed it to be correct.

Dr. Tanaka-Okonkwo's contribution to this section is a formal proof that the snooze sequence terminates not through mathematical convergence but through one of two external mechanisms: (1) the schedule catastrophe — the moment at which remaining preparation time falls below a threshold that even Person B cannot deny, and (2) a third party — typically a partner, a child, or a meeting notification — who introduces an exogenous shock to the system.

Ttermination = min(Tcatastrophe, Texternal shock)
The series terminates when panic arrives or someone else arrives first. In households where a partner is present, Texternal shock is typically earlier and arrives with a specific tone that Person B has learned to classify as "not negotiable." This is the most effective termination mechanism documented. Its effectiveness increases with relationship duration.
** Dr. Tanaka-Okonkwo notes that the 9-minute snooze interval is not arbitrary — it is a deliberate product decision based on human sleep cycle research suggesting that 9 minutes is long enough to feel like meaningful sleep but short enough that deeper sleep stages are not re-entered, theoretically making waking easier. She notes that this interval was designed to facilitate gentle waking. She notes that it instead facilitates seven gentle wakings followed by one catastrophic waking, and that this outcome was apparently not fully modeled in the original design. She has been in contact with the relevant engineering teams. They have not responded.

§ 5 Neuroscience: The Prefrontal Cortex Is Not At Work

Dr. Venkataraman-Holloway's neuroimaging contribution to this paper is, she reports, "the section that required the least analysis and produced the most nodding at conferences." The finding is simple. The implications are enormous. The finding is this:

During snooze events, the prefrontal cortex — the brain region responsible for rational decision-making, impulse control, planning, and the evaluation of long-term consequences — is functionally offline.

Sleep inertia — the transitional state between sleep and full waking — produces a measurable suppression of prefrontal activity lasting between 15 and 30 minutes after awakening, with maximum suppression in the first 5 minutes. Person B makes his snooze decision at approximately T+4 seconds — 4 seconds after the alarm sounds, while sleep inertia is at its peak and the prefrontal cortex is at its lowest measured activity.

Person B is not deciding to snooze. Person B is a biological process with a thumb.

§ 6 Behavioral Economics: Hyperbolic Discounting and the Nine-Minute Premium

Standard economic models assume that humans discount future value at a constant rate — a dollar tomorrow is worth slightly less than a dollar today, and the discount is consistent across all time horizons. This is called exponential discounting. It is rational. It is also not what humans do.

Humans use hyperbolic discounting: they apply a dramatically higher discount rate to the immediate future than to the distant future. A reward now is worth enormously more than a reward in five minutes, which is only slightly less valuable than a reward in an hour.

V(t) = V₀ / (1 + k·t)
Where V(t) is the present value of a future reward at time t, V₀ is the undiscounted reward value, and k is the hyperbolic discount rate. At 6:00am, with sleep inertia active, k approaches its maximum documented value in human subjects. Nine minutes of additional sleep becomes worth approximately 47 minutes of future productivity. This is not a calculation. This is a feeling. The feeling is wrong. The feeling is extremely compelling.

Prof. Abara-Whitfield's analysis demonstrates that at 6:00am, the subject applies a hyperbolic discount so severe that nine minutes of immediate sleep is valued, by his nervous system, as approximately equal to an entire functioning morning. This is incorrect by every objective measure. It is also completely consistent with documented hyperbolic discounting behavior. The subject is not irrational. He is human. The distinction matters less at 6am than you might hope.

§ 7 Economics: The Lifetime Cost of Nine Minutes

Prof. Abara-Whitfield's lifetime cost analysis proceeds from the following documented parameters: average 7 snooze presses per morning, 9 minutes each, 5 mornings per week, 48 weeks per year, across a working lifespan of 45 years. The arithmetic produces a number he asked the research team to present with what he called "appropriate gravity."

Tsnooze lifetime = 7 × 9 × 5 × 48 × 45 = 680,400 minutes = 11,340 hours = 472.5 days
The average person who snoozes habitually spends approximately 472.5 days of their life in the snooze window — the specific, liminal, neither-asleep-nor-awake state that is too disrupted to be restorative sleep and too unconscious to be productive time. It is not rest. It is not waking. It is 472 days of being a biological process with a thumb, floating in the specific misery of knowing the alarm will sound again in nine minutes.

At the U.S. median hourly wage, this represents a productivity opportunity cost of approximately $98,400 across a working lifetime. Prof. Abara-Whitfield notes that this figure does not represent what was lost — the snooze window is not productive time that could be redirected. It represents what was neither gained nor enjoyed: not real sleep, not real morning, not rest, not work. It is 472 days of being in a waiting room that has an alarm in it.

‡‡ Prof. Abara-Whitfield also calculated the cost of the Lucid Snooze Window specifically — the period after snooze 4 when the subject is making conscious decisions. Approximately 31% of total snooze time occurs in the LSW. This represents roughly 146 days of the lifetime total in which the subject was fully aware he should get up, chose not to, and then had to live with himself about it. No monetary value can be assigned to this. Prof. Abara-Whitfield tried. He stopped. Some things cannot be quantified without becoming depressing in a way that exceeds the scope of this paper.

§ 8 Selected Case Studies

Subject M-58, age 58, has set his alarm for 6:00am on 312 of the 365 days documented in the longitudinal study period. He has risen at or before 6:15am on 26 of those occasions (8.3%). On the remaining 286 occasions, his wake time ranged from 6:27am to 7:44am, with a median of 7:08am.

Subject M-58 describes himself as "a morning person who just needs a minute to get going." His phone records show that "a minute to get going" has a documented range of 9 to 104 minutes and a mean of 68 minutes. When presented with this data, Subject M-58 said: "That doesn't sound right." The data is right.

Most significantly: Subject M-58 re-sets his alarm for 6:00am every evening. Without exception. After 286 consecutive mornings in which Person B defeated Person A, Person A sets the alarm again with what Subject M-58 describes as "genuine intention." The research team does not doubt this. Person A always has genuine intention. Person A has never met Person B. Person A will never learn. This is, the research team notes, both the flaw in the system and the thing that makes Person A kind of wonderful.

Person A is an optimist. Person A has always been an optimist. Person A will set the alarm for 6am tomorrow. Person B will press snooze. This is not a tragedy. This is a love story between a man and his best intentions, playing out every morning across nine-minute intervals, for the rest of his life.

Subject F-55, the partner of Subject M-58, was interviewed as a longitudinal observer of the SBP phenomenon. She was asked, first, how many times Subject M-58 woke at his intended alarm time in the past year. She said: "Maybe twenty times." The documented figure is 26. She was close.

She was asked whether she found this frustrating. She thought about this for a long time. Then she said: "He sets it for 6 every night. He believes it every time. There's something about that." When asked to elaborate, she said: "He's always going to try to be the person who gets up at 6. He just never quite is." She paused. "I find that sort of sweet and sort of maddening in equal measure. Both at the same time."

The research team has classified Subject F-55's response as the most accurate description of the Snooze Button Paradox in the entire collected data. We have proposed it as the clinical summary. "He's always going to try to be the person who gets up at 6. He just never quite is." The person who sets the alarm and the person who hits snooze are different people. Person A keeps trying anyway. The research team is, ultimately, rooting for Person A.

§ 9 Conclusions: On Person A, Person B, and the Alarm That Gets Set Anyway

We have proven, across philosophy, game theory, neuroscience, mathematics, and behavioral economics, that the man who sets the alarm and the man who hits snooze are demonstrably distinct individuals. Person A cannot be held responsible for Person B's behavior. Person B cannot be expected to honor Person A's commitments. The game is structurally unwinnable by Person A because the commitment device is operated by the very agent it was designed to constrain, who has a prefrontal cortex offline and a thumb that knows where the button is.

And yet Person A sets the alarm.

Every evening. 10:47pm. 6:00am. With genuine intention and the specific, undefeated optimism of someone who has never successfully inhabited the morning they planned and has never stopped planning it.

The research team has spent considerable time with this finding. It is, technically, irrational. It is also, we have come to believe, one of the most human things a person does. To plan a version of yourself that you will not quite be. To commit, every night, to a morning self who is more organized, more punctual, more intentional than the warm unconscious person who will actually wake up and immediately begin negotiating. To believe — with the quiet confidence of someone who has never won this particular game — that tomorrow morning might be different.

It will not be different. The mathematics confirm this. The neuroscience confirms this. The game theory is unanimous.

Set the alarm anyway. Person A is worth believing in.
