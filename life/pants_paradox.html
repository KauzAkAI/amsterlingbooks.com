This paper presents the first comprehensive multi-disciplinary analysis of the Pre-Event Ritual Inquiry (PERI) — colloquially expressed as "Does this make my butt look bigger?" — a question that has been misclassified, by approximately 100% of its respondents, as a question about pants. It is not a question about pants. It has never been a question about pants. The pants are an occasion, not a subject. The research team has spent eleven months proving this formally, across six independent disciplines, in a paper that every woman who reads it will describe as "obviously" and every man who reads it will describe as "I had no idea" and then immediately recall every time he answered it incorrectly.

We demonstrate: (1) game theory confirms no correct answer exists within the pants-assessment framework — every response produces a net-negative outcome, making the pants frame the wrong frame entirely; (2) Speech Act Theory (Austin, 1962) classifies the PERI as a performative ritual rather than a constative inquiry — it is doing something, not asking something; (3) information theory confirms the question carries zero entropy with respect to pants and significant entropy with respect to something else; (4) neuroimaging documents the pre-event anxiety loop from which the question emerges, confirming it originates in a state that no pants assessment can resolve; and (5) the outfit was decided before the question was asked in 94.7% of documented cases.

Our central finding, stated plainly: the question is the ritual. The ritual is the point. He was the witness. The pants were never on trial. The verdict was already in. The correct response has nothing to do with pants. The correct response has everything to do with presence, attention, and the specific quality of being genuinely seen before walking out the door. The research team presents this finding with the considered hope that at least some of the men reading it will recognize what they were being asked and what they could have said instead.

§ 1 Introduction: The Question, the Pants, and the Thing That Was Never the Pants

At approximately 7:23pm on a Saturday evening, in a bedroom that is approximately 18 minutes away from the stated departure time, a woman stands before a mirror wearing pants — or a dress, or a skirt, or any garment whose relationship to her silhouette has become, in the past 40 seconds, the subject of acute and specific scrutiny — and asks the person behind her: "Does this make my butt look bigger?"

The person behind her — typically male, typically in a state of operational readiness that he achieved approximately 35 minutes ago and has been maintaining with diminishing patience — processes this as a question. About pants. He evaluates the pants. He formulates an answer about the pants. He delivers the answer about the pants.

This is the mistake. This has always been the mistake. This paper exists to document why it is a mistake, what the correct response would have been, and how we know — with the full weight of six academic disciplines — that the pants were never the subject and the question was never about pants.

The woman changes outfits anyway. She was going to change outfits anyway. She changed into the outfit she had in mind before she asked the question. She looks excellent. She knew she would look excellent. The question was not about whether she would look excellent. The question was about something else entirely, and the research team spent eleven months figuring out what that something else was, and the answer, when it arrived, was both simpler and more important than pants.

§ 2 The Taxonomy of Wrong Answers: A Complete Survey

Before establishing what the question is, the research team documented every available answer within the pants-assessment framework and its outcome. The survey covers 847 documented PERI events across 312 couples, representing the full range of possible responses. Every response was classified. Every outcome was recorded.

The research team presents these findings not with judgment but with the specific sympathy of scientists who have studied a problem long enough to understand why it is hard — and who have concluded, unanimously, that it is hard specifically because the responder is solving the wrong problem.

§ 3 Game Theory: There Is No Nash Equilibrium in Pants

Prof. Abara-Whitfield's formal game theory analysis begins with a definition that reframes the entire problem: the Pre-Event Ritual Inquiry is not a two-player game with symmetric information. It is a game in which the two players have different understandings of what the game is. Player A (the asker) is playing the Pre-Event Presence Request game. Player B (the responder) is playing the Pants Assessment game. These are different games. They share a surface — the question — but they have different objectives, different winning conditions, and different payoff matrices.

In the Pants Assessment game, there is a theoretically correct answer (the accurate description of the pants' effect on the silhouette). The correct answer is achievable. The correct answer does not produce a positive outcome. This is because the correct answer is not what is being sought.

In the Pre-Event Presence Request game, there is also a theoretically correct answer — but it has nothing to do with pants. It requires the respondent to: (1) recognize that a pre-event anxiety state has been signaled; (2) acknowledge it; (3) provide genuine, specific, present-tense attention; and (4) communicate that the asker is seen, valued, and accompanied. None of these steps involve pants assessment.

Nash Equilibrium (Pants Game) = ∅
Nash Equilibrium (Presence Game) = {genuine attention, specific acknowledgment}
The Pants Assessment game has no Nash equilibrium — no response that produces a positive outcome for the responder within that frame. The Pre-Event Presence Request game has a clear Nash equilibrium, but it is only accessible to a player who knows they are playing the Presence game rather than the Pants game. The transition between these games is the entire paper.

3.1   What the Correct Response Actually Is

The research team, having established that no correct pants-related answer exists, turned to the question of what response does produce a positive outcome. The data from 847 PERI events includes 54 cases (6.4%) in which the respondent produced what the asker, in post-event interview, described as "the right response." These 54 cases were analyzed for common structure.

The correct responses shared the following features: they did not assess the pants. They assessed the person. They were specific rather than general ("you look like yourself, which is exactly right" rather than "you look great"). They communicated presence — that the respondent had been paying attention before the question was asked, not scrambling to produce an assessment after it. And they acknowledged, in some form, the thing underneath the question: I see you. I am here. You are ready. Let's go.

"The right answer was never about the pants. It was about me. Whether he was paying attention to me, not the pants. Whether he was present with me before the event, not just waiting for me to be ready. The question was: are you here? The pants were just how I asked it." — Subject F-58, post-event interview, documenting the 1 correct response in her 23 documented PERI events

§ 4 Philosophy of Language: The Question Is Not a Question

Prof. Ravenswood-Clarke's analysis begins with J.L. Austin's foundational distinction between constative and performative utterances. A constative utterance makes a claim about the world that can be true or false: "The cat is on the mat." A performative utterance does something — it enacts a state of affairs rather than describing one: "I now pronounce you married." Performatives are not true or false. They succeed or fail. They are judged not by their accuracy but by their effect.

"Does this make my butt look bigger?" is, Prof. Ravenswood-Clarke argues, a performative disguised as a constative. It looks like a request for information. It is, in its actual function, a ritual act — specifically, a Pre-Departure Witness Request (PDWR): a bid for the respondent to become actively, genuinely present in the moment before the subject enters a social environment.

The question is not asking: "Provide an assessment of how this garment interacts with my silhouette." The question is performing: "I am preparing to go somewhere that matters to me, I am feeling something about that, and I need you to be here with me for a moment before we go."

§ 5 Information Theory: The Shannon Entropy of "Does This"

Dr. Tanaka-Okonkwo's information theory analysis addresses a foundational question: if the asker already knows what outfit she is wearing, has already decided what she will wear, and will change into that outfit regardless of the answer — what information is the question actually seeking?

H(pants | asker's knowledge) ≈ 0 bits
H(presence | respondent's state) = significant
The Shannon entropy of the pants question, with respect to pants, is approximately zero — the asker has full information about the pants and will receive no new information about the pants from the respondent's answer. But the entropy of the respondent's state — whether he is genuinely present, paying attention, and engaged — is nonzero. The question is not requesting pants information. It is sampling the respondent's attention state. It is a presence check, encoded as a pants question.

This reframing resolves a paradox that the research team spent three weeks unable to explain: why does the asker ask a question whose answer she already knows? The answer: because the answer she already knows is the answer about the pants. The answer she does not yet know — the answer with nonzero entropy — is about the respondent. Whether he is there. Whether he is paying attention. Whether, at this specific transitional moment before walking out the door, she is accompanied or alone.

The pants question is a channel. It carries information in one direction — from the respondent to the asker — but the information it carries is not about pants. It is about presence. A respondent who understands this channel structure can transmit the correct signal. A respondent who treats it as a pants-information channel will transmit the wrong signal at high confidence, which is, Dr. Tanaka-Okonkwo notes, "one of the more poignant communication failures I have formally analyzed."

§ 6 Neuroscience: The Pre-Event Anxiety Loop and the Mirror Cycle

Dr. Venkataraman-Holloway's neuroimaging analysis documents the brain state from which the PERI emerges — a state the research team terms the Pre-Event Anxiety Loop (PEAL): the specific cognitive cycle that activates in the 20–40 minutes before a significant social event, in which the subject oscillates between self-assessment, social anticipation, self-reassurance, and renewed self-assessment, without converging on a stable self-evaluation.

6.1   The Mirror Cycle: A Real-Time Neural Map

The PEAL manifests physically as the mirror cycle — the iterative loop of inspection, adjustment, reinspection, and reappraisal that produces the phenomenon externally visible as "taking a long time to get ready." The mirror cycle is not vanity. It is anxiety with a specific object. The object happens to be the mirror. The anxiety is about the event, the social context, the judgment of others, the quality of showing up right.

Table 2 — Neural Activity During the Pre-Event Anxiety Loop
PhaseBrain ActivityBehavioral Expression
Initial Assessment Medial prefrontal cortex active. Self-referential processing. "How do I look?" First outfit tried. Generally positive. Then: second thoughts.
Social Anticipation Temporoparietal junction activates. Social simulation: imagining how she will appear to others at the event. The first outfit is now evaluated against the anticipated social environment. The first outfit may no longer be optimal. The second outfit is retrieved.
The Mirror Cycle Sustained activation of self-evaluation and social simulation networks in rapid alternation. The brain is doing a lot of work. The mirror is not to blame for this. The mirror is just reflecting. Multiple outfits examined. The outfit decision tree activates. See Section 6.2.
The Question The PEAL has not resolved through internal processing. The brain seeks external input — not about the pants, but to interrupt the loop. The respondent's presence and attention can resolve what the mirror cannot: the feeling of being accompanied. "Does this make my butt look bigger?" The question is the PEAL requesting an external anchor. The anchor is not pants-shaped. The anchor is person-shaped.

6.2   The Outfit Decision Tree

Dr. Venkataraman-Holloway documented the outfit decision tree across 312 subjects. The tree has, on average, 847 branches. No path through the tree produces certain resolution — it produces a decision state that is stable until the next social simulation cycle activates, which is typically within 90 seconds. The tree's size is not irrationality. It is the number of social variables being simultaneously optimized.

Does this look right for the event?
→ Yes → But does it look right for who will be there? → New branch
→ Maybe → Try the other one. The other one creates a new branch.
→ What does "right" mean here exactly? → 47 sub-branches. None converge.
Is this what I want to say about myself tonight?
→ Yes → Is it still yes in the mirror? Check mirror. New branch.
→ Almost → The "almost" creates 23 sub-branches exploring what "completely" would require.
The question is asked. The loop pauses. The respondent answers about pants. The loop resumes.
The respondent says something that is not about pants. The loop stops. She gets ready in four minutes. They leave on time.
** Dr. Venkataraman-Holloway notes a finding she describes as "the most clearly actionable neuroimaging data I have produced in 14 years": the PEAL resolves significantly faster — and produces measurably lower anxiety and higher pre-event wellbeing scores — when the respondent delivers a presence-based rather than pants-based response. The correct response is not merely emotionally preferable. It is neurologically effective. It interrupts the loop at its core mechanism. It does what 847 mirror-check branches cannot: it provides an external confirmation that the internal simulation has been resolved by someone who is paying attention. The pants were, in this analysis, the key that opens the door. The room behind the door is not about pants.

§ 7 Anthropology: Every Culture Has This Question

Dr. Asante-Morrison's cross-cultural survey examined pre-departure ritual communication in 29 societies, specifically focusing on verbal exchanges between partners in the 30-minute window before significant social events. His finding is consistent with his previous cross-cultural work and consistent with what is becoming, across this research team's portfolio, a recurring theme in human behavior: it is universal.

Every culture surveyed has a version of the Pre-Event Ritual Inquiry. In 29 of 29 cultures, one partner asks the other for pre-departure assessment. In 29 of 29 cultures, the question is phrased in terms of appearance. In 29 of 29 cultures, the respondent answers in terms of appearance. In 29 of 29 cultures, the respondent's appearance-based answer fails to fully resolve the exchange.

The cultural variation is in the surface form — the specific garment named, the specific attribute questioned, the precise phrasing of the inquiry. The deep structure is identical. The question is a pre-departure witness request. The respondent misreads it as an appearance query. The exchange fails to achieve its intended function. This happens in every culture. In every language. At every income level. Before every kind of social event. Without exception.

"The question is one of the most cross-culturally consistent communicative behaviors I have documented. It is asking, in the language of appearance, for the experience of being accompanied. The answer, in the language of appearance, cannot provide that experience. The gap between what is asked and what is answered is, in all 29 cultures, the same gap. It is a gap that a different kind of answer — not harder, not longer, just differently aimed — would close entirely." — Dr. Asante-Morrison, field notes, February 2024

§ 8 Selected Case Studies

Subject F-55, age 55, asked the question at 7:23pm before a dinner party. The pants in question were dark, well-fitting, and — per researcher assessment — entirely unremarkable in the way that excellent pants are unremarkable. Subject M-58, her partner, looked at the pants for approximately two seconds and said "they look fine." Subject F-55 said "okay" and turned back to the mirror. Subject M-58 interpreted this as the successful conclusion of a pants assessment. It was not.

Subject F-55 changed into a dress she had already laid out on the bed before the pants had been tried on.

In the post-event interview, Subject F-55 was asked what she had actually wanted when she asked the question. She thought about this for a moment. Then she said: "I wanted him to look at me. Not the pants. Me. I was nervous about the dinner and I wanted him to be there with me for a second before we left." She paused. "He looked at the pants."

Subject M-58 was interviewed separately. He was asked what he thought had happened during the pants question. He said: "I told her they looked fine." He was asked if he thought that had been the right answer. He said, after a pause: "I'm guessing not, or I wouldn't be in this study."

Subject M-58 is correct that he is in this study for a reason. Subject M-58's answer — "they look fine" — was delivered sincerely, efficiently, and with genuine belief that he had successfully completed the requested task. He had not completed the requested task. He had completed a different task. The task he completed was fine. The task that was requested went uncompleted. The dress was waiting on the bed. It was always waiting on the bed.

Subject M-47, age 47, was asked the question at 6:47pm before a work gala. He had been in the study for three months. He had read nothing. He had been told nothing. He simply looked at Subject F-44 — not at her outfit, at her — and said: "You look like you're about to walk into a room and own it. I've been watching you get ready and you've been beautiful for the past hour, I just didn't say anything. You're ready."

Subject F-44 did not change outfits.

She was interviewed afterward about this exchange. She said: "He saw me. He wasn't assessing the dress. He was just... there. With me. That's all I needed." She was asked whether the dress looked good. She said: "I have no idea. I stopped thinking about the dress."

Subject M-47 was asked what had made him respond that way. He said: "I could tell she wasn't really asking about the dress. She had that look." When asked what look, he said: "Like she needed me to pay attention. So I paid attention."

Subject M-47 is the only subject in this study who identified the actual question on first attempt without coaching or prior knowledge. The research team has spent three months trying to understand how he did this. The answer, per his own account, is that he looked at her rather than at the dress. The dress was what was mentioned. She was what was there. He looked at what was there. This is, the research team has concluded, the entire paper in one case study.

§ 9 Conclusions: The Pants Were Never On Trial

We began this paper with a question about pants. We have spent nine sections demonstrating that it was never about pants. The pants are fine. The pants were always fine. She knew the pants were fine before she asked, and she was going to wear the dress anyway, and the dress was already on the bed, and the question was not about what was on the bed or on her body.

The question was about what was in the room. Whether he was in the room — genuinely, attentively, present-tense in the room — or whether he was somewhere else, waiting for her to be ready, assessing garments when asked, providing correct pants evaluations with the confidence of a man who has been asked to do a job and has done the job and does not understand why the job did not land.

The job was not the pants. The job was presence. The job was specific attention delivered at a specific transitional moment. The job was, in its simplest form: look at her, not the pants, and say something that only someone who has been paying attention could say.

This is not harder than assessing pants. It is differently aimed. The aim is the whole thing. The aim has always been the whole thing. The dress was on the bed. The witness was in the room. The question was whether the witness was present enough to know what he was witnessing.

In 6.4% of documented cases, he was. In those cases, the question resolved. The loop stopped. They left on time. The research team is rooting for the other 93.6%. The research team believes the number will improve. The research team has published this paper as a contribution to that improvement.
