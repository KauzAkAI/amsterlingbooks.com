You Were Sincere. The Couch Was Also There. Both Things Were True.

This paper presents the first formal multi-disciplinary analysis of the "I'm Almost There" Statement (IATS): a pre-arrival communication delivered with confidence by a person who has not departed, frequently has not located their keys, is not wearing shoes, and will not arrive for a period of time that bears no mathematical relationship to the word "almost." The statement is communicated via phone, text, or app and is received by a waiting party who, in 94% of documented cases, already knows the statement is not geographically accurate.

The IATS is not a lie in the conventional sense. Information theory confirms it contains essentially zero spatial data. Philosophy of language classifies it as a performative rather than a constative — it is not describing a location but managing a relationship. Neuroscience documents that the speaker genuinely believes they are almost there, because the brain under mild time pressure compresses anticipated travel time by 34–58%. The game theory is unambiguous: "I haven't left yet" is never the dominant strategy. "Almost there" always is.

Our central finding: both parties understand the statement is not a location report. Both parties complete the exchange as if it were useful. The relationship is maintained. The waiting continues. The arrival eventually occurs. The statement worked exactly as it was designed to work, which was never to transmit geographic data and always to communicate: I know you are waiting. I take this seriously. I am coming. Please continue waiting.

§ 1 Introduction: The Statement and Its Conditions

At approximately 7:43pm on a Saturday evening, Subject M-67 receives a text from Subject F-55 reading: "Running a little behind, leaving now — almost there!" Subject M-67 has been waiting at the restaurant since 7:30pm. He reads the text. He looks at his watch. He orders a drink. He says "okay."

Subject F-55 is not almost there. Subject F-55 is in her bathroom. Subject F-55 has not yet located her second earring. Subject F-55's shoes are in the closet. Subject F-55's drive to the restaurant takes 19 minutes under ideal conditions. Subject F-55 will arrive at approximately 8:11pm — 28 minutes after Subject M-67 received the message that she was almost there.

Subject M-67 knows this. He knew it when he read the text. He said "okay" anyway. The exchange was complete. Both parties proceeded. The evening was, ultimately, excellent.

This paper asks what happened in those three words — I'm almost there — and finds that what happened had nothing to do with location and everything to do with the specific, efficient, cross-culturally universal act of communicating care across a geographic gap.

§ 3 Game Theory: The Dominant Strategy

Prof. Abara-Whitfield's game theory analysis of pre-arrival communication begins with a payoff matrix that he describes as "the most one-sided I have analyzed since the pants paper, and for similar reasons — there is only one viable strategy and everyone already knows what it is."

Table 1 — Pre-Arrival Communication Strategies and Their Outcomes
StrategyWhat It TransmitsReceiver ResponseOutcome
"I'm almost there!" Zero geographic data. Maximum relational warmth. Implicit commitment to arrival. Positive emotional tone. "Okay! See you soon." Waiting continues. Drink ordered. Phone put away. Patience maintained. Optimal. Relationship intact. Waiting tolerable. Arrival eventually resolves ambiguity. Evening proceeds.
"I haven't left yet." Accurate geographic data. Negative emotional valence. Uncertainty about timeline. Possible concern about commitment. Variable but skewing negative: "Okay..." (with ellipsis doing significant work), silence, or a follow-up question that requires another explanation. Accurate. Also initiates a friction event that the eventual arrival will need to repair. The evening can recover but must recover. Recovery takes energy. Energy spent on recovery is energy not spent on the evening.
"I'll be there in about 25 minutes." Accurate geographic data. Neutral tone. Full information. Rational processing. Receiver adjusts expectations. Waiting recalibrated. Technically optimal for information transfer. Rarely deployed. The research team notes that "25 minutes" produces a specific and somewhat long wait that "almost there" converts into an indefinite but apparently short wait. Indefinite-short feels better than definite-long. The brain prefers indefinite-short. "Almost there" delivers indefinite-short at no accuracy cost because accuracy was never the point.
"Running a bit late, so sorry!" Admission of tardiness. Apology. No location data. Negative self-assessment. Sympathy response required. Receiver must now manage sender's guilt in addition to their own wait. Double burden. Well-intentioned. Creates more work for the receiver than "almost there" does. The receiver preferred "almost there." Nobody told the sender this. This paper is telling them.
Dominant Strategy: "I'm almost there." It outperforms all alternatives on relational outcome while being the least accurate option available. The research team is noticing a pattern.

"Almost there" is cheap talk that both parties have agreed, implicitly and universally, to treat as meaningful. The agreement makes it meaningful. The meaning is not "I am near" — the meaning is "I am coming and I know you are waiting." This meaning is true. It is transmitted accurately. The location data is false. The relational data is true. § 4 Information Theory: What "Almost There" Actually Contains

Dr. Tanaka-Okonkwo's analysis proceeds from a formal question: what information does the IATS actually transmit? She decomposes the statement into its geographic content and its relational content and measures each independently.

H(location | "almost there") ≈ maximum bits
H(intent | "almost there") ≈ 0 bits
Geographic entropy: maximum. The receiver cannot update their probability estimate of the sender's location. "Almost there" is consistent with being in the car, in the parking lot, at home on the couch, or in another country. It eliminates no possibilities.

Intent entropy: near zero. The receiver knows with certainty that the sender intends to arrive, is aware of the wait, and wishes to communicate this awareness. This information is fully transmitted. The statement achieves its actual communicative purpose with perfect efficiency.

Dr. Tanaka-Okonkwo notes that the statement is, informationally speaking, an extraordinary achievement: it transmits zero useful data about the question it appears to answer (where are you?) while transmitting complete information about the question it actually answers (are you coming and do you know I'm waiting?). The surface content fails completely. The actual content succeeds completely.

She further notes that the receiver's "okay" response performs a similar function: it transmits zero information about whether the receiver believes the statement while transmitting complete information about whether the receiver accepts the relational communication. "Okay" means: I received your care. I acknowledge it. The wait continues. The relationship is intact. See you soon. This is not a small amount of information to transmit in four letters. This is, in relational terms, everything that needed to be said.

§ 5 Philosophy of Language: A Performative Disguised as a Location Report

Prof. Ravenswood-Clarke's analysis classifies the IATS, per Austin's speech act theory, as a performative — a statement that does something rather than describing something. "I'm almost there" does not report a location. It performs a relational act: the acknowledgment of the other person's wait, the affirmation of the relationship, and the renewal of the commitment to arrival.

This analysis explains a finding that pure information theory cannot: why the statement feels satisfying to receive even when the receiver knows it's not geographically accurate. A failed location report would be useless or annoying. The IATS is neither. It is, per post-event interviews, experienced as reassuring — even by receivers who know the sender has not left.

The reason: the receiver was never waiting for location data. The receiver was waiting for connection — for evidence that the sender knows they are waiting, cares about the wait, and is coming. "I'm almost there" delivers all three with warmth, brevity, and a mild upbeat energy that "I haven't left yet" categorically cannot match. The performative succeeds where the location report would fail because the need it meets is relational, not geographic.

"The statement is a verbal hug delivered across a 23-minute distance. It is not a GPS coordinate. Nobody needed a GPS coordinate. They needed to know someone was coming." — Prof. Ravenswood-Clarke, first draft, unedited. The research team kept it verbatim. It is the whole section.

§ 6 Neuroscience: Why the Speaker Believes It

The finding that distinguishes the IATS from a conventional lie is this: the speaker, in the overwhelming majority of documented cases, genuinely believes they are almost there. Dr. Venkataraman-Holloway's neuroimaging and behavioral data confirm this. The optimism bias is not performed. The temporal compression is real.

Under mild time pressure — the awareness that someone is waiting, that one is already late, that one should be in motion — the brain activates the optimism bias (the tendency to overestimate positive outcomes and underestimate the time required to achieve them) and temporal compression (the perceptual narrowing of anticipated future intervals). A 23-minute drive feels, under these conditions, like it should take 8 minutes. Not because the speaker is irrational. Because the brain, under time pressure, is producing a motivated estimate rather than an accurate one.

Table 2 — Temporal Compression by Motivational State
Motivational StateActual Drive TimePerceived Drive TimeCompression
No time pressure, going anywhere 23 min ~21 min 9% — near-accurate. The brain is not invested in a particular outcome.
Mild lateness, someone waiting 23 min ~15 min 35% — significant. The brain wants the drive to be short. It estimates accordingly.
Moderate lateness, important occasion 23 min ~10 min 57% — severe. The 23-minute drive feels like "just a few minutes." "Almost there" is the honest expression of this feeling.
Very late, highly motivated 23 min ~7 min 70% — the speaker believes, with genuine conviction, that they are almost there. They are on their couch. They have not found their keys. They are almost there.
Note: Dr. Venkataraman-Holloway confirms that the temporal compression produces a genuine subjective experience of nearness. The speaker saying "almost there" is not being dishonest about their location. They are being honest about their experience of time. Their experience of time is wrong. It is wrong in a documented, neurologically consistent, cross-culturally universal way. The keys are still in the coat pocket. The drive is still 23 minutes. The brain has compressed both facts out of existence. The statement is sincere.

§ 7 Selected Case Studies

Subject F-55 sent "Almost there!" at 7:29pm. At the time of sending she was: in her bathroom (second earring unlocated), not wearing shoes, in possession of her phone but not her keys, and 19 minutes of driving plus approximately 5 minutes of pre-departure activity away from arrival. Actual arrival: 7:53pm. Gap between statement and arrival: 24 minutes.

Subject M-67 received the statement, said "okay," ordered a drink, and did not mention the 24-minute gap upon arrival. Subject F-55 did not mention it either. The evening proceeded. When asked, in a follow-up interview, whether she had believed she was almost there when she sent the text, Subject F-55 paused and then said: "I mean... I was getting ready to leave." This answer does not contain the word "almost" or the word "there." The research team received it as confirmation of the neurological analysis.

Subject M-67 was asked what "almost there" meant to him when he received it. He said: "It meant she was coming and she knew I was waiting." He was not asked if he thought she had left yet. He was not asked this question because the research team already knew the answer and felt that the evening had gone well and some things should be left as they are.

Dr. Asante-Morrison documents the functional equivalent of the IATS in a culture with no direct translation for "almost." The phrase used — rendered approximately as "I am on my way to being near you soon" — contains no temporal or geographic precision. It is used exclusively in pre-arrival communication. It is understood by all receivers to mean the sender is coming and is aware of the wait. Receivers respond with the cultural equivalent of "okay." Both parties proceed.

Dr. Asante-Morrison notes: "I have studied 31 cultures. Every one has this. Every one understands that the statement is not a location report. Every one accepts it anyway. It is the most universally understood false statement in human communication. It works in every culture without translation because it is not saying what it appears to be saying. What it is saying translates perfectly. What it appears to be saying does not need to."

§ 8 Conclusions: The Statement Was Never About Location

We set out to analyze a statement made by a person who had not left their house. We found that the statement, examined under six independent disciplines, is doing something entirely different from what it appears to be doing — and doing it perfectly. It is not reporting a location. It never reported a location. The location was never the information. The care was the information. The arrival was the information. The commitment, transmitted across a 23-minute gap, was the information.

The keys were in the coat pocket. The drive was 23 minutes. The earring was in the vanity drawer. These facts coexisted with "almost there" in complete harmony because they were never in the same category as "almost there." "Almost there" was in the category of things people say to each other to keep the thread of connection intact while the material world — its coats, its earring drawers, its traffic — briefly intervenes.

The dominant strategy is clear. The neuroscience is sympathetic. The speech act is understood. The receiver says "okay." The arrival happens. The evening is excellent.

It is not a GPS coordinate.
Nobody needed a GPS coordinate.
They needed to know someone was coming."
