Q1: Which parent is available?
→ Soft Parent (M-67): proceed to Q3. Favorable starting condition.

§ 3 The 23-Point Gap: How Subject F-4 Found It

The gap between the Soft Parent's compliance threshold (84%) and the Firm Parent's compliance threshold (61%) is 23 percentage points. This is the most productive single asset in Subject F-4's strategic portfolio. She did not know it was there on her third birthday. By her fourth birthday it was operational.

Dr. Blackwell-Tanaka documents the developmental timeline of gap discovery. The comparison produced the observation: same request, different outcomes depending on who was asked. The observation was tested. The test was replicated. The replication confirmed: the Soft Parent is more likely to say yes. The gap was now operational.

§ 5 The Request with Eyes: A Neuroscience Report

Dr. Venkataraman-Holloway's analysis of the Request with Eyes is the most technically precise section of this paper. The Request with Eyes is not a manipulation in the pejorative sense. It is the deployment of a socially evolved signal — the infantile gaze — that activates the caregiving system in adult humans with the specific efficiency of a key that was shaped by evolution to fit a particular lock. Subject F-4 did not design the key. Subject F-4 discovered that she had it and learned when to use it.

The infantile gaze — upward angle, widened pupils, slight forward head tilt — activates the nucleus accumbens and ventral tegmental area in adult observers, producing dopamine and oxytocin release. The oxytocin specifically reduces the prefrontal cortex's inhibitory function — the function responsible for "no, it's almost dinner." With reduced prefrontal inhibition, the caregiving motivation (give her what she needs) becomes proportionally stronger relative to the regulatory motivation (maintain the pre-dinner cracker policy). The crackers are provided.

Subject F-4 deploys the Request with Eyes at T+3.1 seconds into a request sequence. Dr. Venkataraman-Holloway's analysis confirms this timing is neurologically optimal: at T+3 seconds, the parent's prefrontal cortex has fully loaded the request and is in the process of evaluating it, but has not yet generated a final decision. The oxytocin release from the gaze intervenes in the decision-formation process rather than the decision-execution process, which is more effective. Subject F-4 found this timing through trial and error over approximately four months. Her timing is within 0.1 seconds of the neurologically optimal moment. She is four years old.

‡ Dr. Venkataraman-Holloway notes the research team's most significant finding in this section: Subject F-4's Request with Eyes is not deployed on every request. It is deployed selectively, on requests where the stakes are higher (the good crackers, the third story) and withheld on routine requests (a drink of water, help with a zipper). The selective deployment serves two functions: it preserves the signal's novelty and therefore its effectiveness, and it avoids habituating the parents to the gaze, which would reduce the caregiving activation response over time. Subject F-4 has solved the economic problem of scarce signal value through correct rationing. The research team wishes to emphasize: she is four years old.

§ 8 Conclusions: She Already Knows

Subject F-4 has, by the age of four, developed a complete operational strategy for navigating a household with two parents who have different compliance thresholds, different emotional states across the day, and an imperfect information-sharing system that she can exploit by sequencing her requests correctly. She did not read a game theory textbook. She did not take a class. She discovered the relevant principles through a program of empirical testing that she conducted over approximately eight months, from roughly age 3.5 to age 4.3, and which produced the stable strategic repertoire documented in this paper.

The research team offers three observations for the parents.

First: the gap between your compliance thresholds is known to her. She has known it for approximately fourteen months. The solution is not to eliminate the gap — parents having different thresholds is normal and arguably adaptive — but to share information about individual compliance decisions in real time, removing the information asymmetry that makes the sequential ask and the "daddy said yes" strategies viable. This will not eliminate cracker acquisition. It will move cracker acquisition into a regime where both parents are making an informed joint decision, which is a better regime. Subject F-4 will adapt. She will adapt within approximately three weeks. The research team looks forward to the follow-up study.

Second: the Request with Eyes is not a manipulation. It is a signal. It is saying: I need something and I want you to know I need it. The fact that it activates your caregiving system is not an exploit of a vulnerability — it is the signal working exactly as it was designed by evolution to work. The appropriate response to a four-year-old using the Request with Eyes is to evaluate the request on its merits and decide accordingly. This is what both parents are doing. Subject F-4's 91% success rate reflects that her requests are, in the main, reasonable. She is asking for crackers, stories, and seven minutes of the Soft Parent lying down. These are things a four-year-old should receive.

Third: the love at 7:34pm and the cracker optimization at 3:47pm are not in conflict. Subject F-4 is four years old. She is learning how the world works by testing it. The testing and the love are happening simultaneously, on separate tracks, in the same small person who got the orange crackers and held your hand for seven minutes and asked why the sky is blue and has approximately fifteen more years of questions before she goes somewhere else and asks them there. The crackers are good crackers. Answer the questions. Lie down for seven minutes. She already knows. She has always known.
