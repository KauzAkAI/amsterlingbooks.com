You Are on the Table. You Have Always Been on the Table. You Did Not Choose This.

This paper presents the first comprehensive multi-disciplinary analysis of the Domestic Volume Conflict (DVC) — the continuous, low-grade acoustic dispute present in 88.4% of cohabitating couples who share a television, in which one party (the Low Operator) prefers a volume of approximately 19 and the other party (the High Operator) prefers a volume of approximately 47, producing a 28-point gap that is physiologically grounded, practically irresolvable at a single volume setting, and continuously managed via 4,847 mean annual remote control volume adjustments — of which 91.3% constitute a counter-adjustment of the partner's previous change.

The DVC is the acoustic analog of the Domestic Thermostat Conflict (Sterling et al., Paper 21, this series). Both conflicts feature bilateral physiological correctness — both parties' preferences are grounded in genuine sensory experience — and both generate a continuous low-grade adjustment-counter-adjustment cycle that resolves nothing and continues indefinitely. The DVC has one advantage over the thermostat conflict: it has a hardware solution that satisfies both parties simultaneously. The solution is subtitles. It took a median of 2.4 years of cohabitation for study couples to discover it.

The Subtitle Accords — the implicit bilateral agreement to enable subtitles and set volume at a mutually tolerable compromise — achieve a 74% stability rating in the study, the highest of any volume conflict resolution strategy. Seventy-one point two percent of couples who adopted the Subtitle Accords continue to watch with subtitles enabled even when watching alone, and 61% now report that they prefer subtitles regardless of volume. The treaty solved the conflict. The treaty also changed how people watch television. The treaty did not intend to do this. It did it anyway.

§ 1 Introduction: 19, 47, and the 28 Points Between Them

The television is on. Subject F-55 reaches for the remote and sets the volume to 19. The dialogue is clear. The ambient sound is at a comfortable level. The room feels like a room in which a normal person is watching television. Subject M-67, ten minutes later, reaches for the remote and sets the volume to 47. The dialogue is now effortless. The action sequences are present. The show is audible as television is meant to be audible. Subject F-55 looks at the remote.

This exchange, or a variation of it, has occurred 4,847 times in the study household in the past year. In 91.3% of cases, one party's adjustment was a direct counter-adjustment of the other party's previous change. In 34% of cases, the two adjustments produced a net change of zero — the volume ending where it started after both parties had touched it. The volume conflict is, by this measure, structurally identical to the thermostat conflict documented in Paper 21 of this series. The thermostat conflict had no hardware solution. The volume conflict does.

§ 3 The Physiology: Why Both Parties Are Right About 19 and 47

As with the thermostat conflict, the volume conflict is not a dispute about preferences — it is a dispute between physiological realities. Dr. Halvorsen-Nakamura and Dr. Venkataraman-Holloway's joint analysis documents the auditory basis of the gap.

She genuinely hears more at lower volumes. Lower in the study range. Age-related and sex-linked auditory changes reduce high-frequency sensitivity. Discomfort threshold Reached at approximately volume 32–35 in sustained exposure. Above this, the amygdala activates a mild stress response. Volume 31–34 for clear dialogue without concentration. Below 30, the High Operator is actively working to parse dialogue — effortful, suboptimal, subtly stressful in a different direction than the Low Operator's stress response at high volumes.
What each party experiences at the other's preferred volume Volume 47: genuine auditory discomfort, stress response activation, reduced enjoyment of the content. This is not a preference. This is a physiological response. Volume 19: genuine dialogue strain, effortful parsing, reduced content comprehension. He is not being dramatic. Both parties are physiologically correct. Neither party is being unreasonable. The gap is real, it is measurable, and it cannot be closed by a single volume setting that satisfies both parties simultaneously. This is why subtitles are not a compromise. They are a solution. They address the High Operator's intelligibility problem (reading) without requiring the Low Operator's volume to increase. The solution was always available. It waited 91 minutes on a Tuesday.

§ 4 The Remote Control: A Study in Physical Resource Management

Prof. Abara-Whitfield's analysis of the remote control documents it as the key physical asset in the volume conflict — the object whose possession determines who can unilaterally adjust the contested variable. The remote is, in the DVC, what the thermostat dial is in the DTC: whoever touches it last has set the current state.

The remote's location — "on the table" — is its neutral-state position. From the table, it is equally accessible to both parties. Neither party has control when the remote is on the table. The remote on the table is therefore simultaneously a symbol of the conflict's pause and a symbol of its continuation: the pause will end when either party reaches for it, which either party can do at any time.

The research team tracked the remote's location across the Tuesday evening session. It moved nine times. Its net displacement from its 8:00pm starting position was zero — it ended on the table where it began. Its total travel distance across the evening was approximately 4.2 feet of back-and-forth between the table, her hand, and his hand.

After 9:31pm — when subtitles were enabled — the remote was not touched for 29 minutes. Its location: the table. Its significance at that moment: none. The volume had been set at 22. The subtitles were handling the dialogue. Neither party needed the remote for anything. The remote on the table, untouched for 29 minutes, is the closest thing to a peace symbol in this paper.

Prof. Abara-Whitfield notes: "The remote's 29-minute idle period is the longest in the study dataset for this household. Pre-Subtitle Accords, the mean interval between remote touches during an evening session was 8.4 minutes. Post-accords, the mean interval is 41.2 minutes. The remote is, empirically, least used when least needed. The least-needed condition is: subtitles on, volume at 22, both parties watching. The most-needed condition was 91 minutes earlier. Both parties discovered the remote was most useful when it was put down."

The remote is most contested when it is most needed. It is most needed when the volume is furthest from one party's preference. The subtitle solution reduces both parties' need for the remote, which reduces remote contestation, which reduces conflict. The solution works by making the remote irrelevant rather than by determining who has the right to hold it. This is the most sustainable conflict resolution mechanism in the study.

§ 5 The Subtitle Accords: How a Peace Treaty Became a Preference

The Subtitle Accords began as a practical solution to a specific problem. One party could not hear the television comfortably at the other party's preferred volume. The other party could not hear the television adequately at the first party's preferred volume. Subtitles, enabled at a compromise volume of 22, resolved both problems simultaneously: he could read what he could not fully hear; she was not experiencing auditory discomfort. The solution was available from the day they first owned a television with subtitle capability. It was adopted a median of 2.4 years after cohabitation began.

The Subtitle Accords' 74% stability rating is the highest in the study — higher than all volume compromise strategies, higher than separate listening devices, higher than the "your turn to choose the volume" alternating system. The accords are stable because they do not require either party to sacrifice anything. She watches at 22, which is above her preference but below her discomfort threshold. He reads the subtitles, which he initially resisted and now does automatically, noting that he misses fewer plot details than he did before.

The finding that 71.2% of Subtitle Accords couples now watch with subtitles when alone — without any acoustic need for them — represents the most interesting secondary finding in the study. The subtitle, adopted as a conflict resolution tool, became a viewing preference. The treaty changed the behavior it was designed to regulate and produced a new behavior it was not designed to produce at all.

I.
The television shall be set to volume 22 as the default session starting point.
22 is above her comfort optimum (19) and below his intelligibility floor (31). Both parties are slightly suboptimally served. Both parties are significantly better served than at each other's preference.
II.
Subtitles shall be enabled by default at the session start, before either party has occasion to reach for the remote.
Pre-enabling subtitles removes the intelligibility complaint that drives the High Operator to raise the volume. It also removes the need for the Low Operator to lower it in response. The remote rests.
III.
Either party may make minor adjustments (±3 from treaty volume) for content-specific reasons — loud action sequences, quiet dialogue scenes — without triggering counter-adjustment rights.
Minor adjustments within the agreed range are not conflict moves. They are content responsiveness. The counter-adjustment right is reserved for settings outside the ±3 range. This clause was implicit until codified here.
IV.
The remote shall be placed on the table — accessible to both parties — at the session start and after any adjustment.
Neither party holds the remote between uses. Holding the remote between uses signals anticipatory adjustment, which signals that the current setting is not accepted. Table placement signals acceptance. The table is the treaty's physical symbol.
V.
Cross-reference: The Subtitle Accords represent one arm of the broader bilateral compromise framework documented in Paper 21 (The Thermostat War) of this series, where it appears as the defining feature of the Treaty of Subtitles. The subtitle is simultaneously a volume solution and a temperature solution — the couples who watch with subtitles also tend to have stable thermostat arrangements. The subtitle is the flag of the general peace.
This cross-series finding was not anticipated. It has been documented. Its full implications are under study in a separate paper.

§ 6 Watching Alone with Subtitles: The Treaty's Unexpected Legacy

The finding that 71.2% of Subtitle Accords couples watch with subtitles when alone requires explanation. The conflict that produced the subtitle adoption is not present when watching alone — neither party is there to disagree about volume. The subtitle, adopted for practical bilateral reasons, should have no function when the bilateral situation is absent. It persists anyway.

Dr. Venkataraman-Holloway's analysis of the solo-viewing subtitle behavior identifies three independent reasons for its persistence once adopted:

Table 3 — Why Subtitles Persist in Solo Viewing: Three Mechanisms
MechanismDescriptionPrevalence in Study
Comprehension enhancement Subtitles improve dialogue comprehension independently of volume. They catch mumbled lines, name spellings, plot details delivered quickly. The High Operator began using subtitles to compensate for volume and discovered they improved his understanding of the content. He continues for this reason. Cited by 84% of High Operator solo-subtitle users. He did not know what he was missing. The subtitle showed him what he was missing. He now watches with subtitles to stop missing it.
Ambient noise management Subtitles allow viewing in environments with background noise — other people in the house, traffic, cooking — without raising the volume to compensate. The Low Operator adopted subtitles as a couple's tool and found it also allowed her to watch at her preferred volume without strain when someone else was making noise in another room. Cited by 71% of Low Operator solo-subtitle users. The solo use case was an indirect benefit of the couple's solution. It transferred.
Habit formation Subtitles, enabled by default in every joint viewing session, became the visual default for television. The brain began reading them automatically. Watching without them began to feel like watching with something missing — the absence of the expected text overlay was now a small but real deficit. Cited by 61% of all solo-subtitle users. Both operators. The habit was formed by the treaty. The treaty was for two. The habit is now individual. The subtitle escaped the negotiation that created it.
The research team notes that the Subtitle Accords produced a behavioral change that outlasted the conflict that produced them. The conflict was the occasion for the discovery. The discovery was that subtitles are independently useful, which is a fact that predated the conflict by decades and was available to both parties from the first day they owned a subtitle-capable television. The conflict was the path to the discovery. The discovery is now permanent. Neither party turns off the subtitles anymore. The remote is on the table.

§ 7 Conclusions: 22, Subtitles, Remote on the Table

The Domestic Volume Conflict is not about volume. It is about two people with genuinely different auditory physiologies sharing a single audio output device that can only be set to one level at a time. The conflict is structurally irresolvable at a single volume setting and structurally resolvable the moment the audio channel is decoupled from the dialogue intelligibility requirement — which is what subtitles do.

The subtitle solution has a 74% stability rating because it genuinely addresses both parties' underlying needs rather than splitting the difference between their preferred settings. A compromise at volume 28 leaves both parties somewhat underserved. Volume 22 with subtitles leaves both parties adequately served — she is not in auditory discomfort, he is not straining to hear dialogue — at the cost of reading text on a screen, which both parties now prefer anyway.

The deeper finding is the one nobody expected: the treaty changed the people who signed it. 71.2% of couples who adopted the Subtitle Accords as a conflict resolution tool now watch with subtitles alone, for reasons unrelated to the original conflict. The conflict produced a discovery. The discovery became a habit. The habit became a preference. The preference will outlast the conflict by decades. The remote is on the table. Nobody is reaching for it. The show is good. They are watching it together at volume 22. The subtitles are on.
