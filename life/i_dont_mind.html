"I Don't Mind"
On the Dominant Strategy of False Neutrality and the Restaurant That Was Always There

A Game-Theoretic Analysis of Preference Concealment, Veto Cascades, and the 38.4-Minute Revelation Cycle in Coupled Dining Decisions
Dr. Adrian Maximus Sterling¹  ·  Dr. Priya Mehendrale²  ·  Prof. Desmond Okafor³
¹ Department of Relational Economics, Sterling Institute for Couples Research  |  ² Game Theory & Domestic Strategy, University of Mumbai  |  ³ Applied Decision Sciences, Lagos Graduate School of Management
Received: September 3, 2025  ·  Accepted: October 11, 2025  ·  Published Online: October 29, 2025
DOI: 10.1093/hjadgt.2025.083117  |  Open Access
Abstract This study investigates a globally documented phenomenon in coupled decision-making: the deployment of apparent preference neutrality ("I don't mind") as a dominant strategy in restaurant selection, with subsequent systematic veto of all offered alternatives until a preferred option, held constant since the moment of inquiry, is proposed or reluctantly self-revealed. In a longitudinal study of 1,847 couples across 22 nations, we document a mean veto-before-revelation count of 7.3 (SD = 2.1), a mean time-to-resolution of 38.4 minutes (SD = 9.7), and a Preference Concealment Rate of 94%: the proportion of participants who deployed "I don't mind" while harboring a specific, named preference at the moment of utterance. We introduce the Veto Log Protocol, the False Neutrality Index (FNI), and the Revelation Threshold Model. Theoretical modeling under Nash equilibrium confirms that false neutrality is the dominant strategy under current relational incentive structures. The restaurant was always there. It has always been there. It will continue to be there. Just say the restaurant.
Keywords: game theory, preference concealment, veto cascade, domestic decision science, Nash equilibrium, applied couples research, dining behavior, false neutrality index

1. Introduction

Every evening, across six continents, in kitchens and cars and living rooms lit blue by television screens, a ritual unfolds with the regularity of a tidal cycle. One partner turns to the other and asks: Where do you want to eat? And from the other comes a response so universal it has achieved the status of cultural artifact: I don't mind.

The response is false. It has always been false. The person who said it minded from the beginning. They minded when the question was still being formed. They are currently minding. In 94% of documented cases, they had a specific restaurant — often a specific dish at a specific restaurant — in their immediate cognitive foreground at the precise moment they said "I don't mind."1

What follows is not consensus-seeking. It is a veto process with a predetermined terminal condition. The offering partner will suggest locations. The receiving partner will decline or redirect each one — not from genuine indifference, but from an incentive structure that makes explicit preference declaration suboptimal under current relational norms. This cycle will continue for a mean of 38.4 minutes before the preferred restaurant either emerges naturally from the suggestion space or is finally, reluctantly named.

The Greek place on Maple Street was the answer. The Greek place on Maple Street was always the answer. It was the answer before the question was asked.

2. Literature Review

2.1 The Preference Concealment Tradition

Schelling (1960) identified coordination games as contexts in which agents pursue private information advantages at the cost of collective efficiency.2 Von Neumann and Morgenstern (1944) established formal frameworks for strategic preference revelation under uncertainty.3 Neither, however, anticipated that these dynamics would be applied most vigorously to the question of whether to eat Thai or Italian on a Wednesday.

Subsequent work in behavioral economics identified what Kahneman (2011) termed "preference scaffolding" — the construction of apparent neutrality as a mechanism for offloading decision responsibility while retaining veto authority.4 Our contribution extends this framework specifically to the coupled domestic dining context, a domain notable for the simultaneous presence of high stakes (relational harmony) and low stakes (it's dinner) that creates uniquely distorted incentive gradients.

2.2 The Veto Literature

Chen & Watanabe (2018) documented veto behavior in 340 couples' restaurant selection processes, identifying a mean of 5.2 vetoes before resolution, with notable variance by relationship duration.5 Our replication across a substantially larger sample (n = 1,847) produced a higher mean (7.3), suggesting either methodological differences in veto counting protocols or a worsening of the condition over time. We favor the latter explanation.

3. Methods

3.1 The Veto Log Protocol

Couples were equipped with standardized Veto Log Cards during the study period. Upon initiation of any restaurant-selection conversation, the "offering" partner recorded each suggestion made; the "receiving" partner independently recorded their private preference at the moment of initial inquiry. Log cards were collected and compared at resolution.

A "veto" was defined as any non-acceptance response including: "I'm not really in the mood for that," "we just had that," silence followed by topic change, "whatever you want," and the sub-type we termed the Distant Hmm — a voiced nasal exhalation conveying neither acceptance nor explicit rejection, which nonetheless functions as a veto in practice.

3.2 The False Neutrality Index (FNI)

We developed the False Neutrality Index as a ratio measure of the divergence between stated and actual preference states at the moment of neutrality claim:

FNI = (Nstated × Pconcealed) / Trevelation
Where Nstated = stated neutrality score (0–1), Pconcealed = concealed preference specificity (0–1), Trevelation = time to revelation in minutes

A perfect FNI of 1.0 indicates complete stated neutrality combined with complete concealed specificity, resolved in exactly one minute. Higher values indicate more extreme false neutrality. The study mean was 0.71. The study maximum was 1.0, recorded six times. All six participants had wanted the Greek place on Maple.

4.2 The Veto Cascade

Veto analysis revealed a consistent pattern we termed the Cascade with Late Narrowing: early vetoes are categorical ("not really Italian tonight"), mid-phase vetoes become more specific ("that place has bad parking"), and late-phase vetoes become nearly arbitrary ("I feel like somewhere new"). This progression has a mean duration of 31.2 minutes and terminates either in exhaustion-based acceptance of the offering partner's suggestion or spontaneous self-revelation by the preferring partner.

4.3 The Preferred Restaurant Was Stable

Cross-referencing veto log cards, we found that in 91.4% of cases, the restaurant ultimately chosen was identical to the restaurant held privately at the moment of initial "I don't mind" declaration. The preference was not formed during the veto process. The preference was not the product of the discussion. The preference predated the discussion by a mean of 8.3 minutes — in many cases, by longer. Subject M-12 reported that his partner had been thinking about the Greek place "since lunch."

5. Game-Theoretic Analysis

5.1 Why False Neutrality Is the Dominant Strategy

Under current relational incentive structures, false neutrality dominates explicit preference revelation for the following reasons, each formally verified under standard Nash equilibrium analysis:

(a) Responsibility Offloading. Stating "I don't mind" transfers decision accountability to the offering partner. If the evening is poor, causal responsibility is attributed to the proposer, not the vetoer.

(b) Preference Optionality. False neutrality maintains the option to accept or reject any proposal without having committed to a preference that might be criticized.

(c) The Unselfishness Signal. "I don't mind" communicates relational flexibility and consideration, which has social value independent of its factual accuracy.

Payoff(False Neutrality) > Payoff(True Preference)
when: δ(blame avoidance) + σ(flexibility signal) > ε(efficiency gain) + τ(partner frustration cost)
This inequality holds for approximately 94% of observed pairs.

5.2 The Efficiency Cost

The 38.4-minute mean resolution time represents 38.4 minutes of foregone leisure, conversation, and dining that occurs while two people circle the same answer from opposite directions. Annualized across 3.2 restaurant decisions per week, the average couple spends approximately 213 hours per year — approximately 8.9 days — in veto cycles that could be resolved immediately by stating the preferred restaurant at T=0.

The 28.7% who "wanted to see if he would suggest it first" represent a secondary test function embedded within the veto process: preference concealment as a check on whether the partner possesses sufficient knowledge of one's tastes to produce the correct answer unsolicited. This is a valid relational test. It is also a test that, by design, produces 38.4 minutes of unnecessary inefficiency regardless of outcome.

7. The Resolution Moment

Despite the duration and apparent frustration of the veto cycle, resolution satisfaction was strikingly high: 91.3% of couples reported satisfaction with the eventual dining choice, and 87.4% reported enjoying the meal. Subject F-55 noted, of her 22nd recorded veto session: "It was a great evening. The lamb was incredible." The preferred restaurant had been the Greek place on Maple Street. It was very good. It always is.

This finding suggests that the veto cycle, while informationally inefficient, may serve latent relational functions — including the demonstration of sustained engagement, mutual accommodation signaling, and the ritualized approach to shared decisions that couples appear to value for its own sake.

8. Conclusions

"I don't mind" is not a preference state. It is a strategic utterance deployed in a game with established payoff structures, a predetermined terminal condition, and a preferred restaurant already selected. The restaurant existed at T=0. It will exist at T=38.4 minutes when it is finally named. In 91.4% of cases, it is the restaurant that was always going to be chosen.

The false neutrality cascade costs approximately 8.9 days per year per couple, produces no informational value for either party, and terminates at the same destination it would have reached immediately with honest preference disclosure. Nash equilibrium analysis confirms the strategy is rational given current incentive structures, which means the incentive structures are the problem, not the participants.

We close with the following recommendation, delivered without academic qualifications, footnotes, or caveats: Just say the restaurant.

It is the Greek place on Maple. It has always been the Greek place on Maple. The lamb is incredible.
