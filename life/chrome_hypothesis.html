Submitted: After One Very Productive Weekend in Scottsdale  ·  Peer-Reviewed: By Colleagues Who Kept Saying "Wait, Seriously?"  ·  Accepted: Unanimously, With Audible Laughter  ·  Published: In the Interest of Science and Also Older Men Everywhere

This paper presents the first comprehensive multi-disciplinary analysis of a phenomenon observed with remarkable consistency across North American parking lots, roadside diners, motorcycle rallies, and at least one Cracker Barrel in Tennessee: the acquisition of a custom, heavily chromed motorcycle by a male subject aged 55–75 demonstrably and repeatedly attracts the attention of female subjects significantly younger than the operator.

We examine this phenomenon through six independent disciplinary lenses: (1) Evolutionary Psychology, in which the motorcycle functions as an honest signal of resource acquisition capacity; (2) Materials Science, in which chrome's reflectivity coefficient functions as a proxy for wealth display that bypasses conscious evaluation; (3) Acoustic Engineering, in which the V-twin firing frequency at idle produces infrasonic resonance that activates the human threat-assessment-adjacent-to-excitement neural pathway; (4) Behavioral Economics, in which the custom motorcycle is a textbook Veblen good whose attraction value scales nonlinearly with price; (5) Neuroscience, in which the combination of chrome, sound, and the sight of a 67-year-old man clearly not worrying about what anyone thinks produces a dopaminergic response that the research team has elected to describe as "surprisingly robust"; and (6) Sociology, in which the motorcycle communicates an entire biographical narrative — freedom, means, experience, irreverence — in approximately 0.3 seconds, which is faster than any sentence a 67-year-old man could realistically open with.

Our central finding is this: it works. The data are unambiguous, the effect sizes are significant, the replication rate across sites is 94.7%, and the research team has spent considerable time arguing about whether publishing these findings is responsible. We have decided it is. The men were going to buy the motorcycles anyway.

§ 1 Introduction: The Phenomenon Stated

There exists, in the empirical record, a phenomenon so consistently observed, so frequently reported, and so thoroughly documented in gas station parking lots across the American Southwest that its continued absence from the peer-reviewed literature represents a significant gap — not in the data, which is extensive, but in the scientific community's willingness to take it seriously.

The phenomenon is this: a man, typically between the ages of 55 and 75, acquires a custom motorcycle — specifically one that has been chromed extensively, customized expensively, and equipped with an exhaust system whose primary engineering purpose appears to be acoustic rather than mechanical — parks said motorcycle in a public location, and is subsequently approached by, photographed by, or enters into extended conversation with women who are, by any standard actuarial measure, significantly younger than he is.

This paper does not ask whether this happens. It happens. The research team has documented it in 34 separate field sites across 11 states. It happens with a reliability that would make most pharmaceutical trials weep with envy. This paper asks why it happens — and finds, to the team's considerable amusement and eventual grudging respect, that the answer involves physics, evolution, neuroscience, economics, and a surprisingly sophisticated signaling system that the men deploying it generally understand only intuitively, if at all.

Subject M-67, our primary longitudinal case study, described his decision to purchase a $78,000 custom chromed touring motorcycle as follows: "I just always wanted one." Subject M-67 has since received fourteen unsolicited phone numbers, been photographed with thirty-one strangers, and been asked his name by a woman twenty-nine years his junior who then spelled her own name out slowly and unprompted. Subject M-67 continues to maintain that he bought the motorcycle because he "just always wanted one." The research team believes him. The research team also believes that "just wanting one" is evolution working exactly as designed.

1.1   Scope and Delimitations

This paper concerns itself specifically with custom motorcycles — those that have been modified, chromed, painted, and otherwise personalized at significant expense beyond the factory configuration. The distinction between a stock motorcycle and a custom chromed one is not merely aesthetic. It is, as Section 4 will demonstrate, economically and evolutionarily critical. A man on a $9,000 stock motorcycle is a man who owns a motorcycle. A man on a $79,000 custom chromed motorcycle is a man who has resources, taste, time, and enough self-confidence to spend eighty thousand dollars on something that will definitely scratch — and that combination, the data suggest, is doing considerable work.

We also note, for clarity, that this paper does not address why this strategy works with the specific frequency it does. We address that in Sections 2 through 8. This paper addresses only the fact that it works — which, upon reflection, is the part most likely to be useful to the target demographic and most likely to be cited in contexts the research team did not fully anticipate.

§ 2 Evolutionary Psychology: The Motorcycle as Honest Signal

Honest Signal Theory, first formalized by Zahavi (1975) in the context of the peacock's tail, holds that certain displays are evolutionarily stable precisely because they are costly — they require genuine resources to produce and maintain, and therefore cannot be faked by lower-quality individuals. The peacock cannot fake its tail. The male bowerbird cannot fake the elaborate structure it has spent weeks constructing. And a 67-year-old man cannot fake an $80,000 custom motorcycle sitting in the parking lot of a Cracker Barrel in Scottsdale.

The motorcycle, from an evolutionary psychology standpoint, is therefore not a vehicle. It is a peacock's tail with a 103-cubic-inch engine and approximately forty square feet of custom chrome work.

A custom chromed motorcycle, assessed by an evolutionarily-tuned female nervous system in approximately 300 milliseconds, communicates the following information simultaneously and without the operator having to say a single word:

2.2   Why Younger Men in Sports Cars Do Not Achieve Equivalent Results

A question the research team was repeatedly asked during peer review: "Why doesn't a 35-year-old in a Ferrari achieve the same effect?" This is an excellent question and the answer is instructive.

A 35-year-old in a Ferrari is, evolutionarily speaking, still a speculative investment. He might have resources long-term. The Ferrari might be financed. The resources might not be as deep as the car implies. The signal, while positive, is not yet fully verified by time.

A 67-year-old on an $80,000 custom motorcycle is a completed proof of concept. He accumulated the resources. He maintained them. He survived to spend them on something purely for pleasure. He is not performing success. He achieved it, had enough left over to chrome everything twice, and has the unhurried energy of a man who no longer needs to prove anything to anyone — which is, the neuroscience literature confirms (see Section 6), one of the most attractive qualities a human being can project.

§ 3 Materials Science: The Reflectivity Coefficient as Wealth Proxy

Dr. Whitmore's contribution to this research represents, in the view of the other authors, the most unexpectedly rigorous section of the paper. When first approached about the project, Dr. Whitmore — the world's foremost authority on industrial chromium application — reportedly replied that he "had better things to do." He subsequently read the preliminary data, requested field access, and spent eleven days in Scottsdale with a photometer. His findings follow.

3.1   Chrome as Sensory Stimulant

Chromium, when electroplated to a mirror finish and applied at the density typical of a custom motorcycle build (approximately 340–580 individual chrome components on a fully custom build), produces a surface reflectivity of 62–72% across the visible spectrum. This is not merely bright. This is, in direct sunlight, visually spectacular in a manner that bypasses learned aesthetic preferences and operates at the level of the primate visual system's fundamental response to moving light.

Achrome = Rλ × Csurface × Ωangular × Isolar
Chrome Attraction Index = Reflectivity × Surface Area × Viewing Angle Distribution × Solar Irradiance
At peak solar irradiance (Arizona, 2pm, June), a fully chromed custom motorcycle produces a total luminous intensity approaching that of a small theatrical spotlight — directed, as fate and physics would have it, outward in all directions simultaneously. The motorcycle is, in the precise sense of the word, radiant. This is not a metaphor. This is optics.

3.2   The Quantity Effect: Why More Chrome is More

A critical finding of Dr. Whitmore's analysis is that chrome coverage is not linear in its effect. Below approximately 40% custom chrome coverage, the motorcycle reads as "nicely maintained." Above 70% coverage, it begins to read as "someone made a significant decision here." Above 85% coverage — the full custom build — it reads as something that has no conventional descriptor in the English language but which the research team's female field observers consistently described, unprompted and independently, using the words: "Oh wow" and "whose is that."

"Whose is that," the research team notes, is among the most useful three-word questions an older gentleman's motorcycle can generate.

§ 4 Acoustic Engineering: The V-Twin as Neurological Event

Prof. Holloway joined this research with a specific hypothesis: that the sound profile of a large-displacement V-twin engine at idle — specifically the characteristic loping cadence produced by the 45-degree cylinder offset and the uneven firing interval — produces acoustic frequencies that interact with the human auditory-emotional processing system in ways that are both measurable and, for the research team's purposes, highly relevant.

Prof. Holloway was correct. The research team wishes it had been less correct, because the findings required three rounds of peer review before the reviewers accepted that the numbers were real.

4.1   The V-Twin Firing Signature

A 103-cubic-inch V-twin engine with aftermarket exhaust, at 900-1100 RPM idle, produces a sound pressure level of 78-94 dB at 10 meters, with a fundamental frequency of approximately 47-58 Hz and significant harmonic content in the 100-200 Hz range. This frequency range is not arbitrary. It overlaps substantially with:

Table 2 — V-Twin Acoustic Profile and Neurological Coincidences That Are Not Coincidences
Frequency RangeV-Twin OutputKnown Human Neurological Response
40–60 Hz (Fundamental) Primary idle thump. The "potato-potato" cadence. Felt in the chest at close range. Activates the same brainstem alerting pathway triggered by large animals, distant thunder, and other stimuli the primate threat-assessment system classifies as "significant and worth paying attention to." Not fear. Attention. Alert presence. The brain says: something is here.
80–120 Hz (2nd Harmonic) The "chest resonance" range. Physically perceptible at 5 meters. Inexplicably satisfying to most humans regardless of motorcycle interest. Overlaps with the fundamental frequency range of the adult male speaking voice at its most resonant. The motorcycle is, acoustically, doing something the male voice does when it is being compelling. This is either a remarkable coincidence or evolution having a moment.

The motorcycle is literally emitting confidence frequencies.

Note: Prof. Holloway wishes to emphasize that this analysis applies specifically to large-displacement V-twin engines with open or lightly baffled exhaust systems. A 250cc inline-4 at idle produces primarily annoyance. The acoustic profile does not scale down. This is non-negotiable from an engineering standpoint.

4.2   The Throttle as Language

A finding that emerged unexpectedly from the acoustic analysis and that Prof. Holloway initially refused to include in the paper on the grounds that it "sounds ridiculous" before the data compelled its inclusion: the brief application of throttle — the characteristic blip produced when an operator revs the engine without moving — functions, in the acoustic analysis, as a communicative act.

A single throttle blip from a large-displacement V-twin produces a rapid increase and decrease in sound energy across the full frequency spectrum described above, lasting approximately 0.8 seconds. In the acoustic data, this event is indistinguishable in its attentional-capture function from a human voice saying a short, declarative sentence in a confident register.

The motorcycle, in other words, can speak. It says something that has no direct translation but that the research team's field observers consistently paraphrased as: "I'm here, and I'm not in a hurry." This sentence, delivered at 94 dB from a custom-chromed touring motorcycle, has a measurably different effect than the same sentence delivered verbally by a 67-year-old man in a parking lot. The research team acknowledges this is not flattering to the verbal approach but insists the data are the data.

§ 5 Behavioral Economics: The Veblen Motorcycle Effect

Prof. Abara-Whitfield arrived at this research from a different angle than his colleagues. As the author of Veblen Was Right About Everything: A Reluctant Concession (2019), he was already familiar with the literature on conspicuous consumption — the phenomenon, first described by Thorstein Veblen in 1899, whereby goods derive part of their value from their visible expensiveness. What he had not previously considered was the application of Veblen good theory to mate-attraction outcomes. He has now considered it extensively, and his conclusions are presented below.

5.1   Why the Price Matters More Than the Machine

Veblen goods are distinguished from normal goods by a counterintuitive demand curve: as price increases, demand increases. A $500 watch tells time. A $50,000 watch also tells time. The $50,000 watch communicates something the $500 watch cannot. The motorcycle market follows exactly this pattern — but with an additional variable that Veblen himself did not have the pleasure of analyzing: the age of the operator.

5.2   The Irrationality Premium: Why Custom Beats Stock Every Time

The single most economically significant finding in Prof. Abara-Whitfield's analysis is what he terms the Irrationality Premium: the additional attraction value generated not merely by the expense of a custom motorcycle, but by the visible fact that the expense was unnecessary.

A stock motorcycle is a rational purchase. It performs the transportation function efficiently at reasonable cost. A custom motorcycle — with hand-stitched leather, eighteen coats of custom paint over a base layer of real metal flake, chrome-plated components that were already perfectly functional in steel, and an exhaust system that costs $4,000 and makes the machine sound like the voice of God — is an irrational purchase. It is more expensive. It is less practical. It is magnificent.

And magnificence, the behavioral economics literature confirms, is itself a signal. The man who spent $78,000 making a functional machine beautiful is communicating that he has resources sufficient to deploy toward beauty. That he values beauty. That he has the security to spend money on something purely because he wanted to. This profile — resources, aesthetics, confidence, security — scores extremely high on every mate-quality assessment instrument the research team could locate.

** In an unexpected finding, Prof. Abara-Whitfield documented what he has termed the "Neighbor Effect": female subjects who had not approached the motorcycle operator directly were observed making positive comments about the motorcycle to companions, using phrasing that in every case reflected on the owner rather than the machine. 6.2   The "Not Trying" Variable

The most counterintuitive finding in Dr. Cruz-Okonkwo's neuroimaging data is what she terms the Settled Confidence Effect: the neural response in female subjects is significantly stronger when the male operator is observed not paying attention to whether it is working.

An operator who is sitting on or near the motorcycle and scanning the environment for reactions produces a measurably weaker nucleus accumbens response in observers than an operator who is examining the machine himself, talking to a companion, or — peak performance, documented 34 times — drinking a coffee and looking at literally nothing in particular.

The man who bought an $80,000 motorcycle for himself, is having a fine time regardless of what anyone thinks, and does not need the validation — produces, neurologically, a stronger attraction response than the man who bought the same motorcycle hoping it would work and is clearly checking whether it's working. The irony is exquisite. The motorcycle works best when the owner has forgotten to wonder if it works.

§ 7 The Age-Differential as Feature, Not Bug

The most common objection raised during peer review — raised, notably, by the youngest reviewer on the panel — was a version of the following: "But isn't the age differential a liability? Shouldn't younger women prefer younger men?" This question, while reasonable in isolation, reflects an incomplete understanding of both the evolutionary literature and the specific signal being analyzed.

The answer is: for some purposes, yes. For the specific mate-quality signals communicated by a custom chromed motorcycle, operated with unhurried confidence by a man who has visibly had a full and successful life and still gets up in the morning and does interesting things — no. The age is not a liability. The age is the evidence.

7.1   The Proof-of-Concept Problem

A 35-year-old man presents a hypothesis: "I will be successful, stable, interesting, and worth knowing." The hypothesis is unverified. It may be true. It may not. The listener must evaluate future potential from present evidence, which is speculative.

A 67-year-old man on a custom motorcycle presents a completed proof: "I was successful enough to accumulate this. I am stable enough that I still feel like doing things. I am interesting enough that I customized something to my own taste rather than buying whatever was available. I am worth knowing because I have, by the actuarial evidence in front of you, been worth knowing for approximately five decades." This is not a hypothesis. This is a longitudinal study with a 67-year sample.

§ 8 Field Case Studies

Subject M-67, age 67, acquired a custom-built touring motorcycle with full chrome package, custom paint (57 hours of labor, 22 coats), aftermarket exhaust producing 91 dB at idle, and an estimated total investment of $82,400. He describes the purchase as motivated entirely by having "always wanted one."

Over 18 months of longitudinal observation, Subject M-67 was approached by 312 individuals in parking lots, gas stations, diners, and at two motorcycle rallies. Of these, 247 (79.2%) were female. Of the female approaches, 71% were by women more than 15 years younger than the subject. Fourteen approached women provided phone numbers unprompted. Subject M-67 used three of them. He describes himself as "not really looking for anything" — a statement that, per Section 6.2, Dr. Cruz-Okonkwo has confirmed is itself doing enormous amounts of work.

Subject M-67 also received, during the study period, one noise complaint from a neighbor, one concerned call from his cardiologist about "the kind of vibration exposure that engine produces," and one voicemail from his adult son asking "Dad are you okay you bought an eighty thousand dollar motorcycle." Subject M-67 is fine. Subject M-67 has never been better. Subject M-67 is, by every available measure, thriving.

Researchers documented approach rates for both subjects simultaneously.

Subject M-71: 23 approaches over two days, 78% female, average age differential of 33 years, four conversations exceeding 30 minutes, two dinner invitations from separate parties. Subject M-71's son: 4 approaches over two days, 50% female, average age differential of 2 years, zero dinner invitations, increasing existential discomfort.

On the second evening, the son was observed examining his father's motorcycle for approximately 12 minutes. He asked where it was built. He looked at his own motorcycle. He looked at his father's again. He said, to no one in particular, "I think I understand now." He has since inquired about custom build shops in three states. His father gave him two recommendations and the phone number of a good chrome plater. The research team considers this outcome a successful intergenerational transfer of knowledge.

Subject M-58, age 58, provides the research team's most instructive data regarding the context-specificity of the Chrome Hypothesis. Upon delivery of his new custom motorcycle — full chrome, custom paint, $71,000 total investment — his wife of 28 years viewed it in the driveway and said, quote: "You look ridiculous. It's a mid-life crisis on wheels. The neighbors are going to talk." This was logged as a strongly negative domestic response, classified as Context A.

The following afternoon, Subject M-58 stopped for fuel at a gas station on Route 17. During the 4 minutes and 12 seconds he was stationary at the pump, he was approached by three women ranging in age from 27 to 34, all of whom complimented the motorcycle, asked questions about it, and in one case asked if he would mind if she took a photo with it. Subject M-58 did not mind. This was logged as a strongly positive public response, classified as Context B.

Subject M-58 returned home and reported both events to his wife. His wife said "Of course they talked to you, you have a very expensive thing." Subject M-58 noted this appeared to concede a fundamental point. His wife did not acknowledge this. She has since been observed, on two occasions, referring to the motorcycle as "our motorcycle" to friends. The research team classifies this as a data point too complex to fully analyze within the scope of this paper and has noted it for a future study titled "The Domestic Reclassification of the Mid-Life Crisis: A Longitudinal Analysis."

‡‡ A finding that appeared consistently across all three case studies and twelve additional subjects: the custom chrome motorcycle generates positive attention from women who do not identify as motorcycle enthusiasts. When field researchers asked approaching women whether they were interested in motorcycles generally, 67% said no, or "not really," or "I've never thought about it." They were not interested in motorcycles. They were interested in this motorcycle. The distinction matters. This is not a hobby interest. This is a status and signal response operating independently of any motorcycle-specific knowledge or preference. The motorcycle does not require the observer to know anything about motorcycles. It communicates in a language older than any motorcycle.

§ 9 The Helmet Problem: A Confounding Variable

No paper that deals honestly with the Chrome Hypothesis can avoid addressing what the research team has, from the earliest stages of this project, referred to as The Helmet Problem.

The Helmet Problem is this: a significant portion of the signal analyzed in Sections 2 through 7 — specifically the evolutionary psychology components relating to confident male bearing, the age-related proof-of-concept signals, and the settled-confidence neurological effects — is produced by the operator. And the operator is, while in motion, wearing a helmet. Which covers his face. Which removes approximately 40% of the biological signaling apparatus from view.

This creates a situation that the research team has struggled to describe in language appropriate for a peer-reviewed journal. The motorcycle arrives — brilliant, chrome, thunderous, magnificent — and sitting atop it is a shape in a helmet who could be, for all the observer can currently determine, literally anyone.

FIELD SAFETY NOTICE: The research team strongly advises that motorcycle operators observe all applicable helmet laws and safety regulations. The Helmet Problem is a research methodological inconvenience. It is not a reason to ride without a helmet. The research team notes that a deceased subject cannot enjoy the results of the Chrome Hypothesis. Wear the helmet. Remove it upon arrival. This is the sequence. This is the protocol. The arrival matters more than the approach.

9.1   The Reveal Sequence

The field data reveals a finding that Prof. Holloway initially described as "absurdly cinematic but empirically solid": the act of removing the helmet after parking — specifically the sequence of silencing the engine, dismounting, and removing the helmet — functions as a distinct and measurable attentional event in the parking lot environment.

Female subjects within visual range of the parking event showed a documented secondary attentional spike at helmet removal — a second orienting response following the initial motorcycle arrival response documented in Section 6. The arrival of the chrome motorcycle generates attention. The reveal of the operator generates assessment. These are two distinct neural events, and the gap between them — typically 30 to 90 seconds — functions, in the research team's framing, as a very effective piece of theater.

The research team documented 23 instances in which female subjects who had been casually observing the parked motorcycle visibly refocused attention at the moment of helmet removal. In 19 of those 23 instances, the refocused attention was followed, within 4 minutes, by an approach or proximal positioning.

In Subject M-67's case study data, his average helmet-removal-to-first-approach time across all documented events was 3 minutes and 14 seconds. This is faster than most people's coffee order. The research team notes that coffee was, on multiple occasions, the instrument of the approach — specifically the question "Is there a good place to get coffee around here?" which the research team has elected to classify as a conversation-opening technique that requires absolutely no motorcycle knowledge and approximately eight words.

§ 10 Confounding Variables, Limitations, and Things We Cannot Explain But Documented Anyway

10.1   The Leather Jacket Invariance Theorem

Early in the research design, the team hypothesized that a leather jacket — the cultural garment most strongly associated with motorcycle operation — would function as an additional positive moderating variable. This hypothesis was tested across 156 subjects.

The leather jacket adds 0.3 standard deviations to approach rate, which is statistically significant but practically modest. More interestingly, the absence of a leather jacket in favor of a button-down shirt, a polo, or — in one documented case — a very nice linen jacket, produced approach rates indistinguishable from the leather jacket condition and in some field sites marginally higher.

The research team interprets this as follows: the leather jacket communicates "motorcycle person." The polo shirt or linen jacket on a man arriving on a custom chromed motorcycle communicates "person who happens to ride motorcycles among other things he does," which is a subtly but measurably different — and measurably more interesting — biographical implication. The leather jacket says biker. The linen jacket says the man contains multitudes.

10.2   The Destination Matters Less Than the Research Team Expected

The research team initially predicted that parking location would significantly moderate approach rates — that a motorcycle rally would produce higher rates than a Cracker Barrel, for instance. The prediction was wrong. The Chrome Hypothesis holds across environments with remarkable consistency. Diner, gas station, motorcycle rally, upscale restaurant parking lot, hardware store, one documented instance at a library — the approach rate variance by location was less than 8%.

The chrome, it appears, does not require a supporting context. It is its own context. It arrives and creates the conditions for interaction wherever it stops, because the signals it carries are not venue-specific. They are human-specific. They work in the parking lot of a Cracker Barrel for the same reason they work at Sturgis: because the observer's nervous system does not know or care what building is in the background.

10.3   One Thing We Cannot Explain

In 7 of 34 field sites, researchers documented what they have termed the Spontaneous Endorsement Cascade: a phenomenon in which a positive interaction between a female subject and a motorcycle operator, observed by other female subjects, produced additional approaches at a rate 2.3x higher than the baseline approach rate.

This is social proof, which is known and understood. What is less understood is that the cascade initiated even when the observing subjects could not have heard the initial conversation — they observed only the physical proximity and body language of the initial interaction. The motorcycle was apparently functioning as a focal point around which positive social dynamics organized themselves spontaneously, drawing additional participants through a mechanism that the research team's sociologist, Dr. Abara-Whitfield, described as "the chrome version of a campfire." People come toward warmth. In this case the warmth is reflective and has a V-twin.

§ 11 Conclusions

We set out to determine whether a 67-year-old man buying a custom chromed motorcycle increases his appeal to younger women. We found that it does, reliably, across six independent disciplinary frameworks, 34 field sites, 847 documented parking events, and one memorable afternoon in Laughlin, Nevada involving a stoplight and a woman who rolled her window down.

The Chrome Hypothesis holds. The effect is real. The mechanism is evolutionary, neurological, acoustic, economic, and optical simultaneously — which makes it, the research team submits, one of the most over-determined results in the history of behavioral science. Nature, economics, physics, and neuroscience all agree. The chrome motorcycle works. They agree because they are describing the same underlying truth from different angles: the man who has lived fully and spent some of what he has on something beautiful and loud and unnecessary because he wanted to is doing something that the human nervous system, across all its evolutionary layers, recognizes as worth paying attention to.

We close with a note for the men in our study, specifically Subject M-67, who will read this paper and continue insisting he bought the motorcycle because he "just always wanted one."

Sir, you are correct. That is exactly why you bought it. And the fact that it works for the reasons documented in this paper does not diminish the fact that you wanted it, got it, and are enjoying it thoroughly. The best mate-attraction strategies are always the ones the operator is not consciously executing. You wanted the motorcycle. The motorcycle wanted to work. Evolution made sure those two desires were compatible. You just showed up and let the chrome do what chrome does.

The research team considers this a beautiful outcome. We hope the extended chrome package is everything you wanted.
